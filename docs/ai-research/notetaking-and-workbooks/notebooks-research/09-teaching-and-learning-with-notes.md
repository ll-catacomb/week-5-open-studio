# Teaching and learning with notes

**Central question:** What are students learning when they make a notebook, and what additional work lets those notes become an argument?

The answer changes with the object, institution, and outcome. A notebook can be a demonstration of competent work, a reference for future use, a device for rehearsing a lesson, or raw material for writing. These purposes should not be collapsed into a single measure of “effective notes.” My argument is that teaching note taking means teaching a sequence of operations and its intended destination: capture, correction, arrangement, retrieval, comparison, and composition. Choosing paper or a keyboard is one decision within that sequence.

The cases below do not establish a continuous ancestry from Enlightenment manuscripts to Cornell pages to laptops. They make different arrangements of intellectual labor visible. Historical artifacts show what somebody produced; instructional manuals prescribe what somebody should do; experiments estimate effects under specified conditions. None is interchangeable with the others. A beautiful surviving page does not reveal its maker's examination score, while an immediate quiz cannot establish the quality of a future research essay.

## 1. Scottish school notebooks: making a usable object

Eddy's 2018 study interprets Scottish school notebooks as manuscript textbooks constructed through paper preparation, writing, and drawing, also displaying instruction to parents. Jemima Arrow's *Maps* (1815), **National Library of Scotland MS 14100**, supplies a concrete example: **figure 10, p. 305, fols. 34–35** shows a map coordinated with a facing letter-keyed legend. I inspected this reproduced spread, not the original volume. It cannot establish Arrow's review habits or later essay production. [Eddy, full paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8D13355B698E5C6F8EE5B025CBDA9249/S0021937117002398a.pdf/nature_of_notebooks_how_enlightenment_schoolchildren_transformed_the_tabula_rasa.pdf).

For a classroom investigation, I would ask students to distinguish the labor of representing relationships from the labor of remembering names. Could a diagram preserve a structure that prose would obscure? Could a legend make the same drawing usable by another reader? Could a finished notebook be assessed for navigability as well as correctness? These are questions prompted by the object, not claims about Arrow's intentions. We should also ask whose materials, time, instruction, and access made such work possible. Archival survival should not be mistaken for a representative sample of all learners.

## 2. Lecture notes: a student's record can become another person's source

Glasgow's Adam Smith collections make the transmission chain unusually explicit. The university identifies **MS Gen. 109** as a **1766 fair copy made by a clerk** from a student's notes; it places the underlying jurisprudence lectures in 1762–63 or early 1763–64. Cannan published the manuscript in 1896. A separate collection guide lists **MS Gen. 94**, with lectures dated December 24, 1762–April 12, 1763, and **MS Gen. 95**, notes of rhetoric lectures from 1762–63. These are distinct witnesses, not three interchangeable titles for one notebook. [Glasgow exhibition record](https://www.gla.ac.uk/explore/adamsmith300/smithandglasgowtoday/); [collection guide, PDF p. 6](https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/teach/enlightenment.pdf).

This case changes the meaning of “student writing.” A lecture record can outlive the assessment for which it might have been made and become evidence for a later scholar. Its authority requires questions about mediation: the lecturer spoke; a listener selected or recorded; another hand produced a fair copy; an editor presented a publication. The lecturer receives the famous name, while the student and copying labor may remain less visible.

The dated fair copy also blocks an easy error: the date of a surviving object need not be the date of the speech it represents. Comparing versions might expose differences in arrangement, omissions, or explanation, but I have not performed that comparison. Nor does overlap between Smith's lectures and his later published work prove that he composed that work *from these students' notebooks*. Student notes can be witnesses to an author's developing ideas without being that author's drafting materials. This is an especially useful distinction when an AI transcript or collaborative class document is later treated as the instructor's definitive statement.

## 3. Cornell notes: an instructional system organized across time

Cornell's institutional history dates Walter Pauk's system to the **1950s** and its promotion in *How to Study in College* to **1962**. That is firmer than repeating an unsupported exact invention year or assigning it to the 1940s. The institution's illustration uses a wide recording column, a narrower cue/question column, and a summary area. [Cornell's historical account](https://alumni.cornell.edu/cornellians/cornell-notes/).

The operative instruction is temporal. Cornell's handout, adapted from Pauk's 2001 seventh edition, asks students to record briefly during a lecture; formulate questions afterward; cover the recording column and answer from cues; reflect on significance and application; and review previous notes weekly. It is an instructional artifact, not an experiment establishing that this exact page layout surpasses all alternatives. [Cornell handout](https://lsc.cornell.edu/notes.html).

I would teach the sequence before evaluating the page's appearance. A learner can draw impeccable columns and never use the questions. Another can implement question generation and self-testing in a plain text document. Equivalence between these implementations would itself require testing, but the distinction between a layout and its enacted use is already useful for designing instruction.

The cue column also raises a question about authorship. Does the student merely invent questions that reproduce the instructor's expected examination, or ask questions that expose uncertainty and suggest further research? Both may be worthwhile; they lead toward different outputs. A summary of one lecture is not automatically a thesis for an essay. An essay needs a contestable claim, evidence that bears on it, and an account of alternatives. A Cornell page can supply ingredients, but teaching the conversion still requires an additional task.

## 4. Experimental research: specify the operation and the outcome

The research literature distinguishes **encoding**—what the act of taking notes contributes during learning—from **external storage**, the value of having a record available for subsequent use. Kobayashi's 2005 meta-analysis examined 57 studies comparing note taking with no note taking **without subsequent note review**. It found a positive but modest overall effect, with outcomes differing by test type; recall measures detected larger benefits than recognition or higher-order measures. Its inclusion criteria concerned paper-and-pen notes, so it is not a laptop comparison. [Kobayashi 2005, full text posted by the author](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223352417_What_limits_the_encoding_effect_of_note-taking_A_meta-analytic_examination).

Kobayashi's 2006 synthesis addressed **taking plus reviewing**. Across 33 studies, the relevant comparisons favored this combination; the mean weighted effect against listening/reading only was .75. Frameworks or supplied instructor notes were more effective interventions than verbal instruction or pretraining alone. Crucially, the review excluded writing outcomes. It cannot directly establish better essays, and subtracting its effect from a different meta-analysis would not isolate a causal “review bonus.” [Kobayashi 2006, full paper](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Keiichi-Kobayashi/publication/247513702_Combined_Effects_of_Note-Taking-Reviewing_on_Learning_and_the_Enhancement_through_Interventions_A_meta-analytic_review/links/55e93cd308ae21d099c2e4e3/Combined-Effects-of-Note-Taking-Reviewing-on-Learning-and-the-Enhancement-through-Interventions-A-meta-analytic-review.pdf?origin=publication_detail).

The distinction suggests a practical design question: are students expected to remember a lesson unaided, to consult their notes accurately, or to create something with them? Those are different assessments. A record that is poor for unaided recall might still be valuable for checking a precise quotation. Conversely, a compact mnemonic might help a test while omitting provenance essential to responsible research writing. “More notes” and “better notes” therefore require an explicit destination.

### The handwriting/laptop dispute, including replication

Mueller and Oppenheimer's 2014 experiments used video lectures with laptop or longhand notes. Their first study reported a longhand advantage on conceptual questions, not factual recall; laptop notes were longer and more verbatim. A third study tested after one week with review manipulated and reported advantages for the longhand/study condition. These were assessments of lecture learning, not evaluations of independently researched essays. Their proposed explanation involving generative processing should be distinguished from the observed medium comparisons and correlations in note content. [Original paper](https://www.hendrix.edu/uploadedFiles/Academics/Faculty_Resources/Faculty_Development_Newsletters/Mueller%20and%20Oppenheimer%20%282014%29.pdf).

Morehead, Dunlosky, and Rawson's 2019 replication and extension added an electronic handwriting device and, in Experiment 2, a no-notes comparison. The designs included immediate and two-day tests; Experiment 2 added seven minutes of review before the delayed test. Its no-notes group completed only the immediate test. The experiments did not produce a consistent medium advantage. This complicates the original generalization while leaving room for effects under different instructional conditions. [Author-posted full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330856100_How_Much_Mightier_Is_the_Pen_than_the_Keyboard_for_Note-Taking_A_Replication_and_Extension_of_Mueller_and_Oppenheimer_2014).

Urry and colleagues' preregistered 2021 direct replication of Study 1 included **142 participants**. Laptop notes again contained more words and more verbatim overlap, but the study did not reproduce a longhand quiz advantage after a short distraction **without review**. Exploratory meta-analyses of eight similar studies also failed to support immediate longhand superiority. The authors acknowledge variable data-collection settings and a restricted, preliminary synthesis; these findings neither establish universal equivalence nor settle delayed study benefits. [Urry et al., full paper, especially pp. 11–13](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/587ea259197aea1c1d66ec5b/t/601f2ac8268c6a7b9735c877/1612655305004/urryetal_psci_2021.pdf).

A broader 2024 meta-analysis by Flanigan and colleagues included **24 studies** and found a small average handwriting advantage for achievement (**Hedges' g = .248**) and a larger typing advantage for note quantity (**g = .919**). The handwriting advantage was larger in the formal-review subgroup. Its discussion states that the included studies concerned a single lecture; its illustration of semester grades is an extrapolation, not an observed semester experiment. The authors also report that the included studies did not account for disabled students' needs. [Full paper, pp. 10–19](https://utstat.toronto.edu/reid/sta2212s/lecturenotesmeta.pdf).

Taken together, these results warrant a narrower conclusion than either “handwriting always wins” or “the original study was disproved, so medium never matters.” Different samples, review opportunities, source presentations, and outcome definitions can produce different estimates. The 2024 average is evidence worth considering; it does not establish each student's optimum or the mechanism producing the effect. Correlations between paraphrasing and performance cannot alone show that forcing every student to paraphrase causes improvement. A classroom should investigate how a learner actually uses a record, including relevant access requirements, rather than make an aesthetic preference stand in for an evidence-based teaching decision.

### What about notes becoming essays?

Benton and colleagues' 1993 experiments provide evidence closer to this report's destination. Undergraduates watched a 19-minute lecture on creativity and wrote a 25-minute compare-and-contrast essay. In both immediate and delayed conditions, students writing from their own notes produced longer, more organized essays than students writing without their notes. This supports an external-reference function for a bounded writing task; length and organization do not by themselves demonstrate originality, source criticism, or argumentative validity. I accessed the abstract and publisher preview, not the complete article. [Benton et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232537263_Encoding_and_External-Storage_Effects_on_Writing_Processes).

Slotte and Lonka's 1999 study involved **226 high-school graduates** reading a philosophical text. Participants chose their own note strategies; approximately half could consult notes during tasks requiring definition, comparison, and evaluation. The abstract reports that access helped detailed learning more than independent inference, while summary notes were associated with stronger performance. Because note strategy was spontaneous, the summary association is not a randomized test of a universal summarizing rule. This is **abstract-only evidence** here, retrieved through PubMed's XML service after the publisher page could not be opened. [Indexed abstract, PMID 9878205](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9878205/).

For teaching, I infer that a notes-to-essay assignment should assess the transformation itself. Can the writer state what each source actually supports? Can they group evidence across sources rather than reproduce reading order? Can they distinguish an observation, someone else's interpretation, and their own claim? These questions go beyond the quiz outcomes measured in much note-taking research. Keeping those outcomes separate makes it possible to test a useful study method without overselling it as a complete method of scholarly writing.

## A source → note → review → arrangement → argument walkthrough

**Modern reconstruction for week 5:** this is a proposed exercise using the sources above, not a documented workflow of Arrow, Smith's students, or Pauk.

1. **Source:** give learners a short extract from the 2014 experiment and the 2021 replication, with titles, dates, and methods attached. Keep the source pack available after class.
2. **Capture:** each learner records one result, its tested outcome, its review condition, and one limitation. A copied phrase must be visibly marked as copied. Add a source locator beside every substantive entry.
3. **Review:** cover the entries and explain the difference between factual recall and conceptual application; then reopen them to correct errors. Record corrections separately, so the initial misunderstanding remains visible.
4. **Arrange:** construct a two-source matrix: sample, activity, assessment delay, review opportunity, result, and scope. The arrangement exposes whether apparently competing statements answer the same question.
5. **Draft:** write a 250-word argument about what a teacher can responsibly conclude. Require a claim, evidence from both sources, and one unresolved question. A possible claim is that any recommendation must state its learning conditions and outcome.
6. **Audit:** a partner follows each cited claim back to the pack. Mark places where a memory result has silently become a claim about essays. Revise the paragraph rather than merely adding another citation.

The output is deliberately modest. The student has transformed records into a comparison and then a qualified argument. The retained notes and corrections let a teacher see where selection, interpretation, and revision occurred. They also show what has been discarded from the final prose without treating that discarded material as wasted work.

## Comparison of the four cases

| Case | Material and social organization | Intended or measured destination | What remains unproved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scottish school manuscripts | Student-produced text and visual reference; instruction made visible | Usable instructional object | Individual review habits and later essay production |
| Smith lecture witnesses | Lecturer → student record → clerk's fair copy → edition | Transmission and subsequent scholarly evidence | Complete fidelity or Smith's composition from these objects |
| Cornell system | Capture followed by student questions, recitation, reflection, review | Prescribed study routine | Universal superiority of the exact template |
| Learning experiments | Assigned conditions and scored tasks; some later note access | Recall, application, or bounded writing performance | General advantage for every learner and research genre |

## Two feasible week 5 classroom experiments

### Experiment A: separate making notes from reviewing them

Use two comparable six-minute mini-lessons. Let students use their usual accessible medium. After lesson one, randomly assign five minutes of note review or a neutral task; give a short closed-note quiz with separately scored recall and application items. For lesson two, reverse the assignment and counterbalance lesson order across the room. Afterward, provide everyone the materials and review time.

Retain note coverage, quiz scores, prior familiarity, and students' accounts of what they did during review. This is a local demonstration of study conditions, not a publishable causal estimate from one small class. The crossover reduces some individual differences, but lesson difficulty and carryover remain. It also keeps the question focused: what did the review opportunity add under this presentation, rather than which device is morally preferable?

### Experiment B: test the conversion, not the notebook's appearance

Provide two short sources that disagree or differ in scope. Students first make notes freely. Randomly assign a second stage of either rereading those notes or rearranging them into an evidence-and-limitations matrix, with equal time. Everyone writes a 250-word argument with sources and notes available. Assess source accuracy, warranted comparison, counterevidence, and claim qualification separately from word count and fluency.

Swap the arrangements using a new matched source pair in a later session. Collect the notes, arrangement, and draft as a visible chain. Ask students where each main claim first appeared. A matrix that improves one task may impose unhelpful structure on another; accept disagreement and document it. This experiment directly examines a teaching proposal advanced here, rather than pretending that lecture-memory findings have already validated it.

## Open questions and further research

- A genetic history needs an actual student's notes, revisions, assignment instructions, and finished essay. Neither an attractive notebook nor a famous lecturer's later book establishes that full chain.
- How do lecture pace, visual material, language proficiency, fatigue, accessibility, and prior knowledge affect the costs of capture? The reviewed medium findings do not resolve these interactions for a particular classroom.
- What happens when review includes correcting and combining notes, rather than simply studying the original record? Research should specify revision as an operation.
- Does a comparison matrix improve source-based argument quality after several weeks, and can students choose when it is useful? The proposed classroom exercise is an untested local reconstruction.
- Historical investigation should extend beyond these Scottish and American institutional settings and ask whose student labor is named, whose notes survive, and whose educational experience the archive excludes.
- AI-generated lecture notes need a separate study design: transcription accuracy, student verification, review behavior, and independent argument quality must all be measured. The laptop literature does not answer those questions by substitution.
