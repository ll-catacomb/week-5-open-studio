# Nothing Read Without Excerpting

### Ancient notes, extracts, and the turning of reading into writing and living

*TDM 155AI · Week 5 research for the studio walls · Draft for iteration*

---

## The essay

In winter, on the road, Pliny the Elder did not travel alone. Beside him in the carriage sat a *notarius*, a shorthand writer, "with a book and tablets" (*cum libro et pugillaribus*), his hands protected by long sleeves or gloves (*manicis*) "so that not even the harshness of the weather could snatch away any time for study" (Pliny, *Ep.* 3.5.15). The scene comes from his nephew, Pliny the Younger, writing in the early 100s AD to Baebius Macer, who had asked for a list of the uncle's books. The nephew sends the list, then something stranger: a portrait of a method. The uncle read, or was read to, at meals, lying in the sun, and in the bath while being scraped and towelled (3.5.10–14). "He read nothing without excerpting it," and he used to say that no book was so bad that some part of it was not useful (3.5.10). When a dinner guest made the reader go back over a mispronounced passage, the uncle asked, "You understood it, didn't you? Then why call him back? We have lost more than ten lines through your interruption" (3.5.12). He left his nephew 160 notebooks of selections (*electorum commentarios*), written on both sides of the page "in the tiniest hand." Once, when there were fewer of them, he said he could have sold them to Larcius Licinus for 400,000 sesterces (3.5.17), a sum on the scale of the property qualification for an equestrian.

None of those 160 notebooks survives. That absence is the first thing to put on the wall. What we have is a letter describing a practice, a body of finished work that the practice fed (the *Natural History*, with its preface claiming some 20,000 facts drawn from about 2,000 volumes, *NH* praef. 17), and a scatter of material objects from other people, places, and purposes. This essay keeps those layers distinct.

### A word with many uses

It is tempting to say that the Greeks and Romans kept *hypomnēmata* and to imagine a single genre: the philosopher's commonplace book. The ancient word does not cooperate. A *hypomnēma* is, literally, a "reminder," something that puts you in mind. In Plato's *Phaedrus*, Socrates' Egyptian myth says writing is a drug not for memory but for *reminding* (275a), and later imagines a writer laying up reminders "against forgetful old age" (276d). In the Hellenistic and Roman administrative world of Egypt the word could name an official memorandum or a petition. In scholarship it named a commentary on a text. For historians it named the rough, unpolished draft: Lucian advises a historian to make a *hypomnēma* first, "a body as yet without beauty or articulation," and only then to give it order and style (*How to Write History* 48). Plutarch tells a friend that he has gathered material on tranquillity of mind "from the *hypomnēmata* I had made for myself" (*On Tranquillity of Mind* 464F). Latin had its own vocabulary, which only partly overlaps: *commentarii* (Pliny's word for his uncle's notebooks, and Gellius's for his own), *excerpta*, *adversaria*, *pugillares*, *codicilli*. Matthew Larsen has shown how wide this field of "unfinished" writing was, and how often ancient readers treated such texts as open to further use rather than as fixed works.

So the question is less "what was a *hypomnēma*?" than "what was this particular piece of writing *for*?"

### Four different acts

Four activities, often run together, deserve to be pulled apart.

**Recording an event** fixes something that happened: a debt, a delivery, a sale, a greeting. Most surviving Roman writing tablets do this. Their purpose is evidentiary; one returns to them to prove or to remember, not to be changed.

**Saving an extract** removes words from their book and keeps them for later. This is what Pliny's uncle did all day. The extract is portable and recombinable; its value lies in a future use that may not yet be known. His motto, that every book holds something useful, is a theory of reading as harvest.

**Rehearsing a maxim** is different again. Here the point is not storage but repetition until the words become disposition. Epictetus tells his students that these are the things philosophers should practise, "write daily," and exercise themselves in (*Discourses* 1.1.25). Seneca ends his early letters to Lucilius with a small gift, a single sentence, often from Epicurus, and says, "I do this myself: from the many things I have read I seize on one" (*Ep.* 2.5). Schoolchildren copied one-line maxims over and over; a second-century wax tablet now in the British Library preserves exactly that drill (Visual 1).

**Assembling material for composition** treats extracts as parts for a new work. Aulus Gellius, around AD 170, says his *Attic Nights* keeps "the fortuitous order of things" that he had already made while excerpting: whenever he took up a book, Greek or Latin, he noted whatever was worth remembering (praef. 2–3). The order of his book is the order of his reading.

One wax tablet could serve all four. What distinguishes them is the return: what you do the second time you look.

### Seneca's bees, Seneca's stomach

Seneca's Letter 84, written around AD 63–64, is the classic statement of what should happen on that return. Reading feeds the mind, he says, but we should neither only write nor only read; we should move between them "so that whatever reading has gathered, the *stilus* may reduce into a body" (84.2). The pointed stylus of the wax tablet is the instrument of digestion.

Then come the bees. We should imitate them, "as they say," since they wander and pluck the flowers suited to making honey, then arrange and distribute what they bring through the combs (84.3). Seneca pauses on a point of natural history he cannot settle: it is "not sufficiently established" whether bees draw a juice that is already honey, or whether they transform what they gather "by a kind of fermentation and mixing with their own breath" (84.4). The hesitation maps onto authorship: is a collection already the product, or does the collector change it?

Seneca chooses transformation. We should separate what we have gathered from varied reading, "for things are better kept when distinguished," and then, applying our own talent, blend those samplings into a single flavour, so that even if it shows where something came from, it is plainly something other than its source (84.5). Food that stays solid in the stomach is a burden; only when changed does it pass into strength and blood. "Let us digest it; otherwise it will go into the memory, not into the mind" (*in memoriam ibunt, non in ingenium*, 84.6–7). He wants Lucilius to resemble his models as a son resembles a father, not as a portrait does, "for a portrait is a dead thing" (84.8). A choir is made of many voices, yet one sound comes out (84.9–10).

Two features matter for a studio. First, Seneca keeps a stage of separation before fusion: the extracts are sorted, not immediately blended. Second, the endpoint conceals its sources. The mind should hide what helped it and show only what it has made (84.7). Elsewhere he is sharper still, mocking those who can only say "Zeno said this": "it is one thing to remember, another to know" (*Ep.* 33.7–8). Pliny the Elder, by contrast, names his sources. Book 1 of the *Natural History* lists the authorities behind every book, and the preface calls it a mark of honesty to acknowledge those from whom one has profited (praef. 21–23). Two ancient ethics of the copied word: digest until the source disappears, or keep the label on.

### Other people's hands

The vivid sources are also records of labour. Pliny's uncle could read nothing without excerpting because he rarely read alone. A *lector* read aloud; a *notarius* took notes or dictation; the gloves in the carriage were there to keep the writer's hands working in the cold, and Pliny presents them as a measure of the master's zeal. These readers and writers were very often enslaved or freed people. A papyrus from Oxyrhynchus (P.Oxy. IV 724, AD 155) records a man apprenticing an enslaved boy to a shorthand teacher for two years; a second-century wax tablet in the British Library preserves a student's shorthand notes. Candida Moss has recently argued that much ancient literary production depended on such literate enslaved workers whose contributions disappear behind a single author's name.

Access and leisure enter the same way. The uncle rose in the dark to attend the emperor Vespasian, served as a procurator, and held the wealth and connections that buy books, copyists, and time; his nephew says he himself feels "the laziest of men" by comparison (3.5.19). Seneca was one of the richest men in Rome. The Vindolanda birthday invitation (Visual 4) shows the arrangement at small scale: a professional hand, probably a household scribe, writes most of the letter, and Claudia Severa adds a few lines in her own hand. When we ask "who selected, who copied, who returned to the words," the honest answer in many documented cases is: more than one person, unequally.

### What the objects can and cannot show

The physical media are well attested. Wax tablets were wooden boards with a recessed, wax-filled surface, written on with a stylus and smoothed for reuse; several could be bound into a small book. Quintilian recommends wax for drafting because erasure is easiest, and parchment notebooks for those with weaker eyes, advising writers to leave blank space for later additions (*Inst.* 10.3.31–33). Thin wooden leaf tablets took ink. Papyrus rolls were normally written on one side; "opisthograph" notes on both sides, as in Pliny's description, signal private working copies rather than books for sale. Whether the uncle's 160 *commentarii* were rolls, parchment codices, or tablets is not stated; the term suggests rolls, but any reconstruction is conjecture.

The surviving objects line up unevenly with the literary practices. Wax and wooden tablets survive mostly as school exercises, accounts, contracts, and letters: recording and rehearsal, not excerpting for composition. The nearest artefact to a working manuscript full of extracts is a carbonized papyrus from Herculaneum, PHerc. 1021, a draft of Philodemus' history of the Academy with marginal additions, columns on the back, and verbatim excerpts from earlier authors (Visual 3). Tiziano Dorandi's reconstruction, in which Philodemus marked passages for a scribe to copy and later dictated linking prose, is persuasive but inferential. No surviving object is a personal notebook of extracts of the kind Pliny describes. The wall should say so.

### Foucault's *hypomnēmata*

Michel Foucault's 1983 essay "L'écriture de soi" ("Self Writing") made the word famous for modern readers. He treats *hypomnēmata* as a broad class of notebooks, including account books, registers, and personal memory aids, and argues that Greco-Roman writers used them to gather "the already-said" and make it into a self. He contrasts this with later Christian practices of writing down one's hidden thoughts for scrutiny. It is a powerful lens.

But it is an interpretation. Foucault assembles Plato, Seneca, Plutarch, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius into one technology across languages that did not share a single word or format. Seneca never calls his practice *hypomnēmata*; the evidence for ethical notebooks is mostly prescriptive or self-descriptive, not artefactual; and Pierre Hadot objected that Foucault's emphasis on fashioning a self underplays the Stoic aim of aligning oneself with universal reason. Use Foucault as a modern reader of the ancients, not as their spokesman.

### A studio experiment, and the machine in the room

For one week, keep a workbook in two columns. On the left, copy by hand ten short passages from at least three sources, each with an exact citation. Do not paraphrase yet. On day three, return and pick three: rewrite one as a maxim you could repeat, compress one into a sentence in your own words, and, following Seneca, fuse two from different sources into a single new paragraph that no longer quotes either. On day five, without looking, write down what you remember. Note which passages went "into memory" and which went "into the mind."

Then repeat the exercise with an AI: give it the same sources and ask it to select three passages and paraphrase them. Put its choices beside yours. Three things change. Selection moves elsewhere, much as it moved to the *lector* and *notarius*, though now the hidden labour is a training process and a data supply chain rather than an enslaved reader at your elbow, and the analogy should not erase that difference. Provenance becomes fragile: a fluent paraphrase can look like Seneca's honey while losing Pliny's labels, so check every paraphrase against its source. And the return, which is where the ancient practices did their work, can be skipped entirely. The experiment asks whether reading something an AI digested for you can ever count as your own digestion, and what you have to do, on day three and day five, to make it so.

## Wall label (150 words)

**Nothing Read Without Excerpting**

Around AD 103, Pliny the Younger described his uncle reading at dinner, in the bath, and in a carriage beside a scribe whose gloved hands kept writing through winter. "He read nothing without excerpting it." He left 160 notebooks of extracts. None survives.

Ancient readers copied words for different reasons: to record an event, to save a passage, to rehearse a maxim until it shaped behaviour, and to gather parts for new writing. Seneca compared the last to bees making honey and to digestion: borrowed words must be transformed, or "they will go into the memory, not into the mind."

Much of this work depended on enslaved readers and scribes, and on wealth and leisure.

The objects here are real but indirect: a schoolchild's wax tablet, a draft papyrus, a letter, a painting. Your workbook asks the old question again: what happens when you return to someone else's words?

### Visual 1 · School wax tablet with copied maxims

**Repository:** British Library, Add MS 34186(1). Catalogue: [https://searcharchives.bl.uk](https://searcharchives.bl.uk) (search "Add MS 34186"). CC0 image: [https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wax_writing_tablet\_(2nd_C)\_-\_BL_Add_MS_34186.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wax_writing_tablet_%282nd_C%29_-_BL_Add_MS_34186.jpg) **Date:** 2nd century AD; Roman Egypt. **Material:** Wooden codex of two wax tablets, written with a stylus on one side; holes in the frame for binding.

**Caption:** A teacher wrote two lines of Greek verse at the top (Menander, *Sententiae* 476: "Accept advice from someone wise; it is not right to trust every friend"), and a student copied them twice below, making mistakes, including twice dropping the first letter. This is the clearest surviving image of *rehearsing a maxim*. It cannot show adult ethical practice, private excerpting, or anything like Pliny's notebooks; it is schoolwork, and its purpose was handwriting as much as wisdom. See William A. Johnson, "A Trifle, Reprised," *BASP* 59 (2022).

### Visual 2 · Woman with wax tablets and stylus (the so-called "Sappho")

**Repository:** Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli, inv. 9084. Images: [https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Sappho_fresco\_(from_Pompeii)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Sappho_fresco_%28from_Pompeii%29) **Date:** c. AD 55–79; from Pompeii (Insula Occidentalis), excavated 1760. **Material:** Fresco (pigment on plaster), tondo about 37 × 38 cm.

**Caption:** A finely dressed woman holds a multi-leaf wax tablet and touches a stylus to her lips. The painting shows what these objects looked like in hand and that they could signal education and status. It cannot show what she is writing. The traditional label "Sappho" is a modern guess; the image is a portrait convention, not evidence of a notebook practice, and some scholars read the tablets as an emblem of household management rather than literature.

### Visual 3 · Philodemus' working draft, PHerc. 1021

**Repository:** Biblioteca Nazionale di Napoli, Officina dei Papiri Ercolanesi, PHerc. 1691/1021. Open-access imaging study with reproductions: [https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aav8936](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aav8936) (image rights: Ministero della Cultura / Biblioteca Nazionale di Napoli; permission needed for wall printing). **Date:** 1st century BC; carbonized in the eruption of AD 79 at the Villa of the Papyri, Herculaneum. **Material:** Carbonized papyrus roll, unrolled in fragments mounted on paper; written on both sides.

**Caption:** This is the nearest surviving thing to a writer's working copy assembled from excerpts: a draft history of Plato's Academy with marginal notes, additions above and below columns, text on the back, and verbatim quotations from earlier authors. It shows *assembling material for composition*. It cannot show a personal commonplace book, and the identification of it as Philodemus' own working manuscript, possibly involving a scribe who copied marked passages, is a scholarly reconstruction (Dorandi; Fleischer), not a fact written on the papyrus.

### Visual 4 · Vindolanda birthday invitation, Tab. Vindol. 291

**Repository:** British Museum, 1986,1001.64. Text and images: [https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/TabVindol291](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/TabVindol291) · Gallery page: [https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/galleries/roman-britain/vindolanda-tablets](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/galleries/roman-britain/vindolanda-tablets) **Date:** c. AD 97–105; Vindolanda, northern Britain. **Material:** Thin wooden leaf tablet written in carbon ink, about 22.3 × 9.6 cm.

**Caption:** Claudia Severa invites Sulpicia Lepidina to her birthday. Most of the text is in a practised scribal hand; Severa adds a closing in her own. The tablet makes visible two things the literary sources mention but rarely picture: writing as shared labour between a principal and a scribe, and the everyday medium of wood and ink. It is *recording and correspondence*, not excerpting; it shows nothing about reading.
