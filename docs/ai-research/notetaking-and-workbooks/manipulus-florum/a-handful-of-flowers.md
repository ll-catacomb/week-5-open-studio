## 1. What a florilegium is, and what the flowers mean

A florilegium is a book made out of other books. The word is a Latin calque on the Greek *anthologia*: *flos* (flower) + *legere* (to gather, to read), so "a gathering of flowers." Medieval florilegia were systematic collections of short extracts, mostly from the Church Fathers and selectively from classical moralists, lifted out of their original works and re-shelved under some other principle: by author, by theme, by the order of a biblical book, or — the late development that concerns us — by the alphabet.

The metaphor is doing more work than it first appears. A flower is the part of a plant you can carry away. It detaches cleanly, survives for a while on its own, and a bouquet is judged by the gatherer's eye rather than by the field. Thomas of Ireland, the Sorbonne *socius* who finished the *Manipulus florum* ("a handful of flowers") in 1306, actually stacks two harvest metaphors in his preface. He opens with Ruth gleaning behind the reapers in the field of Boaz, casting himself as "a pauper without any books" gleaning in the library of Robert of Sorbon, "behind the backs of the doctors." Then he borrows Seneca's bees (*Epistle* 84): gather from many flowers, arrange what you bring home in the comb, and blend the samples "into one flavour so that, even if their origin should be known, yet they will appear to be different than the place from which they are taken" ([Nighman's translation of the preface](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/Preface.pdf), from Rouse and Rouse 1979, pp. 236–8).

Gleaning and honey-making describe two different ethics of extraction. The gleaner leaves the field intact and takes only what the owner has dropped; Thomas closes the preface by warning the reader not to "despise the fertile original field on account of these paltry ears of grain." The bee transforms what it takes into something that no longer tastes of its source. The *Manipulus* is both at once, and the tension between them is the subject of this essay.

## 2. Two dates: the compilation and the copies

The compilation is dated by its own colophon and preface to 1306, in Paris, by a man who describes himself as a former fellow of the Sorbonne and who compiled it from that college's library (the IRHT's FAMA record, [œuvre 268409](https://fama.irht.cnrs.fr/en/oeuvre/268409), is the authority file for the work; the Complutense group's [MANIPULUS database](https://manipulus.ucm.es/handle/20.500.12238/243) records it as "c. 1306" and inventories sixteen Spanish witnesses). No autograph survives. The Rouses identified three early Paris manuscripts as the closest witnesses: **BnF lat. 15985** (the copy Thomas gave to Nicholas of Bar, who bequeathed it to the Sorbonne before his death in 1310), **BnF lat. 15986**, and **Bibliothèque Mazarine 1032**, the stationer's exemplar whose pecia marks the Rouses tabulated ([Nighman, About page](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/about.html); Rouse and Rouse 1979, App. 3). From there the text travelled with unusual stability: over 180 manuscripts by the Rouses' count (Nighman now says over 200) and more than fifty printed editions between the Piacenza *editio princeps* of 1483 and a Paris printing of 1887.

Each copy therefore has its own date, and that date is never 1306. The witnesses I worked from in preparing this essay are:

- **BnF lat. 15986**, early fourteenth century, Paris — digitized on [Gallica](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b9076618v), but from a black-and-white microfilm, so rubrication is invisible. The BnF catalogue record is [here](https://archivesetmanuscrits.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cc76483r).
- **Ghent, Universiteitsbibliotheek HS.2582/291**, three paper bifolia from a copy dated 1351–1400 in the [Fragmentarium record](https://fragmentarium.ms/overview/F-kgjw) — colour, IIIF, Public Domain Mark.
- **Piacenza, Jacobus de Tyela, 5 September 1483**, the first printed edition — [BSB/MDZ digital facsimile](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb00069777?page=,1).
- Nighman's open-access [critical edition](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/browse.html), collated from the three Paris manuscripts and five early printings, with per-entry apparatus PDFs.

## 3. The interface

**Alphabet.** 266 topics run from *Abstinencia* to *Christus* — *Christus* comes last because it is abbreviated with the Greek letters *Xpc*, and the alphabet is applied letter by letter within the word, so that, as Thomas insists, *Conuersacio* precedes *Conuersio*. He says he did this "in the manner of concordances," which dates the method: the alphabetical verbal concordance to the Bible was a thirteenth-century Dominican invention, and the Rouses' larger argument ("Statim invenire," 1982) is that the *Manipulus* belongs to a family of tools built so that a preacher could *find immediately* rather than read through.

**Reference letters.** Under each topic every extract carries a letter in the margin: a, b, c … z, then ab, ac, ad. *Vsura* has two entries; *Mors*, the largest topic, runs to 97 and ends at *di*. The letters exist so that an extract can live in one place and be pointed to from many.

**The *Ubi* list.** At the end of nearly every topic sits a list headed *Ubi* ("where"): other topics, sometimes bare (meaning "everything under that heading applies"), sometimes followed by letters (meaning "only those entries"). This is a cross-reference system in the strict sense, and Thomas explains it as a way "to avoid repeating quotations under different topics."

**Attribution in two registers.** The source is named in full inside the text block — "Augustinus libro X confessionum" — and abbreviated again in the margin. In lat. 15986, fol. 6r (the opening of *Adulacio*), the margin reads *Aug* against an in-text "Augustinus super Psalmos" (Rouse and Rouse 1979, p. 31). The margin is the scanning register; the text block is the citing register. Thomas is candid that he cannot give chapter numbers, "because in different books they are arranged in different ways, and often the same quotation is ascribed to various authors."

**Ordering within a topic.** Augustine first, the Fathers roughly by weight, and Seneca always last, with a Senecan justification: "the last drink delights the person devoted to wine."

**Bibliography.** At the end Thomas appended a list of the books he used, with incipits and explicits "so that they can be known more easily and ascribed more accurately" — effectively a works-cited list, edited by the Rouses (pp. 251–310).

All of this had to be copied. Nighman's edition counts 5,821 entries holding roughly 6,000 quotations; the Tortosa copy (Archivo Capitular Ms. 75) fills 128 parchment folios. In Paris the stationers broke the exemplar into *peciae* and rented them out quire by quire, which is why the text is so stable and why so many copies exist. A reader had to run the alphabet letter by letter, know the abbreviations (*Aug.*, *Greg.*, *Bern.*, the *-us* hook, the *con-* and *per-* sigla), track a marginal letter across a column, and flip to another topic to find a specific letter there. The design lives in the margins.

## 4. Retrieval, reconstructed: *Curiositas*

*This section is a reconstruction. No source documents a particular user working through this topic. Documented users are discussed at the end of the section.*

Suppose a Parisian master is asked to preach to students on the verse *In superuacuis rebus noli scrutari multipliciter* — "do not pry into needless things" (Ecclesiasticus 3:22). He wants authorities on idle curiosity.

**Locate.** He runs the alphabet to C, then within C past *Cupiditas* to [*Curiositas*](http://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/lemmata/Curiositas.html). Forty-one entries, lettered a through at. In the margins he sees the author abbreviations clump: a run of *Aug.*, then *Ier.*, *Greg.*, *Bern.*, *Cris.*, *Prosp.*, *Hugo*, *Ric.*, *Petr. Rau.*, a *Glossa*, and then a long tail of *Sen.*

**Read the headings and attributions.** The first entry, *Curiositas a*, is Augustine from *Confessions* X: a curious race of men, eager to know others' lives, lazy to correct their own. *Curiositas e* gives him the sermon-ready antithesis "humble piety finds the maker of the stars more easily than proud curiosity finds their order." *Curiositas u* is a definition he can put at the head of a *distinctio*: Hugh of St Victor, "curiosity is the superfluous investigation of things that do not pertain to oneself." Near the end, *Curiositas ad* turns out to be the very gloss on his *thema* verse: "you have found honey; eat what is enough for you."

**Follow the cross-references.** The *Ubi* list sends him to *Doctrina x* and *ap*, *Philosophia l*, *Studium ag*, *Error g*, *Iudex u*, *Meritum c*, *Consciencia o*. He turns to *Studium ag* for an entry on the right kind of study, so that his sermon can distinguish *curiositas* from *studiositas*.

**Put them to work.** The preacher's sermon form in 1306 is the divided sermon: a *thema*, a division into members, each member confirmed by authorities. The *Manipulus* is organised for exactly this. Three members fall out of the entries almost on their own: curiosity about the neighbour's life (a, l, m), curiosity about creation that forgets the Creator (e, f, g, ap), and curiosity in the schools — syllogisms about a mouse eating cheese (am), "sophisms that neither harm the ignorant nor help the learned" (af). He cites each as the margin gives it, "Augustinus in libro Confessionum," "Seneca XLIX epistola," and nobody expects a chapter number.

**Documented users.** Christine Boyer (2006) showed that the Dominican Guillaume de Sauqueville was mining the *Manipulus* for sermons within a few years of 1306 (his collection is BnF lat. 16495). Nighman has traced it into a 1332 sermon on friendship by King Robert of Naples, into Walter Bower's *Scotichronicon*, and — via Matthew Steggle — into Thomas Nashe's English prose in the 1590s. Clemens Weidmann (2020) found that a set of "pseudo-Augustine" sermons printed in the nineteenth century were in fact composed in the mid-fourteenth century by a preacher working from the *Manipulus*: the florilegium generated new "Augustine."

## 5. Who chose, what changed, what world resulted

**Who selected.** Thomas presents himself as a gleaner, but the Rouses showed that he gleaned largely from other gleaners: two early thirteenth-century Cistercian florilegia, the *Flores paradysi* and the *Liber exceptionum*; the twelfth-century *Florilegium Gallicum* and *Florilegium Angelicum* for classical matter; and intermediate reference works he cites by name — the *Glossa ordinaria*, Gratian's *Decretum*, and an anonymous collection he calls "Prouerbia philosophorum." The selecting hand is therefore plural and layered, and mostly monastic, with a Paris secular master doing the final arrangement.

**How excerpts changed.** Nighman's apparatus, which prints Thomas's text beside the modern edition of the source, shows that a large share of the "quotations" are paraphrased, trimmed, interpolated, or spliced from two or more passages. *Curiositas y* is a clean specimen. The *Manipulus* labels it "Petrus Rauennas in sermone" — Peter Chrysologus, the fifth-century bishop of Ravenna. Nighman's [source file](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/MFfontes/CuriositasY.pdf) traces it instead to Peter of Blois's *Epistle* 101 (twelfth century), which was itself lifting John of Salisbury's *Metalogicon* 2.8, which was in turn stitching together Seneca the Elder's *Controversiae* and Seneca the Younger's *Epistle* 82. Four hands, two Senecas, one wrong Peter. The excerpt arrived as a timeless patristic *sententia* and is in fact a twelfth-century polemic against the Paris schools wearing a borrowed name.

**What world resulted.** The list of 266 lemmata is a moral map. It is overwhelmingly virtues, vices, estates, and sacraments; the headings for intellectual life (*Curiositas*, *Studium*, *Doctrina*, *Philosophia*, *Sapiencia*, *Ingenium*) are present but framed by warning. Within a topic, order is argument: Augustine opens, Seneca closes, and the arrangement tells the reader what counts as weight. Thomas's own disclaimer flattens authority even further: "every truth spoken by whomever is of the Holy Spirit." Once a saying is on the page under a heading, its origin is a courtesy, not a condition.

## 6. Three kinds of gathering

It is tempting to say the *Manipulus* is "a medieval commonplace book" or "a medieval course pack." Neither is right, and the differences are the point.

A **personal commonplace book** is made by its reader, out of that reader's reading, for that reader's recall and later composition. Thomas's preface actually describes one — "I collected the ears of grain of original sources… from various books" — and then describes the moment it stopped being one: "realizing that they were not organized and so would not be of much use to anyone else after me, I have concisely gathered them here… in alphabetical order." The commonplace book is private, cumulative, and ordered by the life of the person keeping it. The florilegium is public, finished, and ordered by a system anyone can operate. Ann Moss (1996) showed that the *Manipulus* was the one medieval florilegium that converted cleanly into the printed Renaissance commonplace book, which is a sign of how systematic it already was.

A **modern curated source pack** — the readings assembled for a seminar — resembles the *Manipulus* in that someone else chose the extracts and the audience trusts the choosing. It differs in almost everything else. A course pack gives long enough passages that context survives; it is built for sequential reading over a bounded term; it is assembled for one audience by one teacher and is expected to be revised. The *Manipulus* gives passages of a sentence or two, is built for random access, and was copied unchanged for six centuries.

What all three share is the thing worth noticing: in each, retrieval has been traded against context, and the trade is made by whoever holds the scissors.

## 7. Wall label (150 words)

**A Handful of Flowers**
*Thomas of Ireland, Manipulus florum. Compiled Paris, 1306. Copies shown: early 14th c., late 14th c., and the first printing of 1483.*

A florilegium is a book made from other books: short "flowers" cut from the Church Fathers and classical moralists and re-planted under new headings. Thomas of Ireland, a poor scholar gleaning in the Sorbonne library, gathered about 6,000 of them under 266 topics from *Abstinencia* to *Christus*. The design is the interface: alphabetical order "in the manner of concordances," a reference letter beside every extract, an author's name abbreviated in the margin, and a *Where* list at the end of each topic pointing to related entries elsewhere. A preacher could find authorities on any vice or virtue in minutes. The cost was context. Sources were trimmed, spliced, and sometimes misnamed, and the arrangement itself quietly ranked who counted as an authority. Over 200 manuscripts and fifty printed editions later, the questions it raises about curated quotation are ours.

## 9. Exercise: build a *manipulus* of your own

Forty-five minutes, in pairs. Materials: one topic word, five sources (books, PDFs, transcripts — at least one must be something you did not choose), index cards or a plain text file.

1. **Pick a lemma** that is live for you this term — *Attention*, *Draft*, *Rehearsal*, *Prompt*, *Credit*. Spell out its alphabetical neighbours; decide what it is *not* (Thomas separates *Curiositas* from *Studium*).
2. **Glean twelve flowers**, each one to three sentences, each copied exactly. Letter them a–m in the margin. Record the source in two registers: a two-letter abbreviation in the margin and a full citation in the text — and, like Thomas, write down what you *cannot* verify (page? edition? did you read the original or someone quoting it?).
3. **Order them** and say why. Thomas puts Augustine first and Seneca last. Who is your dessert?
4. **Write the *Ubi* list**: three other lemmata (yours or a classmate's) and the specific letters that belong under them.
5. **Swap stacks.** Using only your partner's cards, draft the opening of something — a toast, a programme note, a pitch — in ten minutes. Then open one of their sources and read the paragraph around one flower. Report what the cutting removed.
6. **Optional AI pass.** Ask a model to produce the same twelve for your lemma. Check each one against a source. Count the Peters of Ravenna.

## 10. The question that stays

Thomas's Peter of Ravenna was a real misattribution with a traceable path — Seneca to John of Salisbury to Peter of Blois to a Paris margin — and the error was *stable*: it was copied faithfully for 600 years because the system was built to reproduce, not to re-check. The question for us is not whether a machine that assembles quotations will make such errors; it will. It is whether we are building the equivalent of Thomas's margin — the visible author mark, the honest note that the chapter number is unknown, the *Ubi* list that lets a reader walk back to the field — or only the equivalent of his honey, blended "so that it appears different than the place from which it was taken."

When a quotation arrives already arranged under the heading you were looking for, by a hand you cannot see, out of a field you have not visited: what, exactly, is its authority, and whose labour are you being glad of?
