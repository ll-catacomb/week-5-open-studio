# Fieldnotes and evidence — whose observation becomes whose argument?

## The question

How does an encounter become evidence? A researcher watches somebody work, listens to a story, or participates in an activity. Later, an essay describes a social world and advances an argument about it. Between those moments lies more than storage: decisions about what deserves attention, what words can stand for an event, which observations belong together, and which person can claim authority over the description.

The central argument of this report is that **fieldnotes are an intermediate form of authorship**. They preserve something while also making something. Their incompleteness need not disqualify them, but their provenance matters: who witnessed an event, who explained it, when the account was written, and how later prose changes its scope. A polished description may be compelling precisely because it hides these intermediate decisions. Studying the working record lets us ask whether that polish clarifies the evidence or outruns it.

The cases below concern three historical projects and a later methodological intervention. They are deliberately unequal. Malinowski supplies a powerful distinction between kinds of diary; Hurston makes the written notebook only one component of fieldwork; Orwell offers a particularly visible note-to-publication comparison; Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw make the construction of notes itself teachable. This is a set of questions for reading evidence, rather than a genealogy claiming that each writer improved on the previous one.

## Malinowski: the ethnographic diary and the private diary

In the introduction to *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922), Bronisław Malinowski makes everyday behavior an essential part of ethnographic knowledge. Rules and institutional outlines alone cannot describe how people actually live. He proposes detailed observations recorded in an ethnographic diary, alongside organized documentation and collections of utterances. He also acknowledges the observer's influence, and recommends sometimes putting away recording equipment and participating. These are instructions within a finished book, not a surviving transcription of every step he followed. [*Argonauts*, Introduction, especially §§VII–IX, pp. 17–25](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55822/55822-h/55822-h.htm).

That proposed ethnographic diary must be distinguished from the personal writing published posthumously as *A Diary in the Strict Sense of the Term* in 1967. Clifford Geertz's contemporary review describes Polish-language entries from 1914–15 and 1917–18 dominated by distant relationships, anxieties, and hostility, including racist language toward the people among whom Malinowski worked. Geertz treated the publication as a challenge to the discipline's sentimental image of the uniquely empathetic fieldworker. This is evidence about reception supplied by a major anthropologist; it is not a substitute for reading the diary in an edition or its manuscript. [Geertz, “Under the Mosquito Net,” 14 September 1967](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1967/09/14/under-the-mosquito-net/).

The distinction changes the notebook story. A private journal might regulate its writer's attention, moods, or ambitions without being designed as a public evidentiary record. Conversely, systematic fieldnotes can contain affect and interpretation without becoming merely personal. Calling both things a diary does not establish that one was the draft of the other. Neither should the private text automatically be declared the authentic truth behind an allegedly false ethnography. A more productive inquiry compares the purposes, audiences, and exclusions of each document, while taking prejudice seriously as a condition affecting observation and relationship.

There is also evidence for arrangement beyond chronological entries. Alexander Street's preview catalog describes an apparent index to Malinowski's field notebooks, containing page references and another numbering scheme whose purpose remains uncertain. It describes writing on reused paper, including a Seligman letter. The catalog's title is “Index to Field Notebooks, 1915–18”; its summary calls twelve pages undated, while the record gives a larger overall page count. The images were not accessible in this research. This is a promising object-level lead, not proof of a fully reconstructed indexing method. [Alexander Street, bibliographic entity 3246245](https://search.alexanderstreet.com/preview/work/bibliographic_entity%7Cbibliographic_details%7C3246245).

The political setting belongs inside the account of evidence. LSE curator Inderbir Bhullar documents Malinowski's promotion of “Practical Anthropology” for imperial administration and his cultivation of links with colonial authorities. The same discussion includes his criticism of discriminatory education in South Africa. These tensions resist a simple division between scientific care and political complicity: knowledge of another society could recognize its institutions while being offered to those governing it. [Bhullar, “Malinowski and colonialism”](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsehistory/2022/12/15/malinowski-and-colonialism/).

For a notebook history, the further question is who made the record possible. A book's author can organize statements into an argument, but the people who explain practices supply intellectual work, not just observable behavior. Without reconstructing particular collaborations, we should not assign unnamed assistants tasks the sources have not established. We can nevertheless notice that the published author and the people whose knowledge enables the publication occupy different positions. That difference is part of the material history of the notes.

## Hurston: a field record distributed across bodies, paper, and discs

Zora Neale Hurston's 1939 *Proposed Recording Expedition into the Floridas* offers a different entry point. This is a research proposal, not an ordinary field notebook. It arranges Florida into areas, linking occupations, migration, and cultural practices; songs attributed to named performers punctuate the plan. It proposes interviews as well as recordings. The accompanying May 23 letter from Florida Federal Writers' Project director Carita Doggett Corse recommends Hurston's participation because of her knowledge of sources. Field knowledge becomes an organized case for allocating institutional attention and equipment. [LOC machine-readable transcription, PDF pages 2–7](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/afc/afc_corresp/afc_corresp_essay1/afc_corresp_essay1.pdf).

The proposal should be read closely rather than celebrated indiscriminately. Its concluding treatment of Asian populations in California as outside the American cultural stream marks a serious limitation in its inclusive portrait. Its categories themselves are evidence of a historical perspective. A researcher can resist the erasure of some people while reproducing boundaries around others. This matters when arranging notes by headings: headings decide which relations can be seen and which people appear as belonging elsewhere.

The archive also unsettles the idea that recording means writing down. In “Halimuhfack,” Hurston herself performs a song learned on Florida's east coast, then discusses collecting and learning songs. LOC identifies the recording as made in Jacksonville on June 18, 1939, credits Herbert Halpert and Stetson Kennedy as collectors, and places it in the Southern States Recording Expedition collection. Its physical carrier was a twelve-inch analog sound disc; the catalog notes that it ends abruptly. [LOC item flwpa000014, AFC 1939/005: AFS 03138 B02](https://www.loc.gov/item/flwpa000014/).

The Library's teaching discussion locates Hurston's methodological account at 1:25 and describes listening, gradually joining in, and finally singing independently for the source singer's approval. The audio was not independently auditioned for this report; the method and timestamp here follow that institutional account. [Eileen Manchester, “Teaching Civics with Musical Primary Sources”](https://blogs.loc.gov/teachers/2026/01/teaching-civics-with-musical-primary-sources/).

This is consequential for notes becoming writing. A performance can function as remembered evidence without being a verbatim written transcript. Learning depends on a relationship in which the source performer can correct the collector. In my reading, this offers a concrete instance of co-production at the point of acquisition: the eventual record depends on another person's competence and judgment. That limited conclusion does not establish shared control over publication, documented permission for every subsequent use, or the provenance of every song in Hurston's books.

Nor does Hurston's familiarity with a setting make mediation disappear. In the surviving item she is collector, performer, and methodological speaker; Halpert's and Kennedy's names enter the institutional record; the source singer of this particular song is not named in the item description accessed here. “Voice” therefore requires specificity. We hear, or can listen to, Hurston's rendition; we should not call it an unmediated recording of its earlier performer. The field archive contains layers of substitution, attribution, memory, and administrative description.

For an essay writer, this case expands what counts as a working record. A proposal may already contain an argument; a performance may store something prose cannot; a catalog may preserve relationships while also losing others. The productive question is not which medium is best, but what each transformation makes available and what it makes harder to recover. A notes system that stores only the resulting paragraph would erase this distinction.

## Orwell: from a particular household to a public claim

George Orwell's journey through industrial northern England in 1936 produced a diary and *The Road to Wigan Pier* (1937). Historian Ben Pimlott explains that publisher Victor Gollancz commissioned the investigation and that Orwell used introductions from journalists and political activists. Pimlott emphasizes the author's middle-class position and the book's interrogation of how people of that background relate to working people. Access was socially organized, and the observer arrived with a publishing destination. [Pimlott, introduction to *Orwell's England*](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/articles/ben-pimlott-introduction-to-orwells-england/).

A small comparison makes the transformation visible. The diary entry dated March 15 describes “G.” returning from the pit, washing before eating, cleaning himself in a basin, and receiving help from his wife with his back. Orwell explicitly registers uncertainty about whether washing before food is usual. In the entry, bodily detail sits among meetings, conversations, future visits, and other observations. [Orwell Foundation diary presentation, entry “15.3.36”](https://theroadtowiganpier.wordpress.com/).

Part One, chapter III of the published book reorganizes similar material into a generalized account of miners' washing, with eating commonly placed first. It connects domestic difficulty to bathing facilities and answers a middle-class accusation about miners' unwillingness to wash. The similarity is strong evidence of textual reuse or development, but the changed meal order warns against treating the public passage as a simple cleaned transcript. [*The Road to Wigan Pier*, Part One, III, paragraphs beginning “As soon as the miner comes above ground” and following](https://www.marxists.org/archive/orwell/1937/road-wigan-pier.htm).

My inference from this comparison is that arrangement changes argumentative force. An entry about one person's routine becomes a description that asks readers to reconsider moral judgment. The essay's claim depends on selection and comparison across observations, not the vividness of one scene alone. It also depends on the reader's accepting the writer's generalization. The diary's qualification is valuable because it makes that generalization available for scrutiny.

The example exposes another form of labor: the wife helping wash the miner's back. Her action contributes to the scene's explanatory power, yet it remains within Orwell's narration. A contemporary reconstruction should ask what her own account might add, without inventing it. Did she understand this as care, obligation, routine, inconvenience, or something else? The observed action cannot answer that question by itself. A description can show social dependence while still withholding the perspectives of some people who sustain it.

## Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw: making construction inspectable

*Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes*, first published in 1995 and revised in 2011, treats writing notes as a learned practice. The second-edition excerpt distinguishes participation from systematic inscription and treats jottings as memory aids rather than complete accounts. On printed pp. 29–34, brief observations provide anchors for fuller writing. On p. 41, an elder's hesitation when his name is written is connected to colonial officials' earlier name-recording practices. The authors show that putting pen to paper can itself become a socially significant event. [Second-edition excerpt, chapters 1–2](https://www.landscapeandpower.com/uploads/1/4/9/7/14977250/emerson_from_writing_ethnographic_fieldnotes.pdf).

The publisher's account confirms the book's use of unfinished notes and its attention to organizing and transforming them into finished texts. It also identifies revisions addressing race, class, gender, coding programs, and drafts. This establishes the intervention's scope, not independent evidence that following its advice improves every research outcome. [University of Chicago Press description](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/W/bo12182616.html).

The distinction I would carry into essay work is between **description, interpretation, and the status of a claim**. These can coexist on a page if the writer marks their relations. A recollected sentence is different from a recording; an observed gesture is different from a motive attributed to it; an analytic hunch is different from a pattern established across occasions. A useful memo identifies the question, links the evidence, proposes an interpretation, and records what could contradict it. This is a classroom design proposed here, rather than an attribution of a particular memo format to the book.

## What the comparison changes

Together these cases suggest that good research notes should retain the conditions under which evidence was made. Chronology helps establish sequence, but arrangement by topic makes comparison possible; both can be valuable if the later system preserves access to the earlier record. Likewise, keeping a private reflection alongside analytic notes may help expose the observer's assumptions, but a personal confession does not validate a public generalization.

Participant voices should be treated as contributions with their own purposes. Quoting someone does not automatically give them authority over the interpretation. Asking them to correct a description can improve it, but disagreement may persist and should remain visible. Co-production is a question about who can shape the record and the argument, not a flattering name for any encounter. These histories help a writer ask a precise question at each transition: **What now licenses this sentence, and whose knowledge does it depend on?**

## A partial source → note → arrangement → output chain

The Orwell comparison above gives two evidenced textual endpoints, not a complete genetic history:

1. **Encounter:** the March 15 diary reports a household routine. The encounter is known through Orwell's account, not independently witnessed by this researcher.
2. **Written note:** the dated entry records a particular person, the order of activities, assistance, and uncertainty about typicality.
3. **Arrangement:** the published material belongs to a chapter on miners' everyday conditions rather than the day's chronological miscellany. This placement is directly inspectable.
4. **Memo/draft:** no intervening memo or draft for this passage was inspected. A memo asking whether facilities explain washing practices would be a plausible teaching reconstruction, not a recovered historical document.
5. **Output:** chapter III combines bodily description with an argument against a class judgment. Comparison supports a connection; it does not identify every other observation, statistical source, or revision that contributed.

## Compact comparison

| Case | Working record or evidence | Transformative operation | What requires caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Malinowski | Proposed ethnographic diary; separate private diary; cataloged notebook index | Recording everyday behavior; organizing knowledge for monographs | Do not merge diary genres or infer a complete workflow from catalog metadata |
| Hurston | Proposal, named song examples, remembered performance, disc and item record | Geographic organization; learning and re-performing; institutional attribution | Performer correction is not proof of shared publication control |
| Orwell | Dated diary and published washing passage | Chronological particulars become topical description and argument | One household cannot alone establish a general pattern |
| Emerson/Fretz/Shaw | Published examples of jottings and field relations | Teaching inscription as a choice within participation | A manual's demonstration is not an outcome study |

## Two feasible week 5 experiments

**1. One scene, three records — 40 minutes.** Use a staged five-minute classroom activity with willing participants, such as a group assembling a paper structure. Assign observers different positions. Each produces jottings, a fuller description immediately afterward, and a separate memo proposing one explanation. Mark remembered speech as remembered rather than verbatim. Participants then annotate a description with corrections or alternative explanations. Preserve both versions. Write a 150-word argument with links to the specific evidence and one unresolved disagreement. Compare which conclusions changed when participants could respond. This tests the construction of evidence without recruiting unsuspecting people as subjects.

**2. Audit a generalization — 35 minutes.** Read Orwell's March 15 entry alongside the chapter III washing passage. Use three colors for retained details, changed scope/order, and newly introduced claims. Produce a table separating what the two texts establish from what would require another source. Then write a clearly labeled reconstructed memo that could guide further observation, followed by two alternative paragraph openings: one beginning with the household, one beginning with the class stereotype. Discuss how the opening changes what the same evidence appears to prove. Do not call the reconstruction Orwell's memo.

## Gaps and further research

The largest gap is a complete manuscript-to-draft chain. Malinowski's index images could show how topical references actually worked; comparing specific notes to a published passage would require further archive access. Reading the private diary in Polish and its translations would also complicate reception based on the 1967 English edition.

Hurston's proposal and recording are adjacent evidence, not a demonstrated source history for a particular passage in *Mules and Men* or another book. The next study should trace one song or narrative across a named source, original record, intermediate text, and publication. It should also seek accounts by the people supplying knowledge, rather than assuming that a collector's papers preserve the whole relationship.

Orwell's passage needs checking against a reliable edition and surviving working papers. The example establishes a strong textual correspondence, but cannot determine whether the changed meal order came from additional observations, revision for a generalized account, or another source. That uncertainty is itself worth teaching: a plausible chain should not be mistaken for a recovered one.
