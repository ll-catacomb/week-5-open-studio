# Before the First Mark

Getting your materials together, from the school-supply aisle to the rehearsal room. An illustrated essay for TDM 155AI and the Learning Lab's Week 5 exhibition on research, notebooks, workbooks, dramaturgy, and preparation.

## Two scenes

It is late August, and an eighth grader is standing in a store aisle holding a printed list from school: two composition books, a pack of pencils, a protractor, a three-ring binder. The list is precise and impersonal. The aisle is not. There are binders in six colors, gel pens in forty, a backpack with a sleeve for a laptop she doesn't own yet. She is not only buying what the list asks for. Item by item, she is deciding who will walk into homeroom in two weeks.

A few years later the same person is a first-year college student holding a syllabus. Somewhere in its first pages is a list of required books, a few "recommended" ones, and a column of dates. Getting those books — new, used, borrowed, downloaded, scanned from a reserve shelf — is the first assignment of the course, though no one grades it. Stacked on a dorm desk, spines out and unread, the books say something back to the student: *this is what you will know by December.*

These scenes are so ordinary they are nearly invisible. But they are versions of something older and wider: a cook laying out her station before service, a stage manager taping a floor plan onto a rehearsal-room floor, a scholar building a card index, a painter letting a studio fill with source material. Before people make things, they gather things. This essay looks at what they actually gather, how they arrange it, and what they hope the arrangement will do.

## The list and the aisle

Getting ready for school is now a large economy. The National Retail Federation's 2026 survey found that families with K–12 students [planned to spend about $864 per household, of which only about $146 went to school supplies proper](https://www.cheapism.com/back-to-school-shopping-costs/); electronics, clothing, and shoes took the rest. Back-to-college spending [passed $100 billion for the first time](https://www.nbcdfw.com/news/local/back-to-school-spending-record-high-rising-costs-survey/4057074/). Nearly half of shoppers [said they would buy only the essentials and replenish later](https://boardretailers.org/back-to-school-spending-surge-set-to-break-records-report-by-samantha-schober-via-nrf-newsletter-report/) — a reminder that this is also a season of budgeting and worry. And survey researchers report that [children's own preferences now rival the school's list](https://www.cheapism.com/back-to-school-shopping-costs/) as an influence on what gets bought.

That last finding has a history. In August 1978, Mead test-marketed a new binder system in Wichita, Kansas, with [a feedback card inside each one offering a free binder in exchange for comments; about 1,500 came back](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/52726/history-trapper-keeper). The Trapper Keeper — whose invention is [still credited differently by the two Mead employees involved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trapper_Keeper) — became one of the most coveted objects in the American locker. It promised organization. It also promised a look.

Even early manufactured supplies sold an identity along with a function. One of the first Crayola sets, made in 1903 and now in the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, is labeled [*No. 51, Young Artists Drawing Crayons, for coloring Maps, Pictures*](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/search/object/nmah_1196565). The box does not say "crayons for children." It says *young artists*. The name Crayola was coined by Alice Binney, [a former schoolteacher](https://si.edu/newsdesk/snapshot/crayola-crayons). *(Figure 1.)*

## The syllabus and the shelf

At Harvard, acquiring course materials began as collective action. The Harvard Cooperative Society traces its roots to 1882, [when students pooled money to buy coal and wood in bulk and then did the same with textbooks](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/08/12/business/harvard-coop-bookstore/); membership still costs a dollar. Earlier still, in 1729, [a Harvard student made his own handwritten copy of a textbook by Charles Morton](https://takenote.library.harvard.edu/74-solution-cost-textbooks) — acquiring a book by writing it out, which is also a way of reading it very slowly.

The rituals keep changing. Harvard's faculty [voted in 2022 to end shopping week](https://thecrimson.com/article/2022/5/4/fas-shopping-week-vote), the decades-old period in which students sampled courses before committing, in favor of [registration in the previous term, beginning with spring 2024 courses](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2023/10/30/faculty-course-registration-reactions/). This past August the Coop [opened a new store in Allston without the towering textbook shelves that once defined it](https://hoodline.com/2026/08/harvard-coop-ditches-bookshelves-for-dj-sets-bets-future-on-allston-prototype/). The stack of books on the dorm desk is becoming a folder of PDFs and a page of links. The question it poses — *am I the kind of person who will read all this?* — has not changed.

Walter Benjamin wrote the classic account of that feeling. In ["Unpacking My Library"](https://www.tetragrammaton.com/article/unpacking-my-library-1931) (1931), he invites us into a room of pried-open crates and nearly two thousand books, and describes his mood not as nostalgia but as anticipation. The books are "not yet touched by the mild boredom of order." Benjamin also retells a story about Anatole France, who, asked by a visitor whether he had read all his books, admitted to not even a tenth of them, and asked in return whether the visitor ate off his best china every day. A library, in this view, is not a record of completed tasks. It is a set of possibilities kept within reach.

## Five rooms

**The kitchen.** In professional kitchens, preparation has a name: *mise en place*, "put in place." Anthony Bourdain called it [the religion of all good line cooks](https://lrb.co.uk/v22/n23/steven-shapin/a-mans-mans-world). In *Kitchen Confidential* (2000) he describes a cook's station — salt, cracked pepper, softened butter, oil, backups — as something like an extension of the cook's nervous system, arranged so the hand finds things without the eyes. The point is not tidiness. It is to make fast, improvisatory work possible.

Julia Child's kitchen in Cambridge, which her husband Paul designed when they moved in in 1961, shows what that looks like when it becomes architecture. [Pots, pans, and tools hung on pegboard, and Paul outlined each one in black marker](https://americanhistory.si.edu/press/fact-sheets/julia-childs-kitchen) so anyone could put it back. [Small Polaroids were added where similar shapes might be confused](https://lithub.com/how-to-arrange-your-kitchen-according-to-julia-child/), because assistants used the room too — and, between 1994 and 2001, [it served as the set for three television series](https://americanhistory.si.edu/press/fact-sheets/julia-childs-kitchen). When Child gave the kitchen to the Smithsonian, [curators took in some 1,200 artifacts](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/julia-childs-pots-and-pans-are-back-in-her-kitchen-13171001/), down to the contents of the drawers. The pegboard outlines are habit made visible: a drawing of where each thing belongs, which also shows at a glance what is missing. *(Figure 3.)*

**The rehearsal room.** Theatre companies begin by building a version of a world that does not exist yet. Before the first rehearsal, stage managers [take the designer's ground plan and tape it onto the floor at scale](https://sites.google.com/oberlin.edu/oberlin-sm-project/procedures/pre-rehearsal-process), measuring from a center line and plaster line, sometimes in different colors for different scenes. The first days often bring a read-through and a showing of the model box, a scaled miniature of the set, whose images may then be [photographed and pinned to the rehearsal-room walls](https://www.mgcfutures.com/henry-v-rehearsal-diary-week1/). A member of Ireland's Druid company wrote that [the first day of rehearsal always feels like the first day of school](https://www.druid.ie/about/news/druidocasey-rehearsal-diary-1). Scripts, schedules, and contact sheets are handed out. The room is arranged so that bodies can learn a space before they ever stand in it.

Bertolt Brecht pushed theatrical preparation in an unusual direction: forward in time. For his 1949 Berlin production of *Mother Courage*, he and his collaborators — especially the photographer Ruth Berlau — produced a *Modellbuch*, [a meticulous photographic record of the staging, published with rehearsal notes and commentary for future directors](https://americanrepertorytheater.org/media/a-model-of-courage/). The *Couragemodell* eventually appeared [in three volumes: the text, scene photographs, and notes](https://search.worldcat.org/title/couragemodell-1949/oclc/17090789). Its makers [typed notes, cut them out, and pasted them beside the photographs, and some images were blurred on purpose to show how actors moved](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-20394-7_9). A model book is preparation handed to strangers: a set of answers that asks to be tested, or argued with. *(Figure 4.)*

**The studio.** Not every working environment is arranged in advance. Francis Bacon moved into 7 Reece Mews in London in 1961 and worked there until his death in 1992. When Dublin's Hugh Lane Gallery acquired the studio in 1998, [archaeologists drew survey plans mapping where objects lay, and curators tagged and packed everything, including the dust](https://hughlane.ie/arts_artists/francis-bacons-studio/). The inventory ran past 7,000 items: [roughly 570 books and catalogues, 1,500 photographs, 100 slashed canvases, 1,300 leaves torn from books, and 2,000 artist's materials](https://hughlane.ie/arts_artists/francis-bacons-studio/), along with [the corduroy trousers Bacon had pressed into wet paint for texture](https://www.francis-bacon.com/removal-7-reece-mews). Bacon's studio was not set up and then used. It was a sediment of use — a reference library torn, stepped on, and painted over until it was hard to tell from the work. *(Figure 6.)*

**The working library.** The sociologist Niklas Luhmann built a different sediment: [about 90,000 handwritten cards, kept between 1951 and 1996](https://niklas-luhmann-archiv.de/nachlass/zettelkasten) in beechwood drawers in his study. The first of his two collections alone held [some 23,000 cards in 108 subject sections, two bibliographies, and a keyword index of roughly 1,250 entries](https://www.uni-bielefeld.de/fakultaeten/soziologie/forschung/luhmann-archiv/pdf/jschmidt_niklas-luhmanns-card-index_-sociologica_2018_12-1.pdf). Luhmann described the box as a partner in communication, and the archive's Johannes Schmidt [describes it as a way of producing unexpected knowledge by relating ideas that seem unrelated](https://www.uni-bielefeld.de/fakultaeten/soziologie/forschung/luhmann-archiv/pdf/jschmidt_niklas-luhmanns-card-index_-sociologica_2018_12-1.pdf). What matters here is that Luhmann's apparatus was never finished before the work began. Each card was itself work; the structure grew by being used. *(Figure 5.)*

**The box.** The choreographer Twyla Tharp offers the most modest apparatus of all. In *The Creative Habit* (2003) she describes beginning every dance with an ordinary file box, [writing the project's name on it and filling it with notebooks, clippings, music, videotapes, books, and images](https://tumblr.austinkleon.com/tagged/boxes) as the piece develops. Writing the name, she says, is the moment the work has started. But she adds a warning: ["The box is not a substitute for creating."](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/twyla-tharps-got-creative-habit-49792/) It holds potential. It does not realize it.

## The workbook

Among the objects of preparation, the workbook is peculiar. A blank notebook asks nothing; it waits. A textbook tells; its pages are finished before you open them. A folder of sources holds; it collects what other people made. A workbook is a structure prepared *for* you that addresses you directly. It has headings, blanks, prompts, and an implied order, and it is incomplete without your hand. It carries a theory of what its user will need to do.

The form has a long history. John Locke, who [began keeping commonplace books at Oxford in 1652](https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=3291), worked out a way to index them by each heading's first letter and first vowel; [his method appeared in English in 1706 with a printed double-page index grid](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/john-lockes-method-for-common-place-books-1685). Later in the century the publisher John Bell sold blank books built on Locke's plan, [with printed instructions and an index up front and hundreds of empty leaves behind](https://www.antipodean.com/pages/books/21917/bells-common-place-book-formd-generally-upon-the-principles-recommended-and-practised-by-mr-locke). You could buy an organized mind in kit form. One surviving copy was used instead to press fern specimens.

Harvard's Houghton Library holds a telling example. [George Ripley started a Lockean commonplace book as a twenty-year-old Harvard student in 1822 and kept it for twenty years](https://takenote.library.harvard.edu/38-precepts-put-practice), copying Gibbon, Milton, and others and ending with notes on Brook Farm, the communal experiment he helped found. Ripley set up pages for an alphabetical index — and then hardly filled it in. The library's curators draw a conclusion worth pinning to a studio wall: "Successful note-taking often involves ignoring advice about note-taking." *(Figure 2.)*

Other books were turned into workbooks by their publishers. Some nineteenth-century scholarly titles were [interleaved with blank lined pages specifically for note-taking](https://takenote.library.harvard.edu/64-book-designed-be-written), fusing textbook and notebook into one object. Brecht's model book does something similar at the scale of a whole production. The school workbook does it at the scale of a lesson — sometimes as an invitation to think, sometimes as a set of blanks whose answers are already known. Every workbook is a bet about what its user will do, and the user is always free to lose that bet on purpose.

## What preparation feels like

Running through all of these cases are feelings that productivity advice tends to flatten. There is anticipation, which Benjamin named. There is belonging: the one-dollar Coop membership, the shared tape lines on a rehearsal floor, the binder everyone else has. There is self-invention: *Young Artists*, the syllabus stack, the name written on the side of a box. There is money, which decides who buys the gel pens and who copies the textbook by hand. There is taste, which turns a binder into a statement. And there are institutional requirements — the supply list, the required reading, the registration deadline — that tell you what a member of a community is supposed to carry.

Underneath them is a hope worth taking seriously rather than mocking: that a new arrangement of things might make a new kind of person possible. Sometimes it does. The Childs' pegboard made a certain kind of cooking, and a certain kind of television, easier to do. Luhmann's cards made a certain kind of theory possible.

## Setup and beginning

Sometimes setup becomes the work instead of leading to it. Ripley's empty index is a gentle example. A writer reflecting on Tharp's chapter admitted that [it is very easy to stay preparing and never get to creating](https://kylerw.substack.com/p/opening-a-new-project-box) — the "fixing to" phase, she calls it. Tharp saw the same risk.

But these cases resist a simple rule. Mise en place is not done once; it is rebuilt before every shift, and letting it run down is a sign that service is going badly. Bacon's studio suggests that a highly productive environment can look like no setup at all. Luhmann's box suggests that setup and work can be the same activity. Brecht's model book suggests that one production's finished work can become another's preparation. Perhaps the useful question is not "Am I preparing too much?" but "Can this arrangement still be changed by what I make?" A setup that the work can surprise, rearrange, or overrule seems different from one that must be protected from the work.

That question stays open. It may have one answer for a line cook at seven in the evening, another for a stage manager the week before tech, and another for a student opening a research workbook for the first time.

## Three experiments

**1. Trace your tools.** Following the Childs, lay out the tools and materials you think your project needs on a wall, a table, or a shared screen, and outline or photograph each one in its place. Work for two weeks, returning things to their outlines. Then look. Which outlines are always empty because the tool is always in use? Which are never disturbed? What do you keep reaching for that has no outline at all? *This may reveal the gap between the practice you imagined and the one you actually have — and which acquisitions were about the work, and which were about the person you hoped to become.*

**2. Build a workbook, then audit it like Ripley's.** Design a workbook for your project: headings, prompts, an index, blank spaces, perhaps interleaved pages for sources. Use it for three weeks. Then photograph every page and mark what you filled, what you left blank, and what you wrote in places that were not designed for it. Revise the structure based on what you see rather than what you planned. *This may show which prompts generate thinking and which only look like rigor, and whether your real system has been hiding in the margins.*

**3. Ask an AI to set your station, then argue with it.** Give an AI model a short description of your project and ask it to propose a complete setup: reading list, materials list, workbook template, folder structure. Separately, and without looking at its version, make your own. Put the two side by side and write one sentence for each difference about why you would keep, reject, or change it. Two weeks later, give the model a dated inventory of what is actually in your box and ask for three unexpected connections between items, as Luhmann hoped his cards would provide. Note which suggestions surprise you and which feel generic. *This may reveal the default assumptions built into the model about what a "researcher" or an "artist" needs; your own taste, which tends to show most clearly when you disagree; and the difference between an arrangement that is generated for you and one that is earned through use.*

## Wall label (150 words)

**Before the First Mark**

Before people make things, they gather things. An eighth grader chooses a binder. A first-year student stacks the books from a syllabus. A stage manager tapes a floor plan onto a rehearsal-room floor. A cook lays out her station before evening service. This exhibition looks closely at what people acquire, label, arrange, and keep within reach as they begin intellectual and creative work — and at the workbook, a structure prepared in advance that asks its user to fill it in.

Preparation carries feelings that checklists leave out: anticipation, belonging, money, taste, the demands of institutions, and the hope that a new arrangement might make a new kind of person possible. Sometimes setup opens a way into the work. Sometimes it quietly takes the work's place. As you assemble your own research and production environment, try asking yourself: can this arrangement still be changed by what I make?
