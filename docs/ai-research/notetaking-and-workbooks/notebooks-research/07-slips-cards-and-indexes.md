# Slips, cards, and indexes — who makes the argument?

## The question

What changes when a researcher can separate notes from the order in which they were collected? A bound notebook preserves an itinerary: this reading, that encounter, the next observation. Loose sheets allow another order to be tried. But what exactly becomes movable—a quotation, a bibliographic description, an administrative instruction, a hypothesis—and who performs the separation?

This report argues that movable notes redistribute the work of authorship. They make comparison easier by allowing selected pieces to be brought together, but selection, citation, classification, copying, checking, and exposition remain consequential intellectual acts. The relation between a pile and an argument is neither automatic nor innocent. A persuasive history therefore follows the operations and their workers, rather than attributing a finished book to its author's cabinet.

Four cases offer distinct answers: Langlois and Seignobos's historical-method manual, the Webbs' social investigations, Otlet's collective documentation project, and Luhmann's working collection. These are comparisons, not a demonstrated sequence of influence. The existing [week 5 Luhmann essay](../luhmann-zettelkasten/junior-partner.md) is a starting point; here Luhmann is placed beside other purposes for movable paper, including a book that apparently never became a manuscript.

## 1. Langlois and Seignobos: making evidence portable

The 1898 *Introduction aux études historiques* prescribed separate, source-referenced slips. The English translation, pp. 101–105, permits regrouping, duplication, and reference slips, acknowledging repeated citation work and possible losses. These are prescriptions, not observations of every historian. [Primary manual, English text of the 1904 printing](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/29637/29637-h/29637-h.htm).

The interesting bargain is between flexibility and responsibility. If an excerpt can leave its original neighborhood, its authority must travel with it. A source reference is consequently part of the movable unit, rather than decoration added at publication. A quotation without a locator may be easy to reshuffle but impossible to check. The repeated work that feels inefficient protects a much larger investment: the ability to discover that an attractive argument has used a text incorrectly.

This also changes what we should exhibit. A polished quotation card conveys an idea; the same card with its full provenance exposes a research operation. A visitor can ask what was selected, what surrounding words disappeared, and whether the new grouping respects the original context. Showing a marginal citation, duplicate, or correction may teach more about scholarship than a photograph of an imposing cabinet.

The manual considers specialization between workers establishing materials and those synthesizing them, pp. 114–120. That hierarchy deserves scrutiny. [Same primary manual](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/29637/29637-h/29637-h.htm).

Calling one task preparatory can obscure how its judgments constrain every subsequent conclusion. Someone who chooses the wording to copy, establishes an attribution, or decides that two sources are independent has already shaped the eventual argument. A division of labor may be practically necessary. It also makes credit and the transfer of uncertainties matters of method. The scholar composing the final narrative inherits decisions, not untouched facts.

## 2. The Webbs: sheets that make a hypothesis testable

Sidney and Beatrice Webb's *Methods of Social Study* (1932), chapter IV, describes large quarto sheets suitable for handwriting or typing. The rules distinguish date, place, and source, while conceding that the boundary of a single subject or fact is less definite. They allow hypotheses and general impressions as well as extracted evidence. Their printed sample on p. 97 pairs an interview record with an extract from council proceedings; the interview contains a substantial narrative. “One fact” was not simply one tiny sentence. [Primary book and digitized copy, Wellcome Collection, work mh7vd3kk](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/mh7vd3kk).

That concession matters. An indivisible fact is not something the researcher discovers merely by making the paper small enough. A fact useful for one inquiry may contain several distinctions needed in another. Splitting a testimony into sentence-sized cards might preserve sortable propositions while destroying the relation between an allegation, its speaker, and the situation in which it was made. The historical prescription can be tested as a question about analytical scale rather than enforced as a universal template.

The Webbs retrospectively describe re-sorting the material used for *History of Trade Unionism* (1894) by subjects and competing hypotheses before further verification and *Industrial Democracy* (1897). Their Poor Law example similarly makes arrangement a stage between collection and explanation. It includes assistants doing the documentary analysis. These claims are evidence of how the authors explained their work, not independent proof that their theories were correct. [*Methods*, pp. 94–96](https://archive.org/download/b31357891/b31357891.pdf).

An especially revealing feature is that a published book could leave a theoretical question unresolved. Finishing a chronology did not terminate the collection's useful life. Returning with another question made a different arrangement relevant. Conversely, successful sorting did not remove the need to investigate again. The movement from notes to writing could loop back through verification.

Gender enters this history through actual work and acknowledgment. Ann Oakley's specialist study identifies Mildred Bulkley as the Webbs' research secretary and documents how women researchers could disappear from disciplinary memory under the designation of assistance. Her separate example of Bessie Hutchins shows transcription, cataloguing, analysis, and commentary used by a higher-status academic with little acknowledgment. We should not infer that every assistant handled the Webbs' sheets identically. [Oakley, accepted manuscript, pp. 6 and 9–10](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10082622/1/Oakley_AAM_sociologymethodsfinal.pdf).

The practical implication is to follow hands as well as authors' names. A sheet may bear the labor of an interviewer, copyist, classifier, and checker before it supports prose signed by someone else. Distinguishing these contributions does not require inventing a simple opposition between mechanical women and intellectual men. The evidence instead invites us to question where that distinction was made and whose analytical work it concealed.

## 3. Otlet and the Mundaneum: the collection is an output

The Universal Bibliographic Repertory, developed from 1895, indexed publications through standardized movable cards and subject classification. The Mundaneum's UNESCO nomination describes separate subject and author files, additional catalogues, and an administrative file designated **K**, recording staff and completed work. It also identifies *Bibliographia universalis* as a published form of the repertory. These catalogue records must be distinguished from cards excerpting a publication's intellectual contents. [Institutional nomination, sections 3.1–3.4 and 4.4](https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/belgium_universal_bibliographic_repertory.pdf).

This case prevents a personal cabinet from becoming the default model. For a public repertory, the record that leads someone to a publication is already a research product. Its value cannot be measured only by whether one celebrated person later writes a book. Consistency among contributors, reliable description, and arrangements usable by unfamiliar readers become central problems.

Otlet's 1934 *Traité de documentation*, section **251.32**, treats notes as bibliographic records, excerpts, criticism, hypotheses, and prospective writing. It allows an element to occupy several cards and envisages both pasting notes into manuscript sheets and rewriting them. Crucially, it cautions against forcing every collected note into the work, against excessive accumulation, and against treating fragments as automatically compatible. The composition retains its own demands. [Primary text, section 251.32, printed pp. 256–257](https://traite.czam.de/en/latest/otlet_traite_1934_FR.html).

This is more useful for an essay workshop than the familiar claim that Otlet invented the internet on paper. His instructions contain a tension that remains current: source collection should be reusable, yet a particular work needs its own limits and proportions. The existence of a note can make it feel obligatory. An author has to decide whether using it improves the argument or merely displays accumulated effort.

Specialist Charles van den Heuvel examines Otlet's *Laboratorium Mundaneum* drawing: documents enter a processing apparatus, and elements emerge into organized transport. He supplies an archival identifier, **Mundaneum Document 8694**, and reproduces the image as figure 7.2. This is a visualization of knowledge transformation, not a photograph proving the routine of the card office. [Scholarly chapter, pp. 132–133](https://pure.knaw.nl/portal/files/2075825442/Building_Society_Constructing_Knowledge.pdf).

The factory metaphor invites a revealing counter-question: where are the workers' judgments? Deciding what counts as residue, repetition, or essential knowledge cannot be left to the metaphorical machinery. For an exhibition, the picture could be paired with one card and a record of its maker, letting visitors compare a diagram's clean passage with the actual mess of reading and revision.

The Mundaneum's own history restores Léonie La Fontaine to the institution's story and records her creation of the Central Office for Documentation on Women in 1909. This identifies another actor and purpose; it does not establish a gender breakdown of all card-office employees. [Institutional history](https://mundaneum.org/en/the-mundaneum/history/).

An apparently universal knowledge arrangement should therefore be investigated through its particular participants. Which associations supplied records? Who could pay, travel, translate, and maintain files? What did women choose to document for other women? These questions make the collective project historically specific without reducing it to either a heroic anticipation of computing or an uncomplicated bureaucracy.

## 4. Luhmann: connections do not guarantee a book

Johannes F. K. Schmidt describes Luhmann rewriting preliminary reading records, integrating notes locally, maintaining references, and using a selective keyword index. This is a distinctive arrangement: stable positions permit connections without continual wholesale reshuffling. Schmidt also reports isolated portions apparently left untouched. Most tellingly, his footnote 9 identifies a table of contents for *Grundriss einer funktionalen Verwaltungslehre*, whose subjects correspond to early sections of the second collection, but reports no further evidence that Luhmann actually attempted the book. [Schmidt, 2018, pp. 55–59, especially p. 55 n. 9](https://sociologica.unibo.it/article/download/8350/8272/26621).

An unrealized outline extends the existing week 5 account beyond its cluster of reflections on the box itself. A collection can preserve a project's architecture after the proposed output has ceased to organize the author's work. That is neither an automatic failure nor proof that the notes secretly produced a different book. The next research task would be to trace subsequent uses. Until then, the gap is part of the evidence.

Luhmann's own 1981 account explicitly says that publications do not emerge by copying the collection. He discusses material that becomes unusable and locates productive work in evaluation and more general relations. Read the German original, especially p. 227, beside any modern promise that linked cards practically write books. [Primary essay, pp. 222–228](https://ckrybus.com/static/papers/luhmann1981.pdf).

The resulting distinction is between a possibility and an accomplishment. An address makes retrieval possible. A cross-reference records a proposed relation. Neither establishes that the author returned, understood the relation in a particular way, adopted it in a draft, or retained it through revision. Counting links cannot substitute for reconstructing those acts.

Nor should a surviving unused note be described as deliberately discarded. Physical retention, intellectual abandonment, accidental neglect, and active rejection are different states. A history of notes becoming books needs evidence of what was omitted, but must resist assigning motives merely from absence in the final text.

## An evidenced chain: Poor Law records become an argument

The strongest chain in this small survey combines a contemporary publication with the authors' later account:

| Stage | Evidence | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Source | *English Poor Law Policy* identifies official documents and correspondence. | This report has not checked the complete source corpus. |
| Note | Its preface describes prescriptions copied to separate sheets, with provenance, sometimes duplicated across categories. | No original working sheet is matched here to a passage. |
| Arrangement | Subject grouping, then chronological ordering; *Methods*, pp. 95–96, describes this as exposing changing policy. | These are authorial accounts of the process. |
| Intermediate output | The preface dates circulation of a report to July 1907. | The circulated report has not been compared with the book. |
| Published output | The 1910 book separates historical analysis and explicit comparison of policy principles. | A paragraph-level genetic reconstruction remains undone. |

The preface names Mary Longman's extraction and analytical help, Mrs. F. H. Spencer's archival investigations, and Bulkley's checking, proofs, index, and suggestions. [Primary preface, pp. vii–ix](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43472/43472-h/43472-h.htm). The chain has named labor and identifiable stages; it still lacks the surviving sheets and drafts needed to observe particular revisions.

What moved between stages was more than text. A document's prescription became a comparable item; comparison supported a claim about change; exposition gave that claim a form readers could follow and challenge. The argument needed a choice of temporal scale, a distinction among policy categories, and a reason why the comparison mattered. Those operations should remain visible when demonstrating any contemporary notes-to-essay workflow.

## Comparison

| Case | Movable unit | Main organizing operation | Output pursued | Question to keep open |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Langlois/Seignobos | Source-referenced excerpt | Regrouping, duplication, reference slips | Critically supported history | Who established the evidence? |
| Webbs | Event, category, or analytic statement on a sheet | Sorting against hypotheses | Institutional explanation and policy argument | What happened between sheets and draft? |
| Otlet/Mundaneum | Bibliographic record; separately, intellectual element | Standardization and classification | Shared retrieval and documentary synthesis | Whose categories count as universal? |
| Luhmann | Addressed reflection within a sequence | Local insertion and references | Theory; also unrealized projects | Which possible connections were actually used? |

The common operation is making relationships revisable. Its success depends on preserving enough context to know what is being related. An essay is a provisional public commitment: a selection of claims, reasons, qualifications, and evidence. A note collection can support that commitment while retaining things the essay leaves unresolved. The two artifacts need different kinds of order.

## Two feasible week 5 experiments

### 1. Re-sort a claim, preserve its ancestry

Use a common pack of eight short, public documents about one institution or event. In pairs, make twelve loose sheets, each with recorder, date, source, exact locator, quotation/paraphrase status, and a provisional category. Keep one paragraph of original context available for every excerpt. These numbers are classroom constraints, not historical prescriptions.

Arrange by chronology and write a 150-word explanation. Rearrange by a competing question—purpose, participant, or rule—and write again. Record which claim changed, which excerpt became misleading when detached, and which missing evidence now needs collecting. Keep the unused sheets beside the finished text. Evaluate the revision and its justification, not the number of notes.

### 2. Divide the labor, then audit the credit

Give one group collection and extraction, a second arrangement, and a third a 300-word essay. Each group receives the preceding group's materials and uncertainty log; let questions travel back. Record each person's work. Ask the final authors to produce both a citation apparatus and a contribution statement before seeing the full labor log.

Compare the two records. Did checking alter a claim? Did a classification choice constrain the writer? Did the acknowledgments credit interpretation as well as typing? Permit an AI pass proposing an alternative arrangement only if every suggestion cites sheet identifiers and remains labeled as a proposal. The exercise reconstructs a possible division of labor; it does not reenact any one historical office.

## Open questions

- Where do the original sheets for the Webbs' Poor Law investigation survive, and can a named assistant's hand be linked to one cited paragraph? The Passfield papers are a promising collection-level lead; no item-level shelfmark was verified here.
- What proportion of unused notes was deliberately rejected, and what proportion simply became hard to retrieve? A complete answer needs dated drafts, correspondence, and physical inspection.
- Does a reference preserve the reason for a connection, or only its destination? Test this on actual cards rather than assuming adjacency or similar language proves influence.
- How did paper cost, copying equipment, storage space, travel, disability, and access to paid help constrain each system? The manuals recommend arrangements; they do not furnish a full social history of who could sustain them.
