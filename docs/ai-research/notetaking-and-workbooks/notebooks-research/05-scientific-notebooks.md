# Scientific notebooks: how working records become public reasons

**Central question:** What must change when a record made to conduct research becomes a record offered to persuade other people?

The change is larger than cleaning up handwriting. An observation must acquire a location, a procedure, a comparison, and a reason for appearing beside another observation. Someone must decide whether a failure is a limitation, a control, an irrelevant attempt, or evidence for a different explanation. Someone must also decide whose work travels into the finished account. A notebook can help make these decisions without recording the decisions themselves.

The four cases below treat scientific notes as parts of working arrangements: Hooke's meeting records, Faraday's experimental diary, Darwin's loose worm-research papers, and the Curies' shared laboratory writing. They are different kinds of objects. Only some are bound notebooks; a scientific paper called a *note* is already a public intervention. Keeping these differences visible prevents an attractive but misleading story in which one universal notebook technique produces discoveries across three centuries.

The existing [Notebook B essay](../darwin-notebooks/i-think-notebook-b.md) follows Darwin's speculative drawings. This report extends that investigation into his later experimental and collaborative practice. Its argument is that **notes become persuasive through accountable selection and rearrangement, rather than through completeness alone**. The public account should be clearer than the working record, but its clarity need not conceal failed trials, uncertainty, or other people's labor.

**Evidence convention:** “The record shows” identifies surviving text or an inspected image. Institutional captions and scholarly interpretations are identified as such. Comparisons between separate documents are this report's interpretations unless an editor supplies the connection. No case establishes an uninterrupted transcript of thought.

## 1. Hooke: a notebook can make an institution, and contest its memory

On 25 October 1677, after the Royal Society's recess, Robert Hooke recorded a meeting that moved between an instrument for comparing liquids, proposed trials with different waters, waterproof leather, and an onion producing small onions. His page is densely corrected: additions compete with deletions, and procedural details coexist with discussion and requests for future work. Dr Whistler helps explain the instrument; Packer raises an objection about the leather. The record includes the Society asking Hooke to take an account of consequential matters after its secretary Henry Oldenburg's death. This is a research community assigning the labor of its own memory. [*Hooke Folio*, CELL/RS/HF_105, manuscript p. 103, transcription and image](https://www.livesandletters.ac.uk/cell/Hooke/hooke_index.php?ref=105&option=both).

The rough page does more than capture results. It records what the group considers worth trying next. Its different voices make an experiment a social event: equipment is demonstrated, its sensitivity questioned, further comparisons requested. Reading it as Hooke's private sequence of insights would erase the meeting that generated it. Conversely, an orderly list of successful experiments would erase its agenda for unfinished work.

The folio also contains a second kind of writing: retrospective extracts from earlier Society records. Hooke assembled evidence for claims to priority, reading older minutes against his sense of what Oldenburg had failed to preserve. Historians Robyn Adams and Lisa Jardine show that the recovered rough records sometimes contain material absent from the official fair copies. They also document Hooke's own failures as secretary: missing material, delayed copying, and blanks left unfilled. Their comparison supports a double caution. Institutional transcription can lose information; the person protesting that loss can also help produce it. [Adams and Jardine, “The return of the Hooke folio,” pp. 235–239](https://www.livesandletters.ac.uk/rs/RSNR20060151p.pdf).

These papers therefore support no simple opposition between honest raw notes and corrupt polished records. Hooke's private compilation is already selected around a grievance. It is evidence that he constructed a case, not proof that every priority claim was justified. The relevant output here is the Society's official record and the possibility of later reputational arguments. This report does **not** make the later folio into the source of *Micrographia* (1665), or claim a particular printed argument descended from HF_105. The surviving route to an institutional fair copy is consequential enough: copying determines what future authors can cite.

## 2. Faraday: chronological trials and the order of an explanation

Faraday's entry for 29 August 1831 describes an iron ring with separately insulated wire coils, labeled A and B. Connecting the battery to A disturbed a magnetic needle attached to B; breaking the connection disturbed it again. The needle returned to rest while the connection continued. The next day's paragraph 14 varies the connections to test where the change matters. Other entries report attempts that produced no chemical action or no observable spark. Apparatus, adjustment, response, and doubt remain adjacent. [*Faraday's Diary*, vol. I, pp. 367–369, paragraphs 1–19; preview PDF pp. 37–39](https://www.faradaysdiary.com/ws2/faraday.pdf).

This is a particularly useful experimental record because “nothing happened” can have several meanings. The phenomenon might be absent; the instrument might be unsuitable; the procedure might miss a brief effect. Changing a connection is also a way of changing the question. The notebook accommodates this provisional work without requiring that each trial contribute immediately to a finished law.

The published First Series of *Experimental Researches in Electricity* announces a different order. In paragraph 5, Faraday says he will describe the results “not as they were obtained,” aiming for a concise account. The iron ring appears in paragraphs 27–30, following other arrangements. In an April 1832 note appended to the paper, he quotes an unsuccessful notebook experiment from November 1825 amid a dispute about chronology and credit. A failed attempt becomes useful public evidence about an earlier investigation. [*Experimental Researches*, vol. I, First Series, paragraph 5, paragraphs 27–30, and concluding note](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14986/pg14986-images.html).

Rearrangement here is declared, not hidden. The paper asks the reader to understand relations between experiments rather than relive the author's schedule. That is also an essay-writing operation: establish the question, group relevant cases, identify the discriminating difference, and explain why the comparison matters. Chronology remains available in the diary, but the argument has another architecture.

The diary's detail should not be mistaken for a full inventory of work. David Gooding warns that the entries underrepresent the time required to wind coils; a later reconstruction estimated substantial labor by two people. Sophie Forgan situates Faraday's research within the Royal Institution's paid assistance, apparatus, and chemical supplies, including Sergeant Anderson's work. These studies reveal institutional support, but do not identify the maker of the particular ring from the sentence recording that it was made. [Gooding, chapter 6, especially p. 133, note 16; Forgan, chapter 3, p. 54, in *Faraday Rediscovered*](https://commons.princeton.edu/josephhenry/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2024/06/Faraday-revisited-page-99-for-de-la-Rive.pdf).

## 3. Darwin's worms: experiments become an argument through a distributed paper trail

The later Darwin offers a useful correction to the solitary figure behind “I think.” His worm research survives across loose observations, calculations, communications, drafts, and proofs. The edition of DAR 63–65 credits family participation: Emma and Henrietta took dictation; sons made measurements, calculations, and experiments. Its present sequence is editorial, arranged chronologically where possible and otherwise by subject. We must not attribute the modern website's order to Darwin. [Chua and van Wyhe, introduction to *Notes on worms*, CUL-DAR63–65](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/manuscripts/Darwin_C_R_worms_CUL-DAR63-65.html).

One small investigation has a checkable afterlife. DAR 63.7 records chalk mixed into worm castings, then watering and inspection for displaced particles. DAR 63.8–9 records further trials, different slopes, and a reservation about how watering-can drops compare with rain. The first entry's year is uncertain; the edition supplies some dates in brackets. These are useful particulars precisely because they resist a neat discovery date. [Same edition, locators CUL-DAR63.7_001–002 and CUL-DAR63.8-9_001–002](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/manuscripts/Darwin_C_R_worms_CUL-DAR63-65.html).

In *The Formation of Vegetable Mould* (1881), pp. 272–273, chalk, saliva or gum-water, the fine watering rose, and corresponding slope angles and distances appear in an organized discussion of soil washed from castings. The unusual combination supports a connection between the working trials and this passage. The public prose preserves a qualification about simulated rain. Elsewhere in the chapter, Darwin credits George's calculation and warns that more observations would be needed for a general result. [*Vegetable Mould*, chapter VI, pp. 264–267 and 272–273](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1881_Worms_F1357/1881_Worms_F1357.html).

The transformation is partly one of scale. A local trace on a lawn becomes evidence in a larger account of how repeated small actions move land. A measurement alone cannot make that argument: it needs comparisons, assumptions about recurrence, and limits on extrapolation. The prose supplies these relations. Yet matching particulars does not tell us the precise moment Darwin reread the notes, who copied a sentence, or which intermediate version carried it.

This research also crosses domestic and geographic boundaries. Gordon Chancellor's historical introduction relates the early worm inquiry to Josiah Wedgwood's observations and discusses the long interval between the first paper and the later book. Scientific writing emerges from correspondence and available places to investigate, not simply a continuously filling pocket notebook. [Chancellor, *Introduction to Earthworms*](https://darwin-online.org.uk/EditorialIntroductions/Chancellor_Earthworms.html). The family home provides opportunities for research and distributes its tasks; the single author on the book's title page gives only one account of that organization.

## 4. The Curies: shared pages, chemical separations, and reasons numbered for publication

An American Institute of Physics exhibition reproduces an opening from the Curies' 1898 laboratory notebook. Its caption assigns the left page's sublimation procedure to Pierre's hand and the right page's chemical processing to Marie's. The image is small, so those assignments here rely on the institutional caption; I have not independently transcribed the entries or established their exact date. The juxtaposition nevertheless gives an artifact-level starting point for studying coordinated work, rather than imagining two independent notebooks joined only by a discovery announcement. [AIP, *Research Breakthroughs*, notebook photograph and caption](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/curie/resbr2.htm).

The December 1898 paper offers a different kind of page. Pierre Curie, Marie Curie, and Gustave Bémont argue for an unknown element by organizing several reasons: radioactivity, increasing activity through chemical fractionation, and an unfamiliar spectral line examined by Eugène Demarçay. They report that lack of substance stopped further enrichment, and that an atomic-weight difference was within experimental error. The proposed name, radium, accompanies a qualified inference; the paper does not announce the isolation of a pure metal. [*Comptes rendus* 127, pp. 1215–1217, scan](https://www.academie-sciences.fr/pdf/dossiers/Curie/Curie_pdf/CR1898_p1215_1217.pdf); [English translation](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/curie/discover_text.htm).

A manuscript of this paper is also digitized. Its numbered manuscript page 2 already contains the transition from properties of the barium-like substance to an assertion of belief and numbered reasons. The corresponding passage appears on printed p. 1216. This inspected pair documents argument organization before typesetting. It does not establish which laboratory leaf supplied each measurement. [Académie des sciences, manuscript, PDF page 2](https://www.academie-sciences.fr/pdf/dossiers/Curie/Curie_pdf/Curie_1898lm.pdf).

For a writer, the striking operation is a separation between the properties observed and the explanation proposed for them. The sequence “we measured, therefore we know” becomes more demanding: observations must agree with one another and resist alternatives. An inconclusive check can remain in the public paper because it specifies the argument's present reach.

The exhibition also describes subsequent industrial assistance and chemical processing beyond the couple's own work. These later arrangements should not be projected backward onto every 1898 notebook entry. They do, however, challenge the familiar image of discovery as two heroic figures alone in a shed. The notebook photograph, the paper's author list, its named spectroscopist, and later processing arrangements disclose different slices of collaboration. None is a complete labor ledger. [AIP, *Research Breakthroughs*, discussion of isolation and industrial assistance](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/curie/resbr2.htm).

## A checkable chain: Faraday's ring, with the missing link left visible

This walkthrough is a comparison of documents, not a reconstructed account of an unrecorded morning.

1. **Source/event:** Battery connections, insulated coils, ring, and responding needle constitute the experimental situation. We know it through Faraday's account, not independent observation of the 1831 trial.
2. **Note:** The dated diary preserves apparatus labels and transient responses. The facsimile facing printed diary p. 367 also shows an A/B diagram. Diagram and labels enable return to a specific arrangement; they do not contain every construction step. [Diary, paragraphs 2–7 and facsimile; PDF pages 36–37](https://www.faradaysdiary.com/ws2/faraday.pdf).
3. **Arrangement:** The public paper's paragraph 5 expressly changes the order of presentation. Its section headings group experiments by the relations they are meant to illuminate.
4. **Draft:** No intervening autograph draft was inspected for this report. Sentence-level copying, revision sequence, and choices among unused results therefore remain unproved.
5. **Output:** Published paragraphs 27–30 describe the corresponding ring arrangement. The recurring dimensions and A/B labels give a strong material match; they do not prove that Faraday copied directly from the diary rather than through another working document. [First Series](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14986/pg14986-images.html).

The research-to-essay lesson is to keep two orders available. A chronological order supports audit and return; an argumentative order supports understanding. A writer can explain why the latter differs without pretending the former was merely an imperfect draft of it. Failures may change their role as evidence when the question changes, so a responsible arrangement should retain a route back to the original question and conditions.

## Comparison

| Case | Working arrangement | Transformation evidenced | What the surviving record leaves open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hooke | Rough meeting notes; retrospective extracts | Copying into institutional records; selection around priority claims | Whether omissions were deliberate; complete oral discussion |
| Faraday | Dated, numbered trials; apparatus diagrams | Explicit reordering into a public explanation | Intermediate draft; full construction labor |
| Darwin | Family-assisted observations, calculations, and loose papers | Matching trial particulars in a thematic book passage | Exact copying sequence; original order versus editorial order |
| Curies | Shared lab writing; manuscript scientific note | Numbered reasons present in manuscript and print | Exact laboratory entries feeding each published claim |

Across these cases, completeness is an insufficient standard for a notebook and coherence is an insufficient standard for an essay. A record can be extensive yet omit crucial labor. A persuasive passage can be elegant yet outrun its observations. The useful relationship is recoverability: the reader should be able to ask where a claim came from, what was changed when it traveled, and which people or conditions disappear when the account is compressed. That standard allows speculation in working notes and forceful arguments in finished writing while preserving the distance between them.

## Two feasible week 5 experiments

### 1. One event, two records, one argument — 45 minutes

In pairs, investigate a harmless repeatable event: rolling a ball down a shallow ramp, measuring cooling water, or comparing paper airplanes. Assign an experimenter and a recorder; switch halfway. Record time, person, apparatus, intervention, result, failed attempt, and uncertainty. Draw the apparatus once. Produce a 250-word argument organized by comparisons rather than time. Attach a five-row claim-to-entry map, including one omitted entry and the reason for omission. Trade records with another pair: can they distinguish an observed failure from an unperformed test? This is a modern exercise inspired by the cases, not a claim to reproduce their experimental methods.

### 2. What the fair copy loses — 35 minutes

Give three groups the same transcription pack: HF_105, Faraday's diary paragraphs 2–19, or Darwin's DAR 63.7–9. One makes a chronology, another makes a topic summary, another writes a 200-word argument. Every group marks observation, reported speech, inference, and proposed next action. Compare what happens to helpers, uncertain dates, negative results, and procedural limits. Optionally have AI summarize the same pack, then check every summary sentence against an exact locator. Revise any sentence that upgrades a proposal into a completed test or an inference into a measurement.

## Open questions and next research

- Can a specific Hooke rough minute be compared line by line with its corresponding Journal Book fair copy, identifying which hand made each omission or addition? This report checked the rough-page example and historians' comparison, not that complete pair.
- Which surviving Faraday autograph drafts mediate the August 1831 diary and First Series? Would revisions distinguish the writing of the paper from the later April 1832 priority note?
- Can the chalk-trial passage be followed through Darwin's chapter VI fair copy and corrected proofs? Darwin Online locates the broader manuscript and proof collections, but those precise intermediary leaves were not established here.
- Where is the full Curie notebook opening reproduced by AIP, and what are its exact folio, date, and sample identifiers? The photographed opening cannot currently carry a detailed laboratory-to-paper chain.
- Whose uncited work supplied instruments, materials, copying, and repeated measurements? These four well-preserved archives make good beginnings, but their named collaborators cannot stand for every worker or for the range of scientific recording traditions.
