# Medieval compilation: when does a collection of notes become a book?

The central question is how a writer makes previously encountered words available for a new occasion. Medieval collections offer several answers: organize authoritative sayings under reusable topics; assemble a dossier for pastoral administration; keep a chronological reservoir of reading and correspondence; or condense a long work into a personally usable text. These operations can produce sermons, historical writing, literary criticism, or another reference collection. The finished output need not be an essay, and a note collection need not be waiting to become something else.

The argument of this report is that **compilation changes the conditions under which a passage can speak**. Selecting a passage gives it boundaries. A heading supplies a new question. An attribution supplies authority, sometimes obscuring the actual intermediary. Rereading puts it into a new argumentative relationship. A collection also transfers labor: later users benefit from someone else's selection and arrangement. Calling all these objects “notebooks” can help us ask comparative questions, but obscures distinctions between a personal working manuscript, a published anthology, a collection copied by several scribes, and a record authorizing transmission.

The comparison here is deliberately bounded. Two Latin Christian cases are set beside two Arabic scholarly cases from Mamluk Egypt and Syria. This is not an account of every medieval Christian or Islamicate tradition. It establishes neither a European origin for Arabic practices nor an Arabic origin for Latin florilegia. Similar operations are evidence for comparison; a claim of influence would require its own evidence.

## 1. Thomas of Ireland: making excerpted authority findable

Completed in 1306, the *Manipulus florum* is a Latin florilegium—an anthology of selected sayings—rather than straightforward evidence of a private pocket notebook. Its modern scholarly edition makes it possible to study the compilation as a system of reusable passages. The project reports approximately 6,000 excerpts and proverbs and more than 200 surviving manuscripts. Those figures establish substantial circulation, not a count of individual readers or proof of identical uses. [Electronic Manipulus Florum Project](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/index.html)

Thomas's preface describes gathering passages in the metaphorical field of Robert of Sorbonne and organizing them alphabetically. Topics have individually lettered quotations; cross-references connect material relevant to more than one subject. This preserves one copy of an excerpt while multiplying its possible uses. He supplies author names and a concluding list of books, yet acknowledges uncertainties about attribution and textual location. His final recommendation is to return to the original sources rather than treating the collection as sufficient in itself. This is primary evidence for the arrangement he wanted readers to use, not independent verification of every claim about his own collecting. [Thomas's preface, translated by Chris L. Nighman, pp. 1–2](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/Preface.pdf)

An important correction to the appealing “medieval sermon search engine” story concerns intention. Chris Nighman's 2005 article argues that Thomas designed the work for university students' self-formation, especially prospective clerics, rather than principally for sermon composition. Its later reception as a preaching aid is a separate question. The article's repository abstract explicitly distinguishes the two. I accessed that abstract, not the article's full argument; the disagreement should remain visible rather than being adjudicated from an abstract. [Nighman, “Commonplaces on Preaching Among Commonplaces for Preaching?”](https://scholars.wlu.ca/hist_faculty/27/)

The editorial apparatus also complicates the apparent source → quotation relationship. The project identifies uncited intermediate collections used by Thomas, including the *Liber exceptionum*, *Flores paradysi*, and *Florilegium angelicum*. It links individual entries to proximate sources; examples include *Abstinencia b* and *Accidia h* with Thomas Aquinas. A reader should distinguish whose authority an excerpt invokes from where the compiler actually obtained it. [Intermediate sources](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/page5.html)

The editor further documents alterations such as omissions, paraphrase, interpolation, and joining passages. Compilation therefore involves editorial decisions, not merely faithful transportation of unchanged units. [Project background](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/page3.html) For present research writing, the useful analogy is a citation notebook that stores both a passage and a reason to reuse it. The historical warning is equally useful: a neat author label does not settle textual provenance. The ease of retrieving an excerpt may encourage a writer to skip the harder work of reconstructing its setting.

## 2. Wulfstan: a dossier for governing and preaching

The collection conventionally called Archbishop Wulfstan's “commonplace book” belongs to an earlier and differently organized setting. Dorothy Bethurum's 1942 study identifies related theological and legal material across several manuscripts and argues that Wulfstan used it in regulating bishops and clergy. Her formulation is cautious about whether he directed its assembly. “Commonplace book” is a modern scholarly characterization here, not proof that one surviving volume records a single individual's successive notebook entries. I accessed the article's published extract only. [Bethurum, “Archbishop Wulfstan's Commonplace Book”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/pmla/article/abs/archbishop-wulfstans-commonplace-book/FAD88784E023B6A6F11DF2F0F0A6ED30)

Cambridge, Corpus Christi College MS 190 makes the dossier's institutional range concrete. Its catalogue includes canon law, penitential material, pastoral letters, liturgical instructions, and texts on Christian warfare. The item is a composite collection whose contents and later additions require differentiation. An inventory of the current codex cannot simply be treated as Wulfstan's original working order. [MS 190 catalogue data, via Biblissima](https://iiif.biblissima.fr/collections/manifest/7cef5aea9592568b35836428ce1c60249a6678f7)

For an evidenced reuse chain, J. E. Cross and Alan Brown compare Abbo of Saint-Germain's Latin preaching with excerpts preserved in this dossier and Wulfstan's vernacular writing. They discuss both copying and corrections attributable to Wulfstan, and argue for a connection to *Sermo Lupi ad Anglos*. Crucially, they leave open whether some stimulus reached him directly through Abbo's sermon or through excerpts. Their study supports transformation across language and occasion without recovering every composing step. [Cross and Brown, “Literary Impetus for Wulfstan's Sermo Lupi,” especially pp. 272–280](https://digital.library.leeds.ac.uk/277/1/LSE1989_pp271-91_CrossBrown_article.pdf)

The dossier invites a different account of authorship from the isolated writer with a blank notebook. The research question becomes who selected, copied, corrected, and redeployed each component. It would be premature to assign unnamed scribes a precise division of labor from the catalogue alone. Nevertheless, investigating their work is necessary: the person who voices the final sermon need not have performed every act that made its sources accessible. Institutional authority and material access become part of the explanation of literary production.

## 3. Al-Ṣafadī: a tadhkira that remained available to other readers

Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Khalīl al-Ṣafadī (1297–1363), a chancery secretary and scholar, kept a *tadhkira* containing texts he read, heard, or composed, alongside drafts. Élise Franssen's object study examines its forty-fourth volume, Princeton University Library **Garrett 3570Y**. The surviving 95-folio manuscript has the *safīna* format: writing runs parallel to its spine. Differentiated inks, textual dividers, and directions for marginal additions show that working notes could receive considerable care. That conclusion rests here on Franssen's descriptions, not my examination of the original manuscript. [Franssen, “aṣ-Ṣafadī's taḏkira and its Holograph,” pp. 217–262, particularly pp. 228, 249–251](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/298584/1/10.1515_9783111037196-007.pdf)

The term must remain historically specific. *Tadhkira* carries the sense of something supporting remembrance, but appears in titles and genres beyond personal notes. It cannot safely be translated as one uniform notebook technology. Nor does *safīna*, a physical format in this instance, automatically identify contents or purpose. Franssen's study explicitly separates defining the term from examining this particular collection. [Franssen 2023, section 3](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/298584/1/10.1515_9783111037196-007.pdf)

The more revealing relation is between chronological accumulation and later purposeful searching. The following chain relies on Franssen's analysis of al-Subkī's testimony and references in al-Ṣafadī's writings; it does **not** establish that Garrett 3570Y supplied each passage in the finished treatise. [Franssen, “al-Ṣafadī: The Scholar as a Reader,” pp. 113–119](https://edizionicafoscari.unive.it/media/pdf/books/978-88-6969-560-5/978-88-6969-560-5-ch-07_YGa8Pw1.pdf)

| Stage | Evidence and limit |
|---|---|
| Encounter → note | The tadhkira accumulated readings, correspondence, and composition over time. Individual source encounters still require identification. |
| Note → retrieval | Al-Subkī reports that al-Ṣafadī searched its volumes for description and simile, marking completed searches. |
| Retrieval → arrangement/output | The testimony concerns *al-Kashf wa-l-tanbīh ʿalā al-waṣf wa-l-tashbīh*, a treatise using literary examples. It attests a procedure, not a complete passage-by-passage genealogy. |
| Further circulation | Colleagues borrowed volumes. A personally maintained collection could also be accessible to a scholarly circle. |

This is especially productive for week 5 because it separates the order of capture from the order of argument. A researcher does not have to anticipate every eventual topic when recording something useful. But accumulation alone does not explain composition. The decisive activity is a second reading conducted for a specific purpose, with a record of where the search has reached. My inference is that such marks turn rereading into accountable work: they document an action on the collection, not only a fact extracted from a source.

## 4. Al-Maqrīzī: summaries, miscellanies, and traces left in source books

The Egyptian historian al-Maqrīzī (d. 1442) provides a complementary case. Frédéric Bauden identifies a surviving notebook in Liège from its handwriting. It contains summaries, excerpts, drafts for insertion into books, and notes about contemporaries. Bauden reports that al-Maqrīzī referred to notebooks as *majāmiʿ*, “miscellanies,” a context-sensitive designation rather than a universal Arabic equivalent of commonplace book. The material includes reused official documents: note production could give an administrative sheet a second intellectual life. [Bauden, “Data Overload and Information Management,” pp. 53, 64–67](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/289006/1/Bauden%202023.pdf)

Another study links traces in source manuscripts to a substantial extracted output. Al-Maqrīzī left consultation notes on copies of Ibn ʿAdī's *al-Kāmil*, a work assessing transmitters of prophetic traditions. His surviving summary, Istanbul **Murat Molla 575**, has a dated colophon at fols. 216a–b recording completion on 17 November 1392. Bauden explicitly warns that discrepancies in catalogue descriptions prevent confident reconstruction of all source volumes; he verified the consultation note in Cairo **Muṣṭalaḥ ḥadīth 94**, while other shelfmarks require checking. The evidence connects source consultation with summary-making, but does not warrant inventing a lost sequence of intermediate drafts. [Bauden, “Maqriziana XVI: al-Maqrīzī as a Reader,” pp. 230–231 and note 2](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/259315/1/Bauden%202022.pdf)

This output is already useful scholarship without becoming an original essay. Condensing a large source makes judgment visible in what remains and what disappears. For a modern reconstruction, one should compare omissions as well as copied words: were qualifications removed, certain people favored, repetitive evidence reduced? Those are questions to investigate, not findings established here. Consultation notes establish contact with a source; the contents of the resulting summary establish something about selection. Neither alone supplies the entire history of thought.

## Authority is also an event, not just a name on an excerpt

Arabic *samāʿ* records require separate treatment. Hamburg's scholarly-archive project describes audition certificates as documents granting participants the right to transmit a text read in a session. They can also record people missing from narrative accounts, including women and enslaved people. This is a socially organized form of textual authority, not the same operation as gathering reusable quotations. The project also investigates how scholars consulted these distributed records for biographical writing. [Reading the Scholarly Archive](https://www.csmc.uni-hamburg.de/uwa-i/cluster-projects/field-e/rfe14.html)

One accessible edition is **BnF Arabe 1130, fol. 214r**, ACP record 1: it concerns al-Nawawī's *al-Adhkār*, Damascus, 676 AH. I accessed the Arabic transcription and metadata; the record describes a copied certificate. It must not be represented as my inspection of an autograph page. [Audition Certificates Platform, record 1](https://www.audition-certificates-platform.org/ac/1)

For comparison, keep three questions distinct: **Who is cited? Where was the wording obtained? Who was authorized to transmit it?** Their answers may overlap, but are not interchangeable. This prevents a loose story in which medieval transmission records become proto-bibliographic citations or every marginal note becomes a commonplace entry. It also opens a research direction beyond “how did a famous author organize his ideas?”: whose participation was recorded, whose copying remains anonymous, and which kinds of authority made a text usable?

## What the comparison lets us ask

| Collection | Main organizing operation | Possible output or continuing use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Manipulus florum* | Topic headings and cross-reference | Reference, formation, preaching reuse | Reception does not settle original intention. |
| Wulfstan dossier | Ecclesiastical and legal materials assembled for use | Sermons and pastoral government | Surviving witnesses are not one untouched personal notebook. |
| Al-Ṣafadī's tadhkira | Accumulation, then task-specific rereading | Literary treatise and shared reservoir | Testimony and a surviving volume document different parts of the process. |
| Al-Maqrīzī's working collections | Excerpting, summarizing, drafting | Condensed sources and historical composition | A consultation mark does not recover every composing stage. |

My comparative proposal is to treat a note collection as both **a set of texts and a history of operations**. Ask what was copied whole; which wording was shortened; how passages were relocated; what a heading made visible; and what evidence shows renewed reading. Then ask which operations the artifact cannot recover. The useful continuity with essay writing is the move from a reservoir to a question-driven selection. The difference is that a sermon, transmission certificate, or reference anthology may aim at purposes that the modern essay does not share.

## Two feasible week 5 experiments

1. **Capture order versus argument order, 45 minutes.** Give pairs six short passages from the same supplied source pack. One person records them in encounter order; the other assigns topic headings and cross-references. Both then write a 300-word argument responding to a question revealed afterward. Preserve the source IDs, every rearrangement, and unused notes. Compare which arrangement helped retrieval and which encouraged premature interpretation. This is a modern experiment inspired by the contrast above, not a reenactment of either historical practice.

2. **Audit an inherited excerpt, 35 minutes.** Give students an excerpt with an author attribution, its immediate intermediary, and the fuller original passage. Ask them to produce an account of what copying changed: boundaries, qualification, wording, and context. Then write a paragraph that can defensibly use it. If AI assists, require a separate list of claims checked against each supplied text. The deliverables are the paragraph and its provenance trail; neither elegant prose nor correct attribution alone counts as completion.

## Open questions and limits

The closest next step is a small genetic study: choose one passage whose source, working version, and finished reuse survive, and compare them without filling gaps with plausible habits. Al-Ṣafadī's completed-search marks are unusually suggestive, but the testimony does not give us an exhaustive sequence of selections. Wulfstan's textual echoes permit more than one route of access. Al-Maqrīzī's consultation evidence shows exactly why catalogue metadata sometimes needs object-level checking.

This selection favors named, learned men with identifiable manuscript corpora. It cannot establish the wider distribution of these practices or the labor of every participant. The record of borrowing complicates the private/public distinction, but says little about unrestricted accessibility. Questions about discarded excerpts remain particularly difficult: absence may reflect selection, loss, rebinding, or modern cataloguing. Nothing here establishes a universal medieval workflow, a direct path to contemporary note software, or a cross-cultural influence chain.
