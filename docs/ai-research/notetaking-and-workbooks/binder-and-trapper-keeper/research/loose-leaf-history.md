# Loose-leaf before the Trapper Keeper

How to read these notes: every claim carries its source, a tag for **primary/contemporary** (made at the time, by the people or documents involved) or **secondary/retrospective** (written later), and a short quote where one was available. Items I could not check against a document are marked **UNVERIFIED**. The strongest finds are at the end of section 4: dated newspaper ads from 1937 to 1984 that price the composition book, the cheap vinyl binder, the Pee-Chee, and the Trapper Keeper on the same page.

## 1. The ring binder and the hole punch: origins

### Soennecken, Bonn, 1886: the patent is for the punch, not the binder

- **Claim:** Friedrich Soennecken of Bonn received German Reich patent no. 40065 for a "Papier-Locher für Sammelmappen, Briefordner u. dergl." (paper punch for collecting files, letter files and the like), dated 14 November 1886. The patent covers the punch mechanism: cutting pins hung loosely in guide heads, not fixed to the pressure lever, with springs to lift them and a box to catch the chads. **Source:** Deutsches Patent- und Markenamt, poster "Papierlocher. Friedrich Soennecken, Bonn 1886, Patentschrift Nr. 40065" (DPMA Postergalerie, 2023), https://www.dpma.de/docs/postergalerieneu/07_papierlocher.pdf . **Type:** secondary, but issued by the patent office and quoting the patent text. **Quote:** "die Schneidstifte (a) und (a') außer Zusammenhang mit dem Druckbügel (b) gebracht und lose in die Führungsköpfe (c) und (c') gehängt" and "Praktisch alle heutigen Papierlocher arbeiten nach diesem patentierten Prinzip."
- **The patent itself:** Google Patents DE40065C, assignee "F. Soenecken in Bonn", title "Papier-Locher für Sammelmappen, Briefordner u. dergl.", https://patents.google.com/patent/DE40065C/de . **Type:** primary. Note: Google's metadata gives a publication date of 1906, which conflicts with the DPMA's 1886. Treat the 1906 as a Google data error unless DEPATISnet says otherwise (not checked).
- **The binder claim:** the widely repeated line "Soennecken invented the ring binder in 1886" (e.g. Wikipedia, "Ring binder," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_binder ) is **UNVERIFIED**. The only Soennecken patent I could find from 1886 is the punch. The punch's title names *Briefordner* (letter files) as an existing thing to punch for, so files with prongs or rings already existed for the punch to serve. I found no separate Soennecken binder patent.

### Leitz, Stuttgart: the Ordner of 1893 and the lever mechanism

- **Claim:** Louis Leitz devised an extraction mechanism for his "Leitz-Registrator" in 1886; around then a "new kind of fold-over mechanism on a board base" arrived from America; Leitz turned that "primitive" bracket mechanism into the lever mechanism (Hebelmechanik) whose arch can be closed and locked, riveted it into a book cover, "and with that the Leitz-Ordner was created in 1893." **Source:** Hans-Joachim de Wall, "Leitz, Louis," *Neue Deutsche Biographie* 14 (1985), https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/sfz50119.html . **Type:** secondary (scholarly reference work). **Quotes:** "Um diese Zeit kam aus Amerika eine neuartige Umlege-Mechanik auf einer Brettunterlage." / "Aus dieser primitiven Bügelmechanik entwickelte L. die heute millionenfach bewährte Hebelmechanik, bei der die Bügel fest geschlossen und arretiert werden können." / "Da er von der Überlegenheit stehender Ordner überzeugt war, nietete er seine Mechanik in einen Bucheinband, und damit war 1893 der Leitz-Ordner geschaffen."
- **The 1896 date:** popular and company accounts give 1896 for the improved lever-arch file with the lever outside the rings and the thumb hole (Griffloch) in the spine (Wikipedia, "Louis Leitz," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Leitz ). The NDB entry does not mention 1896. **UNVERIFIED:** I found no patent or Gebrauchsmuster number for an 1896 Leitz mechanism. Worth noting for the essay: the NDB says the starting point was an *American* mechanism, so the "German invention" story is already transatlantic.

### Tengwall, Helsingborg: the Trio binder, 1889–1890

- **Claim:** Andreas Tengwall, a wholesaler in Helsingborg, Sweden, devised a ring binder in 1889 and patented it in 1890 as the "Trio" binder (named for Tengwall and two partners), with four prongs spaced 21–70–21 mm; the spacing is still the Swedish standard ("triohålning"). Esselte bought the company in 1926 and created the Jopa brand. **Sources:** Nordiska museet object record via DigitaltMuseum, https://digitaltmuseum.org/011023818493 ; Swedish Wikipedia, "Triopärm," https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triopärm . **Type:** secondary. **Patent number UNVERIFIED** (neither source gives it).
- **Contemporary corroboration of a Swedish origin:** a 1907 American accounting article (below, section 2) says "The first practical system of loose leaf bookkeeping was invented in Sweden about twenty-five years ago." Wootton and Kemmerer (2007) add that the Krag Manufacturing Company "began sales of the Tengwall file system in 1897" in the US, citing H. I. Stoeckel, "Marketing the Loose-Leaf Ledger," *Bookbinding and Book Production* 31 (May 1940). **Type:** secondary citing a 1940 trade-journal source; I did not see Stoeckel directly.

### US patents, 1859–1904

- **1859, a precursor:** Henry T. Sisson, Providence, R.I., US Patent 23,506, 5 April 1859: a spring-loaded shaft with hooks inside a barrel that clamps sheet music, pamphlets and papers in a portfolio. https://patents.google.com/patent/US23506A/en . **Type:** primary. It is a clamp, not a ring binder; popular accounts that call it the first ring binder overstate it.
- **1903:** John L. McMillan, Syracuse, N.Y., US Patent 724,599, "Loose-Leaf Binder," filed 2 June 1902, granted 7 April 1903: pivoting rods carrying impaling pins, with a latch. https://patents.google.com/patent/US724599 . **Type:** primary. **Quote:** "a simple and efficient binder which shall serve all the purposes of such occasional use or reference."
- **1904, the ancestor of the American ring binder:** William P. Pitt, Independence, Mo., assignor to the Irving-Pitt Manufacturing Co., Kansas City, US Patent 778,070, "Loose-Leaf Book," filed 1 December 1903, granted 20 December 1904. https://patents.google.com/patent/US778070A/en . **Type:** primary. **Quote:** "my object is to provide means whereby said hooks may be readily opened or closed by simply drawing their free ends apart or pressing them together, so that loose leaves may be quickly attached thereto or removed when desired." The mechanism: two plates carrying half-hooks, held by a curved spring plate; pressing them closes the halves into rings. This is the snap-ring principle of the later school binder.
- **The court's description of the Pitt mechanism:** *Irving-Pitt Mfg. Co. v. Blackwell-Wielandy Book & Stationery Co.*, 238 F. 177 (8th Cir., 4 Dec. 1916), https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/irving-pitt-mfg-co-892898792 . **Type:** primary (court opinion). **Quote:** "When the toggle joint is depressed, the spring-back throws the ends of the hooks ... in contact thus making of them complete rings." The opinion refers to a parallel suit, Irving-Pitt v. Twinlock (S.D.N.Y.), which shows how crowded and litigated the field was by the 1910s.
- **Popular framing:** Emily Matchar, "The Patents Behind Seven Classic Back-to-School Supplies," *Smithsonian*, 8 Aug. 2019, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/patents-behind-seven-classic-back-to-school-supplies-180972832/ , calls the Pitt patent "the first U.S. patent" for the 3-ring binder. **Type:** secondary. "First" is **UNVERIFIED** (McMillan's 1903 patent and many other 1900s "loose-leaf binder" patents exist; Google Patents lists dozens from 1903–1915, e.g. US809557, US841920, US859303, US866845, US867191, US868683, US928342, US988982, US1011346, US1101780). A fair wording: Pitt's 1904 patent is an early, commercially important US patent for a snap-ring loose-leaf mechanism.
- **"The first US patent for a loose leaf system was in 1891"** and "To Chicago belongs the distinction of being the first American City in which loose leaf devices were manufactured and used": both from the 1907 *Journal of Accountancy* article below (contemporary, but unsigned trade promotion). The 1891 patent is **UNVERIFIED** (not identified).
- **"The Chicago Binder and File Company sold the first US binder in 1899":** repeated on stationery-industry blogs (e.g. https://www.sbdpro.com/blog/business-articles/ring-binder-history.htm ). **UNVERIFIED**; I found no period source.
- **Wilson-Jones:** said to have begun in Chicago in 1893 as the Chicago Shipping and Receipt Book Co., came under Ralph B. Wilson in 1899, and merged with the Jones Improved Looseleaf Specialty Co. **Source:** Made in Chicago Museum, https://www.madeinchicagomuseum.com/?p=1611 . **Type:** secondary/retrospective. A 1920 trade directory ad calls it "Wilson-Jones Loose Leaf Company, Largest Manufacturers of Loose Leaf Products in the [world]" (*Stationery & Office Products* 1920, archive.org id `stationeryoffice1920toro`, OCR snippet only; **contemporary, wording partly illegible**).

## 2. The loose-leaf movement in American business, c. 1890–1930

### What the loose-leaf ledger did, in a 1907 voice

**Source:** "Mechanical Help Talks No. 2: Loose Leaf Accounting," *Journal of Accountancy* 4, no. 2 (June 1907), advertising-section pp. XVI–XIX, unsigned. https://egrove.olemiss.edu/jofa/vol4/iss2/17 . **Type:** primary/contemporary (a trade-promotion feature run by the journal's "Department of Mechanical Office Help"). Key quotes:

- On novelty and timing: "Up to a few years ago bound books were exclusively used in accounting and the use of loose leaf books is comparatively new. The loose leaf system has been before the American public about fifteen years. But it is not until the last five years that it has made rapid progress."
- On resistance: "Like all new inventions the manufacturers found it extremely difficult to interest business men and particularly accountants in loose leaf devices. They deserve a great deal of credit for their perseverance in overcoming obstinate prejudices."
- On what it is: "Strictly speaking there is no such thing as a loose leaf system. It is a method of record which substitutes for bound books loose leaves contained within covers of variable capacity held together by mechanical devices, permitting the removal and insertion of leaves at will."
- On control (the ledger locked with a key, unlike the school binder): "A leaf can be removed from the binder only by a person having the key. Thus to any one not possessing a key the book to all intents and purposes is a bound book."
- On why removability mattered, its numbered list: no new bound books every year ("changes his account books from a continuous expense to a continual investment"); no wasted space ("In bound books at least twenty-five per cent. of the space is wasted"); one account per leaf ("it makes no difference whether an account contains one item or a thousand"); "Perpetuality of the ledger" (a revived account's old leaf moves back from the transfer ledger); and clearing out the dead ("The elimination of these dead accounts saves time in taking off a balance").
- On arrangement: accounts filed alphabetically by "the lexicon method," or by salesman's territory so "the firm [can] compare the amount of business done in certain territories."

### Scholarship

- Charles W. Wootton and Carel M. Wolk, "The Evolution and Acceptance of the Loose-Leaf Accounting System, 1885–1935," *Technology and Culture* 41, no. 1 (2000): 80–98, https://muse.jhu.edu/article/33508 . **Type:** secondary (the standard scholarly account). Paywalled; I saw only its opening line as quoted in search results: "No improvement in financial accounting since the introduction of double-entry bookkeeping has been of such importance as the introduction of removable-leaf systems to accounting books." Whose words those are (period source or the authors') is **UNVERIFIED**.
- Charles W. Wootton and Barbara E. Kemmerer, "The Emergence of Mechanical Accounting in the U.S., 1880–1930," *Accounting Historians Journal* 34, no. 1 (June 2007): 91–124, open access, https://egrove.olemiss.edu/aah_journal/vol34/iss1/7 . **Type:** secondary. Useful points, with their period sources:
  - "Two leading companies in the vanguard of the loose-leaf system were Baker-Vawter, which began national sales of a loose-leaf ledger in 1896 [Vawter, 1917, p. 17], and the Krag Manufacturing Company, which began sales of the Tengwall file system in 1897 [Stoeckel, 1940, p. 26]. Both systems were similar to today's three-ring notebooks in that leaves could be added to or removed from the binder." The Vawter source is W. A. Vawter, "Origin and Development of Loose-Leaf," *Office Appliances* (May 1917): 16–19 (a primary-ish company founder's memoir; I did not locate a scan).
  - Bound ledgers "prevented more than one person at a time working on an account, thus greatly slowing the closing process," and loose leaves could go into a typewriter or bookkeeping machine; before 1896 the typewriter had to be rebuilt as a flat-bed "book-typewriter" to write in bound volumes.
  - Quoting Moore (1932): without the "equal development" of loose-leaf and standardized forms, "the business machine industry could not successfully exist."
  - By 1928 the merged firm was advertising "the Baker Vawter-Kalamazoo loose-leaf system."
- Related reading not consulted for this note (bibliographic pointers only): JoAnne Yates, "From Press Book and Pigeonhole to Vertical Filing: Revolution in Storage and Access Systems for Correspondence," *Journal of Business Communication* 19, no. 3 (1982): 5–26, and *Control through Communication: The Rise of System in American Management* (Johns Hopkins, 1989), on vertical files and the business of paper; Markus Krajewski, *Paper Machines: About Cards & Catalogs, 1548–1929* (MIT Press, 2011), on the card index, the loose leaf's cousin. Also Henry Metcalfe's 1885 card system for factory costs, which Wootton and Kemmerer treat as part of the same "loose-leaf (often represented as the 'card system')" movement.

### A literary witness, 1922

- Sinclair Lewis, *Babbitt* (1922), ch. 1, Project Gutenberg https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1156 . **Type:** primary (contemporary fiction). **Quote:** "Most significant of all was his loose-leaf pocket note-book, that modern and efficient note-book which contained the addresses of people whom he had forgotten, prudent memoranda of postal money-orders which had reached their destinations months ago, stamps which had lost their mucilage, clippings of verses by T. Cholmondeley Frink and of the newspaper editorials from which Babbitt got his opinions and his polysyllables, notes to be sure and do things which he did not intend to do, and one curious inscription—D.S.S.D.M.Y.P.D.F." Later, Zenith's boosterism mocks countries that "don't know a loose-leaf ledger from a slip-cover." By 1922 the loose-leaf notebook stands for modern business efficiency, and Lewis can already make fun of it.

## 3. Arrival in schools

### Early evidence: loose-leaf notebooks "for school use" by 1914

- **A research workbook in a school loose-leaf notebook, 1914.** Ellsworth Paine Killip (1890–1968), later head curator of botany at the US National Museum, kept his "Account of a canoe trip through the Adirondacks: my first travelogue" (summer 1914) in a notebook whose label reads: "SCRANTOM, WETMORE & CO.'S Loose Leaf Note Books FOR SCHOOL USE. Style A, for English. Style B, for History Chemistry, Physics. Style C, for Biology. SPECIAL STYLES TO ORDER. MANUFACTURED BY SCRANTOM, WETMORE & CO., ROCHESTER, N.Y." **Source:** Smithsonian Institution Archives via the Smithsonian Transcription Center, project "A Botanist's Travelog 'Camping in the Adirondacks'," page 2, https://transcription.si.edu/project/6648 (page view https://transcription.si.edu/view/6648/EBNnf ; image id SIA-SIA2012-8735). **Type:** primary (the object). The label's subject-specific styles show a Rochester stationer selling loose-leaf notebooks ruled for particular school subjects before 1914. Killip took his A.B. at the University of Rochester in 1911, so this is probably a college notebook (an inference). For an essay about research workbooks, this one is a good fit: a scientist's field journal kept in a school loose-leaf binder. Flag for the images agent.
- **1923:** *The School Arts Magazine* (March 1923) describes a class project in which "the loose-leaf note book covers may be" decorated by students, calling the base "a strong, loose-leaf note book" (archive.org id `sim_school-arts_1923-03_22_7`, OCR snippet only). **Type:** primary; context not read in full.
- **1922:** the *Babbitt* passage above shows the loose-leaf notebook was a familiar adult object by then.

### The 1930s: composition books and loose-leaf side by side

- **Fabian's Quality Drug Store, Bristol, Pa., back-to-school ad, *The Bristol Courier*, Friday evening, 27 August 1937, p. 3.** "120-Page Composition Books, 5c / 240-Page Composition Books, 10c / Flexible Loose-Leaf Books, 10c / 100-Page Loose-Leaf Fillers, 5c." The same page has a second store, Norman's, offering "FILLER SHEETS OF OUR SPECIAL FINE FILLER PAPER" and "LOOSE-LEAF NOTE BOOKS Very heavy quality; black and weave design; your name in gold letters free." https://archive.org/details/bristol-courier-1937-08-27/page/n2 (Norman's ad is on n5). **Type:** primary. By 1937 a small-town American drugstore sold loose-leaf books and filler as ordinary school supplies at the same price as the bound composition book.
- Canadian comparison: *The Edmonton Bulletin*, 28 Aug. 1930 and 28 Sept. 1935, list subject loose-leaf notebooks for students ("Science Loose Leaf Note Book," "Geography Loose Leaf Note Book," "Students' Loose Leaf Note Book 25c," "'Dominion' Loose Leaf Note Books—With filler"), archive.org ids `EDJ_1930082801`, `EDJ_1935092801` (OCR snippets only; **prices unchecked against the page images**).

### Mid-century: the student binder as a standard product

- **The 3-hole spacing, stated as standard in 1951:** A. Siegel (Star Loose Leaf Co., New York), "Loose Leaf Construction, Part Two—Variations in the Production of a 3-Ring Binder," *Bookbinding and Book Production* 53, no. 6 (June 1951), https://archive.org/details/sim_book-and-magazine-production_1951-06_53_6 . **Type:** primary (trade journal, by a manufacturer). **Quotes:** "the common 11" x 8½" 3-ring binder of 1" capacity with 8½" centers (the distance between the centers of the 1st and 3rd holes)." So the outer holes are 8½ in apart and the middle hole sits halfway, which gives the familiar 4¼ in spacing. Also: "Most of the student-type ring binders, irrespective of the number of rings, are made with the exposed rivet construction"; cheap binders could drop the "boosters or triggers which open the rings ... if economy is desired," and "the extremely cheap metal constructions made of formed wire rings ... are used when economy is the only factor to be considered." His baseline cover is "canvas-over-board" (the canvas binder predates vinyl). Ring metal: "Regular (or Standard) metal" for capacities under 1½ in, "Heavy Duty steel" for 1½–2 in.
- **The 3-ring as the default, 1950:** Kenneth H. White (Remington Rand), "New Fields in Loose-Leaf," *Bookbinding and Book Production* 52, no. 3 (Sept. 1950), https://archive.org/details/sim_book-and-magazine-production_1950-09_52_3 . **Type:** primary. **Quote:** "It is undeniable that the standard three ring binder is the most simple and convenient loose-leaf device available for sales work. It opens perfectly flat at any point, revisions are quickly and easily made, simply by snapping open the rings at the exact spot where changes are needed." He names the weakness every student knows, "the hazard of torn punchings so prevalent in the three-ring binder," and the fixes (heavier paper, more rings) that school binders never adopted.
- **The 4¼ in spacing as a formal standard: UNVERIFIED.** I found no ANSI or federal standard document; it appears to be a de facto standard (Wikipedia, "Hole punch," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hole_punch , no primary citation). The Siegel 1951 quote is the best period evidence found.
- **8½ x 11 paper:** commonly traced to the 1921 Committee on the Simplification of Paper Sizes under Herbert Hoover's Commerce Department, while the federal government used 8 x 10½ until about 1980. **Source:** David Berman, https://davidberman.com/why-is-us-letter-paper-8-5-x-11-was-hoover-a-tree-hugger/ . **Type:** secondary. **UNVERIFIED** against a Bureau of Standards document.
- **When the binder displaced the bound composition book as the default: UNVERIFIED as a date.** The evidence here supports a gradual coexistence: subject loose-leaf notebooks for school by 1914, composition books and loose-leaf books at equal prices in 1937, the "student-type ring binder" as a standard manufactured category by 1951, and by 1967–1973 California back-to-school ads where "binder paper" (the West Coast term for filler) and binders lead the list. The Daly City *Post* of 6 Sept. 1967 advertises "BINDER PAPER ... 88¢" and a "Zipper Binder Carryall" (archive.org id `cdc_004556`, OCR snippet only).

### The Pee-Chee

- **First use, May 1943 (primary):** the US trademark PEE-CHEE, serial 71526166, registration 0443755, filed 3 July 1947, registered 7 February 1950, original registrant Western Tablet & Stationery Corporation; first use anywhere and in commerce 13 May 1943; goods "PAPER [PACKETS AND] FOLDERS FOR LOOSE WRITING PAPER [AND LOOSE LEAF BINDERS]" (bracketed goods later deleted); later owners The Mead Corporation, MeadWestvaco, Mead Products LLC, ACCO Brands. https://trademarks.justia.com/715/26/pee-71526166.html . **Type:** primary (USPTO record via Justia). It confirms the popular "1943" date, and the goods description places the Pee-Chee inside the loose-leaf system: a folder for loose paper.
- **Design and the cover art (secondary):** Sarah C. She cites a 1989 *Spokane Chronicle* interview with Golden. The Wikipedia article (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pee-Chee_folder ) also cites Gary Klein, "As Fads Go, This One Is Pee-Chee Year After Year," *Los Angeles Times*, 25 Nov. 1993 (not read). The company's location is given variously as Kalamazoo, Mich. (Wikipedia) and St. Joseph, Mo. (Western Tablet's founding city, per the Big Chief tablet article, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Chief_tablet ); **UNVERIFIED** which plant made the first Pee-Chees.
- **Mead's acquisition of Westab, June 1966 (primary):** "Mead And Westab Merger Effective. DAYTON — George H. Pringle, president of Mead Corp., and Paul V. ## 4. The ordinary cheap binder as a comparison object

### Construction

- **How a cheap vinyl binder is built:** a 1994 patent describes the conventional binder as "a pair of superimposed sheets of thermoplastic material, preferably vinyl, and three rigid, spaced-apart, stiffener panels" of "chipboard (e.g., 110 pt.), cardboard, paperboard or the like" sandwiched between the sheets, which are "heat-sealed to one another around their respective peripheries and on either side of [each] panel," with the "snap-ring assembly ... riveted or otherwise fastened directly to rear cover." Edward Podosek (Avery Dennison), US Patent 5,607,246, "Ring binder," filed 26 Aug. 1994, granted 4 Mar. 1997, https://patents.google.com/patent/US5607246A/en . **Type:** primary as a technical description, though late (1990s) for the 1960s–80s object. The same three-panel, two-hinge, riveted-ring-metal layout runs back to Siegel's 1951 canvas-over-board binder; vinyl replaced the canvas and heat sealing replaced the gluing and turning-in.
- **When vinyl replaced canvas: UNVERIFIED as a date.** The ads below show canvas and vinyl sold side by side in 1973 ("Vinyl or Canvas Binder") and again in 1984 ("Blue Canvas Binder" next to "Poly Binders"). Earlier, a 1944 US patent (US2390125, "Binder case construction for books," https://patents.google.com/patent/US2390125 ) already claims thermoplastic cover panels; not read in detail.
- **D-ring vs. O-ring:** a 1994 patent (Steve Gillum, U.S. Ring Binder, US 5,332,327, "D ring binder," filed 23 Sept. 1991, https://patents.google.com/patent/US5332327 ) explains that with round rings "threading of the pages thereon must be accomplished with a swinging or rotational displacement," whereas a D-ring's straight leg lets pages sit flat and stack higher; it cites D-ring prior art back to US 1,537,502 (1925), US 1,915,140 (1933), US 2,265,822 (1941), and US 2,460,718 (1949). **Type:** primary (patent background); the cited early patents were not inspected. When D-rings reached cheap school binders is **UNVERIFIED**; every cheap binder in the 1973–84 ads below gives ring size only ("½-inch," "1" or 1½" rings"), which suggests ordinary round rings.

What the table shows:

- In 1973 a plain vinyl 3-ring binder cost about 49–79¢ at California drugstores and supermarkets, and a Pee-Chee 9–10¢.
- The pocketed, clip-and-pad "organizer" binder existed before the Trapper Keeper. Westab (Mead's subsidiary since 1966) sold "The Organizer" tri-wing vinyl binder in August 1973, with pockets, a clip inside the back cover, and a pad, at $1.66 against a $2.75 list. The same month Value Giant sold "The Arranger" (six pockets and a clip) at $1.97. These are the Trapper Keeper's immediate ancestors and are flagged for the Trapper Keeper agent.
- In 1979 Ralphs sold the Trapper Keeper and the "Data Center-Organizer" at one price, $2.88, the same week as a 15¢ Pee-Chee and 99¢ filler. In 1982 Lucky charged $3.99. In 1984 a small Carmel Valley shop listed the Trapper Keeper, The Organizer and Flex 3 at $5.88 "your choice," against $1.33 for a poly binder and $2.33 for a canvas one. On the same counter, then, the Trapper Keeper cost roughly 2½–4½ times the plain binder, and in 1984 about 20 Pee-Chees.

- Any patent or utility-model number for the Leitz lever-arch mechanism of 1896. The NDB dates the Leitz-Ordner to 1893 and does not mention 1896.
- Tengwall's 1890 Swedish patent number.
- The 1891 "first US loose-leaf patent" named in the 1907 *Journal of Accountancy* article.
- The "Chicago Binder and File Company, 1899, first US binder for sale" claim.
- That Pitt's 1904 patent was the "first" US three-ring binder patent (it is early and important; "first" is a popular gloss).
- A formal standard document (ANSI, federal spec or Simplified Practice Recommendation) fixing the 4¼ in three-hole spacing; the best evidence is Siegel's 1951 "8½" centers."
- The date when the vinyl binder replaced the canvas binder, and when D-rings reached cheap school binders.
- A date for when loose-leaf binders overtook bound composition books in US schools. The evidence shows decades of overlap, not a switch.
- The Pee-Chee's first plant (Kalamazoo vs. St. Joseph); the 1993 *LA Times* and 1989 *Spokane Chronicle* pieces were not read.
- Sears / JCPenney / Montgomery Ward catalogue binder prices. Not searched once the newspaper ads gave dated prices with the Trapper Keeper on the same page.
