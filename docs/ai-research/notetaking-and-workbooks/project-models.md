# Models for organizing work

How other systems divide work into layers: projects, tasks, and the things in between, such as stations, handouts, short videos, prototypes, and builds.

Marlon's reaction (2026-10-04): "The story/epic/theme one is good, as is the agency/production-co one." Those two come first and get the most room.

## Agile: theme, epic, story, task

Software teams working in Scrum or Kanban, and tools like Jira.

- **Layers.** Theme (or initiative), then epic, then story, then task or subtask. Jira puts *initiative* above epic in its advanced plans; in the Agile literature a *theme* is a group of related stories or a strategic focus, and usage varies between writers.
- **The story** is a slice of work that delivers something someone can see and use, small enough to finish within one sprint. The classic form is "As a [person], I want [something], so that [reason]." Each story carries **acceptance criteria**, and the team shares a **definition of done**.
- **The epic** is a body of work too big for one sprint, broken into stories. It ends when its stories are done.
- **The sprint** (often one or two weeks) is the unit of time. It begins with planning, drawing from the **backlog**, and ends with a **sprint review**, where the finished stories are shown, and a **retrospective** on how the team worked.
- **Story mapping** (Jeff Patton) lays stories out under the steps of a larger activity, so the gaps and the first usable slice show up.
- **For the Lab:** the vocabulary already overlaps with the Lab's own words: the week has a *theme*, Story Lab tells *stories*, and the sprint review resembles the week-ending report. A story's test, something finished you can show, is close to the 09-19 canvas's test for a prototype: "something you'd put in front of the undergrads to try out."

## Agencies and production companies

Advertising and design agencies, film and TV production, theatre.

- **Agency layers.** Client, then account, then job (each with a job number), then deliverables, then tasks. A job starts from a **creative brief** and a **statement of work** that lists the deliverables. Work that drifts beyond it is **scope creep**.
- **The deliverable** is the unit that gets shown, approved, and billed: a poster, a 30-second cut, a set of social posts. One job usually has several, and each passes through rounds of review and approval.
- **Production phases.** Film and TV work runs through **development** (research, rights, scripts), **pre-production** (casting, locations, the shot list, the schedule, the budget, assembling crew and kit), **production** (the shoot), **post-production** (edit, sound, color), and **distribution**. In film, *deliverables* also means the contractual list of items handed to a distributor at the end.
- **Departments** (camera, sound, art, wardrobe) each run their own work inside one production, coordinated by the production office through **call sheets** and the schedule.
- **The bible.** A TV series has a **series bible** (characters, world, rules, tone) that writers work from; a showrunner keeps it current. A theatre production has a **dramaturg's casebook** of research and a stage manager's **prompt book**.
- **For the Lab:** faculty clients map onto clients; a course visit or workshop onto a job; stations, handouts, and videos onto deliverables. Week 5's theme (research and preparation, not yet production) corresponds to development and pre-production.

## GTD (*Getting Things Done*, David Allen)

- **Layers, called horizons of focus.** Purpose and principles, vision (3–5 years), goals (1–2 years), **areas of focus and responsibility**, **projects**, and **next actions**.
- **A project** is any outcome that takes more than one action and can be finished within about a year. Many are small.
- **An area** is ongoing and never finished: a standard to maintain, such as health, a team, a course.
- **Next actions** are the single physical steps, filed by **context** (where or with what tool they can be done).
- **The weekly review** goes through every project and list.

## PARA (Tiago Forte, *Building a Second Brain*)

- Four buckets: **Projects** (an outcome with a deadline), **Areas** (ongoing responsibilities with a standard to keep), **Resources** (topics and material of interest), **Archives** (finished or inactive things from the other three).
- Things move between buckets: a finished project's useful outputs become resources.
- It resembles Marlon's 2026-10-02 point in ll-system-27: mature, reused pieces "should exist in resources," and building one is work until it's approved and added.

## OmniFocus

- **Layers.** Folders, then projects, then **action groups** (nestable to any depth), then actions.
- **Project types:** *sequential* (one available action at a time), *parallel* (all available), and *single-action list* (a holding place for loose tasks that belong to no real project).
- **Tags** (formerly contexts) cut across projects.
- **Review:** each project has its own review interval, and the Review view shows which ones are due.
- **Defer dates** (when something becomes available) are kept separate from **due dates** (when it must be done).

## Work breakdown structure (project management, PMBOK)

- **Layers.** Portfolio, then program, then project, then **deliverables**, then **work packages**, then activities.
- The breakdown is by **deliverables**, nouns rather than verbs: what will exist at the end, not what people will do.
- **The work package** is the smallest unit that gets estimated, assigned, and tracked. A common rule of thumb, the **8/80 rule**, keeps it between 8 and 80 hours of effort.
- A **program** groups related projects that share a goal; a **portfolio** is everything an organization has under way.

## Shape Up (Basecamp)

- Work runs in **six-week cycles** followed by a two-week **cool-down**.
- A **pitch** shapes a project before it's taken on, starting from its **appetite**: how much time it's worth, fixed first, with the scope shaped to fit.
- Inside a project, the team discovers **scopes** (pieces that can be finished independently) while working, rather than planning them up front.
- **Hill charts** show each scope going uphill (still figuring it out) or downhill (executing).

## Asana

- **Layers.** Portfolios, then projects, then tasks, then subtasks.
- **Multi-homing:** one task can belong to several projects at once and show up in each. This is Asana's answer to work that serves more than one project.

## Linear

- **Layers.** Initiatives, then projects, then issues, then sub-issues.
- **Cycles** (usually one or two weeks) run across all projects, so a project's issues are spread over several cycles.
- **Project milestones** mark stages inside a project.

## What the models share (Claude's reading, for Marlon to keep or drop)

- **Ongoing vs finite.** GTD, PARA, and agencies all keep a level for things that never finish (areas, client relationships) apart from projects that end. "Our support of a course" may be ongoing; one semester's workshop for it is finite.
- **The reported unit is a deliverable.** Stories, deliverables, and work packages are all defined by something finished you can show.
- **Size belongs to the layer.** The 8/80 rule and Shape Up's appetite both size the middle layer by time, as Marlon's 1–40 hours does.
- **Many-to-many is handled.** Asana's multi-homing and Jira's issue links exist because one piece of work often serves several projects.
- **Time cuts across.** Sprints, cycles, and the weekly review run across the hierarchy rather than forming a level in it, the way the Lab's week does.
