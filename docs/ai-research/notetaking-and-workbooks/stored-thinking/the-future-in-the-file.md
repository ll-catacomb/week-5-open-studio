# The Future in the File: Six Historical Designs for Collecting Material to Think With

## Information management before “information”

A research collection is never merely a place where material waits. The moment something is copied rather than left in a book, excerpted rather than copied whole, placed under a heading rather than another, given an address, cross-referenced, translated, summarized, or discarded, it has already been interpreted. **Storage is transformation postponed; retrieval is transformation resumed.** The six cases considered here—a Roman miscellany, a medieval florilegium, John Locke’s commonplace system, Minakata Kumagusu’s *London Extracts*, Niklas Luhmann’s slip-box, and Salman Rushdie’s computer files—are useful precisely because they solve this problem differently. They do not form stages in an inevitable ascent from manuscript to database. They are competing answers to a persistent question: how can something encountered now be made available to a thought that does not yet exist?

Ann Blair’s *Too Much to Know: Managing Scholarly Information before the Modern Age* provides the best starting point because it dislodges a familiar assumption: anxiety about having too much to read is not a consequence unique to computers. Blair documents complaints about an overabundance of books from antiquity through the early modern period and studies the practical responses that followed—selection, excerpting, annotation, indexing, compilation, concordances, reference books, slips, cutting and pasting, and the delegation of reading to other people. Her emphasis is not simply on the quantity of information but on the material and social techniques required to make abundance usable. *Too Much to Know*, especially its chapter “Note-Taking as Information Management,” treats notebooks and reference works as technologies of intellectual labor rather than passive receptacles. citeturn18search0turn18search1turn18search11

That argument was already sharply formulated in Blair’s 1992 essay “Humanist Methods in Natural Philosophy: The Commonplace Book.” In the humanist method she describes, a reader selects a passage for its language, argument, or factual content, copies it into a notebook, and groups it beneath a heading for later retrieval and composition. The procedure produces possibilities for comparison, but it also produces blindness: because entries become autonomous units under separate headings, incompatible claims can coexist without confronting one another. Blair’s discussion of Jean Bodin is especially important here. Commonplacing does not simply preserve knowledge; it **changes the scale at which knowledge is encountered**, converting arguments and narratives into reusable units. See Blair, pp. 541–551, especially the definition of the method near p. 541 and the discussion of contradiction and decontextualization around p. 548. citeturn22search1turn22search4turn19view0

Blair’s wider research also prevents us from treating the scholar’s notebook as the product of a solitary mind. Early modern scholars sometimes hired amanuenses to extract material; compilers read on behalf of other readers; printers, indexers, librarians, and booksellers turned private techniques into public infrastructures. Money mattered alongside memory and time. Blair explicitly describes early modern information management as a response to shortages of all three, and she shows scholars outsourcing excerpting when they could afford to do so. A collection that looks intensely personal may therefore sit on top of invisible collective labor. citeturn17search0turn17search8

The six examples below use four questions consistently:

**What enters? What happens to it? How does it become findable again? What new work can it produce?**

A fifth question shadows all four: **what has to disappear for the system to work?**

## Ancient miscellany and medieval florilegium

**Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* / *Attic Nights*, second century CE.** Gellius gives us something rare: an ancient author explicitly describing the relationship between reading notes and a finished work. The original notes themselves have not survived, so this is not an archaeological reconstruction of an ancient notebook. Our evidence is textual: Gellius’s preface describes the earlier *annotationes* from which *Attic Nights* was assembled. That distinction matters. We can reconstruct a practice, but not confidently specify whether a particular surviving codex reproduces the physical look, size, or substrate of Gellius’s working notes. Later medieval manuscripts are witnesses to the book that resulted, not to his lost note collection. citeturn17search2turn17search6

What entered the system was strikingly heterogeneous. Gellius says that whenever he encountered something memorable in a Greek or Latin book—or heard something worth remembering—he jotted down whatever interested him. His inputs therefore crossed media: reading, conversation, teaching, oral report. They also presupposed a formidably literate, bilingual scholarly world in which Greek and Latin books and learned teachers were available to an educated Roman reader. In *Attic Nights*, Preface §§2–3, he says that the notes were initially made without a systematic order; he stored them as an aid to memory, a kind of literary reserve from which a forgotten word or subject could later be recovered when the original book was unavailable. The final work deliberately preserves something of that miscellaneous order. citeturn17search2

The absence of a taxonomy did not mean an absence of selection. In Preface §§11–12 Gellius distinguishes his procedure from compilers who accumulate indiscriminately; he presents himself as choosing a smaller set of things worth knowing or reflecting on. In other words, his strongest information-management operation occurs **before storage**. Much is never admitted. That is a form of discarding that leaves no trace. The surviving collection records what passed the threshold, not what was rejected. Later, to make the miscellany usable by others, he supplied chapter summaries—the equivalent of a lightweight external retrieval layer—so that a reader could identify what each book contained. See *Attic Nights*, Preface §§2–3, 11–12, 25, Loeb vol. I, roughly pp. xxvi–xxxviii. citeturn1search7turn17search2

What does such an arrangement make possible? It preserves the contingencies of encounter. Subjects not already recognized as belonging together can become neighbors simply because Gellius encountered them near one another. The cost is predictable retrieval: a user cannot issue a topical query across the whole corpus in the manner of an alphabetical index. What disappears is much of the original argumentative context. A sentence taken from a Greek historian or philosopher returns as a Gellian item: still attributed, perhaps, but now serving a new local conversation. The new work is *Attic Nights* itself—a miscellany meant both as intellectual recreation and, Gellius says, as a resource for his children. Notes have ceased to be a private prosthesis and become literature. citeturn17search2turn17search6

**The *Florilegium Gallicum*, represented by BnF MS Latin 17903, compiled in twelfth-century France.** Roughly a millennium later, a different kind of excerpt collection makes the transformation of source into reusable fragment even more visible. Silverio Franzoni’s recent reconstruction describes the *Florilegium Gallicum* as a collection of more than 4,700 extracts from roughly eighty Latin texts by about thirty authors, probably compiled in central-to-northern France in the second half of the twelfth century. Four manuscripts preserve a relatively stable form of the collection, while other witnesses show substantial later selection and reworking. Franzoni argues that it was probably not a school textbook in the narrow sense; he proposes understanding it as something like a **“portable library.”** See his dissertation, especially pp. 173–214 on contents, organization, paratexts, compiler, and likely use. citeturn16search3turn23search0turn23search1

“Portable,” however, is relative. BnF Latin 17903, an early-thirteenth-century witness to the compilation, consists of 170 parchment folios measuring approximately 340 × 250 mm; it is a substantial codex, decorated with red and blue initials. Its portability lies not in fitting in a pocket but in replacing the need to consult scores of separate source books. A second important witness, BnF Latin 13582, dates to the last quarter of the twelfth century and contains 192 folios at the more compact dimensions of about 240 × 165 mm. These are expensive material condensations: parchment, scribal time, rubrication, binding, and access to source manuscripts have been converted into one navigable artifact. citeturn26search7turn26search11

What enters is already authored text; what happens to it is more than copying. Franzoni stresses that the compiler selects, organizes, sometimes retouches, and supplies titles or subtitles to excerpts. His dissertation devotes separate discussions to running titles, titles for individual extracts, and the excerpts themselves, pp. 179–200. Those paratexts become the finding apparatus. Unlike Locke’s later alphabetical index, retrieval still depends heavily on moving through the codex and recognizing its sequence and headings. The format facilitates browsing and sampling rather than exhaustive keyword lookup. citeturn25search0turn23search1

The most revealing loss is also the source of the florilegium’s power. An excerpt can circulate because it has been cut loose from the larger argument, narrative, rhetorical situation, or sequence to which it once belonged. Compression creates availability by removing surroundings. At the same time, the *Florilegium Gallicum* is not simply the work of one “collector.” Its anonymous compiler depends on source manuscripts and scribal reproduction; subsequent copyists become editors in practice, generating secondary versions through subtraction and addition. Franzoni traces its later influence into works including Vincent of Beauvais’s thirteenth-century *Speculum maius* and the fourteenth-century *Flores moralium auctoritatum*. The collection therefore produces new work not only when a reader consults it, but when later compilers recompile it. citeturn23search4turn23search7

There is even a concrete textual link to the first example: the *Florilegium Gallicum* excerpted Aulus Gellius, and Franzoni’s sample critical edition specifically treats material from Gellius alongside Martial and Quintilian. **This is documented transmission of text, but not proof that the medieval compiler inherited Gellius’s note-taking method.** The distinction is essential. A medieval compiler used Gellius as a source; a modern observer may notice that both Gellius and the florilegium transform books into excerpts. Only the first claim is genealogical. citeturn23search0turn23search7

## Commonplace index and multilingual working notebook

**John Locke’s commonplace book and his “New Method,” late seventeenth century.** Locke offers a striking solution to the rigidity of a bound notebook. Bodleian MS Locke d.1 is a 189-page commonplace book used between 1679 and 1692. Other surviving Locke papers show the indexing procedure itself in action: MS Locke c.28, ff. 113–114, consists of two sheets on which alphabetical headings are subdivided by vowels. In 1706 the method was published in English as *A New Method of Making Common-Place-Books*, making what might otherwise have remained personal technique into a transmissible protocol. citeturn26search1turn26search9turn26search5

Locke’s problem is a material one. A bound book cannot easily be rearranged. If a reader reserves twenty pages for “Politics” and later needs two, nineteen are wasted; if “Virtue” unexpectedly fills its allocation, the remaining entries must go somewhere else. Locke therefore separates **logical order from physical order**. His two-page index gives each headword an address determined by its first letter and the first vowel that follows it. A heading such as *Epistle*, for example, goes to the E–I portion of the index. On the notebook page the topical head appears prominently in the margin; the index points to the page. When one area fills, the writer can continue on another blank page and establish reciprocal page references so the discontinuous physical locations can be read as a continuous conceptual series. In the 1706 *New Method*, see especially pp. 4–11. citeturn8view0turn8view1turn26search5

The visual economy of Locke’s printed index makes the conceptual move clear: the notebook does not have to **be** alphabetically arranged in order to **behave** as though it can be queried alphabetically.

This method also distinguishes capture from selection. The prefatory advice attributed to Jean Le Clerc warns against excerpting too much: readers should select material of genuine value and record enough bibliographical detail—author, title, edition, page—to return to its source. Locke’s scheme thus performs a revealing compromise. Excerpting detaches a passage from its context; citation repairs the detachment partially by leaving a path back. The source is not carried along, but an address to it is. citeturn8view1turn22search1

What becomes easy is retrieving a topic without deciding in advance how many pages it deserves. What remains difficult is changing the conceptual vocabulary itself. A passage entered under one head will not magically appear under another unless the reader supplies additional indexing or cross-reference. Blair’s warning about commonplaces applies exactly here: headings can produce productive confrontations, but they can also hide contradictions from one another. Locke has solved the spatial problem of the codex more effectively than the epistemological problem of classification. citeturn22search1turn17search9

**Minakata Kumagusu’s *London Extracts* (*Rondon nukigaki* / ロンドン抜書), 1895–1900.** For the nineteenth-century working notebook, Minakata is especially valuable because assimilating his practice too quickly to a European “commonplace book” would erase what is interesting about it. The Japanese term *nukigaki* means excerpt-copying; Minakata had been copying books since childhood, including major Japanese and Sino-Japanese works, long before he entered the British Museum. His London notebooks belong simultaneously to a Japanese history of transcription and encyclopedic learning, to a global scientific career, and to the institutional conditions of an imperial museum. They should be understood through that entanglement rather than treated as an Asian waypoint on a European road to information science. citeturn26search4turn10search0

Between the mid-1890s and 1900, Minakata produced fifty-two thick *London Extracts* notebooks. The Minakata Kumagusu Museum reports 13,346 pages in total. Their tiny handwriting records excerpts from books in English, French, German, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, Greek, and Latin; his reading ranged across archaeology, anthropology, folklore, religion, travel literature, natural history, and other fields. The official archive records that he could spend five to seven hours a day at the British Museum copying. This was information management by bodily endurance: thousands of pages made manually because ownership of the source books was impossible or unnecessary when a great library could be converted, passage by passage, into notebooks one could carry away. citeturn26search0turn26search4turn10search0

The notebooks were not merely stenographic mirrors. Ryūgo Matsui’s work on volumes 16 and 17 shows Minakata inserting Japanese commentary into transcribed European texts and using marks to connect comments with particular locations; Matsui gives examples around *London Extracts* vol. 16, including p. 150. The act of copying was therefore also an encounter among languages and interpretive frames. Material coming out of a British source could acquire a Japanese gloss before reaching the next page. citeturn11search5

Retrieval is less visibly engineered than in Locke. These are bound volumes: copied passages cannot be physically shuffled once written. The stable volume-and-page structure provides an address, and Minakata’s marks provide local connections, but the surviving evidence does not reveal a comprehensive alphabetical index comparable to Locke’s or Luhmann’s. It is reasonable—here explicitly an inference—to see rereading, remembered location, and the accumulation of stable page addresses as more important to his personal retrieval. The difficulty modern scholars have faced in reconstructing the bibliography of the fifty-two volumes is itself revealing: a later research project had to produce a complete bibliography of the books he excerpted in order to make the corpus searchable in ways the notebooks themselves do not permit. citeturn12search3turn11search5

The institutional labor behind this “personal” collection was enormous. Friends and intermediaries helped Minakata gain access to British Museum figures such as Augustus Wollaston Franks and Robert K. Douglas; he in turn assisted with cataloging Japanese books and manuscripts and research on Buddhist objects. The British Museum’s collections made material from Asia and Europe available in one room, though under the political and economic conditions of a nineteenth-century imperial collecting institution. Minakata himself lived cheaply and eventually returned to Japan in financial difficulty. Access, then, substituted institutionally accumulated books for private ownership, while manual copying converted access into possession of another kind. citeturn26search0turn26search8

Recent scholarship by Bernard Lightman and Ruselle Meade argues that the *London Extracts* supported something more ambitious than an archive of curiosities: Minakata was developing a comparative, descriptive account of Japanese society and nature, drawing together Asian sources and contemporary European thought while resisting the assumption that Western scientific traditions should occupy the only authoritative center. Their interpretation places the notebooks within his later writing for *Nature*, *Notes and Queries*, and Japanese audiences. That larger purpose should be described as scholarly reconstruction rather than read mechanically off every notebook page, but it explains why enormous amounts of apparently miscellaneous material could be worth copying: their future use was comparative rather than simply commemorative. citeturn11search2turn12search0

## Slip-box and digital folder

**Niklas Luhmann’s *Zettelkasten*, approximately 1952–1997.** Luhmann’s slip-box is famous enough to have become a contemporary productivity genre, which makes the surviving object especially useful: the historical system is stranger and more materially specific than most modern imitations. The Niklas Luhmann Archive records about 90,000 handwritten DIN A6 slips in two main collections, housed across wooden and reinforced-cardboard drawers. Zettelkasten I contains roughly 23,000 cards; Zettelkasten II about 67,000. To reduce bulk, Luhmann often used thin paper rather than commercial index cards, sometimes cutting slips from already-used A4 sheets. The celebrated “knowledge machine” was literally made in part from scrap. citeturn16search0turn19view1turn20view0

The decisive transformation occurred between reading and filing. Johannes Schmidt’s study shows that Luhmann did not simply transfer quotations into the box. He often made provisional notes while reading and then rewrote them later, asking how an idea might connect to cards already present. Approximately 75,000 cards contain reading-derived notes, theoretical propositions, concepts, and Luhmann’s own arguments. Usefulness was not necessarily decidable at the moment of capture; the point was to create a note capable of acquiring new relations later. Schmidt, pp. 54–55. citeturn19view1turn20view0turn20view1

The retrieval system combines **stable address, local adjacency, cross-reference, and a sparse keyword index**. A card might be numbered `1/1`; a later thought inserted between `1/1` and `1/2` could become `1/1a`; a branch from that might become `1/1a1`. The notation preserves a permanent address while permitting effectively indefinite insertion. Cards also point to other cards that may be physically distant. Luhmann’s indexes serve as entry points rather than exhaustive classifications. Schmidt describes the arrangement as capable of growing “inwardly”: instead of allocating every thought to a final taxonomy, the system asks only for a plausible local connection and a retrievable address. See Schmidt, pp. 55–57; the archive also preserves Luhmann’s own reflections on his procedure in ZK II cards 9/8 and 9/8h. citeturn20view1turn20view2turn20view3turn16search4

That choice makes some things extraordinarily easy and others surprisingly hard. It favors following chains, revisiting old propositions from new points of entry, and producing unexpected adjacency. It discourages the fantasy that the whole field can be represented by one clean hierarchy. It is also intentionally history-bearing: Schmidt notes that discarded hypotheses and intellectual dead ends remained in the box rather than being removed. The system remembered not only Luhmann’s conclusions but portions of his route toward them. His more than 500 publications emerged from this apparatus, but the box is not reducible to an efficiency machine; it is also a record of unfinished and superseded thinking. citeturn16search6turn19view1

There is a history behind card indexing, and Schmidt connects Luhmann’s practice to longer traditions of excerpting and card-based information organization. But the often-repeated claim that the Zettelkasten simply anticipates the hyperlink or database should be treated as **analogy, not documented descent**. Schmidt himself uses database comparison heuristically. Nothing in the archive demonstrates a chain Locke → Luhmann → personal computer. Indeed, the Luhmann Archive warns against an even smaller causal story: although Luhmann began a second box after his year at Harvard, no document establishes that the Harvard stay caused the change. Similarity is cheap; transmission needs evidence. citeturn19view1turn16search2

**Salman Rushdie’s Macintosh files, especially the Performa 5400 environment, 1990s–2000s.** Rushdie’s archive at Emory supplies a particularly revealing contemporary digital “research folder” because the computer environment survives alongside paper. When Emory acquired the collection in 2006, it received roughly 100 linear feet of physical papers and about 40,000 digital files totaling 18 GB, distributed across a Macintosh desktop, three laptops, and an external hard drive. Born-digital materials include drafts, correspondence, research, calendars, virtual sticky notes, and email; much of this material never had a paper equivalent. citeturn17search3turn14search7

Here the transformation is partly delegated to the operating system. Rushdie named files explicitly and placed them in folders; folders were displayed alphabetically, and documents could be duplicated, revised, moved, and saved without recopying the whole text. A novel could exist as multiple draft files rather than as layers of handwriting on one sheet. The digital folder therefore makes rearrangement almost costless compared with a bound notebook. But cheap rearrangement produces a new problem: a file tree can be changed without leaving the visible scar that a cut page, pasted slip, or inserted card would leave. Digital flexibility can remove the history of arrangement even as it preserves multiple versions. Emory’s archivists noted that Rushdie’s unusually systematic filenames and folder structures made processing significantly more tractable. citeturn17search3turn14search2

Most importantly, **Rushdie’s original retrieval system and Emory’s archival retrieval system are not the same thing**. Rushdie navigated filenames, folders, applications, and a Macintosh desktop. Emory later created emulated environments and searchable databases so researchers could experience aspects of the original computer while also searching at item level. Archivists Laura Carroll and Erika Farr and software engineers including Peter Hornsby had to extract data, preserve dependencies, document formats, protect private material, and build access mechanisms. What appears to be one writer’s personal digital folder becomes, once it enters an archive, an institutional software project. citeturn17search7turn17search3

That later transformation generated new work of its own. Rushdie subsequently used the archived material while working on his memoir *Joseph Anton*; Emory reported that searchable access to his earlier records became a resource for reconstructing his past. Yet search creates its own detachment. A full-text result can find a phrase without showing why a file sat in a particular folder, what application rendered it, which neighboring drafts surrounded it, or which version the writer considered authoritative. Emulation attempts to restore precisely some of that lost environmental context. Digital research therefore does not eliminate provenance; it makes provenance easier to overlook. citeturn14search17turn17search7

## Comparison without a ladder of progress

The six systems are better understood as different distributions of labor among **memory, surface, address, and sequence** than as successive technological improvements.

| Concrete example | What enters and how it changes | How it is found again | What it enables; what it makes difficult |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gellius, *Attic Nights*, 2nd c.** | Things read in Greek and Latin or heard orally are selectively jotted, then recomposed while retaining miscellaneous order. | Personal memory plus local sequence; later chapter summaries provide a finding layer. | Juxtaposition and literary miscellany; weak systematic topical retrieval, substantial loss of source context. citeturn17search2turn1search7 |
| ** *Florilegium Gallicum*, BnF Lat. 17903, c. 1200–1225** | Thousands of classical passages are selected, shortened or retouched, titled, and recopied onto parchment. | Codex sequence, running titles, extract titles. | Many source books become one consultable corpus; original arguments and textual surroundings are stripped away. citeturn26search7turn23search1 |
| **Locke, commonplace book / *New Method*, 1679–1706** | Selected passages enter a bound paper notebook under topical heads, with bibliographical provenance. | Two-page consonant/vowel index, marginal heads, reciprocal page references. | Bound pages behave more flexibly without physical rearrangement; headings still constrain what counts as related. citeturn26search1turn8view0 |
| **Minakata, *London Extracts*, 1895–1900** | Rare books are manually transcribed across languages; Japanese comments and symbols enter alongside copied text. | Stable volume/page locations, marks, rereading; no comparable universal index is evident. | A museum’s dispersed books become a transportable personal corpus; retrieving across 13,346 pages remains labor-intensive. citeturn26search4turn11search5 |
| **Luhmann, Zettelkasten, c. 1952–1997** | Reading notes are rewritten into compact propositions and inserted into branching sequences; old and superseded cards generally remain. | Permanent alphanumeric addresses, cross-references, sparse keyword indexes. | Local connection and unexpected recombination flourish; global overview and use by outsiders are difficult. citeturn20view1turn20view2turn20view3 |
| **Rushdie, Macintosh archive, 1990s–2000s** | Drafts, research, correspondence, notes, and email become named digital files, folders, and versions. | Originally folder hierarchy and filenames; later emulation, metadata, and database search. | Moving, duplicating, and searching become cheap; software dependence and loss of arrangement history become new problems. citeturn17search3turn17search7 |

Several transformations recur, but their effects reverse from case to case.

**Copying** may look conservative, but copying always selects. Gellius copies only what catches his attention; the florilegist amputates passages from books; Minakata’s immense transcription changes language, scale, and ownership conditions even when it is verbally faithful. **Excerpting** lowers the cost of future consultation but raises the risk of context loss. **Annotating** returns some of the researcher to copied material: Minakata’s Japanese interventions and Luhmann’s reformulations make clear that a note can be less a miniature source than a record of an encounter with one. citeturn17search2turn25search0turn11search5turn20view1

**Indexing** separates the place where something lives from the route by which it is found. Locke’s notebook is the clearest case: physical sequence need not equal conceptual sequence. Luhmann pushes the separation further because a card has a stable address while participating in several chains of reference. Search in Rushdie’s archived computer appears to complete this separation, but only because archivists have created metadata and indexes that the original system did not possess. A search box can therefore hide more infrastructural labor than a handwritten index precisely because its labor is less visible on the surface. citeturn8view0turn16search4turn17search7

**Rearrangement** also has no simple historical trajectory. Bound volumes resist it, which can be a virtue: encounter order survives. Loose cards permit insertion without rewriting, but their detachability means that address systems become crucial. Digital files can be moved at almost zero marginal cost, but movement may become historically invisible. The supposedly least flexible medium can preserve sequence most stubbornly; the supposedly most flexible can erase it most easily.

The same is true of **discarding**. Gellius filters aggressively at the door. Le Clerc’s advice around Locke’s method tells readers not to extract indiscriminately. Luhmann, by contrast, preserves many intellectual dead ends once they have entered his box. Digital systems tempt users to keep almost everything because marginal storage is cheap, but archival privacy review, file loss, obsolete formats, accidental deletion, and preservation policy produce forms of exclusion elsewhere. There is no technology without forgetting; the only question is **where forgetting happens, who performs it, and whether the act remains visible**. citeturn1search7turn8view1turn16search6turn17search7

This is why the history should not be told as notebook → slip-box → database → AI. Documented relationships exist, but they are local. The *Florilegium Gallicum* really did excerpt Gellius. Humanist commonplacing really did inherit and refashion rhetorical and pedagogical traditions. Card indexing really did develop within longer histories of excerpting and filing. But a resemblance between Luhmann’s links and hyperlinks, between Locke’s index and a database, or between a florilegium and an AI summary is our analogy unless historical evidence connects them. citeturn22search1turn23search0turn19view1

The more interesting lesson is not that earlier systems anticipated ours. It is that they made **different things expensive**. The parchment florilegium makes copying expensive and rereading comparatively cheap. Minakata makes copying laborious in order to make distant sources portable. Luhmann spends time rewriting and linking so that future recombination becomes easy. Digital folders make copying nearly free while shifting expense to naming, version control, software maintenance, privacy, and archival preservation. What counts as an efficient research practice depends on where a culture is willing—or able—to put the labor.

## Wall label, visual candidates, and design provocations

**Wall label — 150 words**

> How do you save something for a future question you cannot yet name? Across six cases—from Aulus Gellius’s second-century notes to Salman Rushdie’s computer files—research appears not as storage but as transformation. Readers copy, excerpt, title, index, number, cross-reference, rearrange, and sometimes refuse to keep. Each operation makes some future action easier while making something else harder. A medieval florilegium compresses many books into one codex but removes passages from their original arguments. John Locke’s index turns a bound notebook into a queryable tool; Minakata Kumagusu’s multilingual London Extracts turn the British Museum into fifty-two volumes of memory. Niklas Luhmann’s slips preserve branching relations rather than a fixed hierarchy. Digital folders multiply search and duplication but depend on software, metadata, archivists, and permissions. These are not steps toward AI. They are competing designs for what deserves to remain, how it should return, and what thinking can happen next.

For a TDM 155AI research pack, three historical design choices are especially worth testing.

**Keep a chronological spine; build a separate route back in.** Rather than sorting every note immediately into folders, let encounters remain in order for a week. Then create a one-page index like Locke’s that points into that sequence. This tests whether preserving the history of discovery produces relations that premature filing would erase. Gellius preserves encounter order almost accidentally; Locke demonstrates how a separate index can compensate for a bound sequence without destroying it. citeturn17search2turn8view0

**Give every durable note an address and make relations explicit.** Borrow from Luhmann without copying his entire apparatus: assign each research-pack item a permanent identifier, then write cross-references between items rather than relying only on folders or tags. The experimental question is whether two or three deliberately made links per item produce better future thinking than dozens of automatically generated similarities. citeturn20view2turn20view3

**Make decontextualization visible.** Every excerpt should contain not only the passage and source page but a small “context field”: what the source was doing around this passage, why you selected it, and what may have been lost by removing it. This turns the central weakness of the florilegium and commonplace book into a design feature. The point is not to prohibit extraction—research depends on it—but to preserve a reversible path between fragment and setting. Locke’s bibliographical discipline offers one historical version of that repair. citeturn23search1turn8view1

The question AI-assisted research introduces but these precedents do not settle is more fundamental: **what happens when selection and transformation occur before the researcher has encountered the source at all?** Gellius, the florilegist, Locke, Minakata, and Luhmann all exercise—or deliberately delegate—a human judgment about what deserves to enter the collection; Rushdie’s files likewise originate in human acts of naming, saving, and arranging. A generative system can now search, rank, summarize, excerpt, cluster, and synthesize material upstream of direct human attention. The unresolved design problem is therefore not merely how to retrieve what has been saved, but how to preserve an inspectable account of **why this material entered the field of thought while other material disappeared before the researcher ever saw it**. Blair’s history teaches that selection has always been consequential; AI makes it newly possible for the decisive selection to become both automated and invisible. citeturn17search0turn18search0
