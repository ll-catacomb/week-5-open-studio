# How we could research notebooks

The ten reports divide the subject by historical setting and use. We can also divide it by **what kind of evidence we examine**. The most promising approach for notes leading to essays is to follow one small piece of writing across several surviving states, then compare that process with other arrangements of notes. This is a proposed research program, not a claim that all ten reports can recover a complete chain.

## Seven methods

| Method | What we would do | What it can reveal | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Close reading of an object | Inspect one opening, its margins, insertions, handwriting, page order, headings, source marks, and blank space | The decisions embodied in a particular artifact | A whole career or the user's unrecorded intentions |
| Notes-to-text study | Match a source, note, arrangement, draft, revision, and published passage | How selection and rewriting change the argument | That a missing intermediate stage never existed |
| Comparison of systems | Give several historical methods the same small modern source pack | How different arrangements affect finding and recombining material | Historical users' actual speed or experience |
| Social history | Examine correspondence, payments, institutional rules, access, assistants, and multiple hands | Who made the supposedly personal research system possible | Individual authorship from a title page alone |
| History of instruction | Compare a manual's rules with actual student or scholarly notes | The distance between prescribed method and practice | That owning a manual means following it |
| Observational or classroom study | Observe capture, rereading, revision, and writing; assess a specified outcome | What participants actually do and what changes under chosen conditions | A universal best method from one short exercise |
| History of preservation | Compare original records with catalogues, editions, digitization, and omissions | How the archive shapes what seems typical or important | The prevalence of lost or erased practices |

Two scholarly approaches are especially useful. Ann Blair describes research that connects education and information management to books, specimens, people, direct experience, and the helpers behind scholarly production. That encourages us to study a notebook as part of a working environment. Her account of reading Bodin also explicitly identifies a commonplace-book explanation as a hypothesis: good notebook history can preserve that distinction instead of turning a plausible reconstruction into a recovered fact. [Blair's research account](https://ablair.hsites.harvard.edu/biocv).

Genetic criticism supplies a particularly close fit for notes becoming essays. Pierre-Marc de Biasi distinguishes planning and documentary research from composition and preparation for publication, while showing that the documents can be functionally interdependent. For us, the practical consequence is to assemble a dossier around a specific passage rather than treating every rough page as the same kind of draft. A reading extract, a chapter plan, and a corrected paragraph do different work. [De Biasi, “What is a Literary Draft?”](https://www.item.ens.fr/articles-en-ligne/?idtexte=what-is-a-literary-draft-towards-a-functional-typology-of-ge&pagename=articles-en-ligne).

Material analysis can change the question itself. Matthew Daniel Eddy studies eighteenth-century Scottish schoolchildren's notebooks through the learned techniques of making pages, arranging writing, and drawing figures. This opens a research route into the notebook's construction and visual organization, rather than only the statements written inside it. [Eddy, “The Nature of Notebooks”](https://doi.org/10.1017/jbr.2017.239).

## A small study we could actually do this week

Choose one short source passage and one later paragraph in a writer's published work. Locate any associated notes and drafts. Make an evidence sheet with six rows:

1. **Source:** exact edition, passage, date, and repository.
2. **Capture:** what the note preserves, paraphrases, or omits.
3. **Arrangement:** where the note sits, its headings, neighboring entries, and references.
4. **Composition:** where the material enters a draft, if that stage survives.
5. **Revision:** changes in wording, sequence, attribution, or interpretation.
6. **Output:** what the published passage asks the borrowed material to prove.

Mark each row **directly inspected**, **reported by an editor/scholar**, **inferred**, or **missing**. These labels describe the state of our research, not the quality of the historical artifact. An inspected page may remain hard to interpret; a reliable scholarly edition may provide knowledge we cannot recover alone.

Then add a seventh row: **alternatives and exclusions**. What nearby note did not make it into this passage? A notebook-to-essay story becomes more interesting when it includes rejected possibilities, rather than selecting only fragments that resemble the eventual conclusion.

For a modern comparison, let two participants use the same six-source packet. One organizes excerpts under fixed topics; the other keeps them in arrival order with a separate index. Both write a short argumentative paragraph with a counterexample. Compare source accuracy, the ease of returning to context, how the counterexample affects the claim, and what each writer says about the process. This is a small studio investigation, not an experiment establishing which system is generally superior.

## Questions to carry into any archive

- Is this a working notebook, a fair copy, a retrospective selection, or an edited publication called a notebook?
- Does the date belong to the original entry, the surviving copy, or an editor's estimate?
- Can a change of ink or hand be securely assigned to a person or time?
- Is a classification meant for recall, retrieval, instruction, argument, display, accountability, or several of these?
- What was erased, discarded, left blank, or never written down?
- Whose speech, knowledge, or labor enters the notes? Whose name appears on the output?
- Does the cataloguing term fit the historical user's terminology?
- Can we demonstrate that this note entered this essay, or have we only found a resemblance?

## Starting scholarship and access record

- [Ann Blair, research biography](https://ablair.hsites.harvard.edu/biocv): opened and read; a concise account of research questions, social context, and hypotheses.
- [Blair, “Note Taking as an Art of Transmission” (2004)](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-8287-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b): repository record located; direct PDF retrieval through the browser failed during this research. A further reading lead, not the basis for claims about its detailed argument here.
- [Richard Yeo, *Notebooks, English Virtuosi, and Early Modern Science* (2014)](https://academic.oup.com/chicago-scholarship-online/book/13803): publisher abstract and contents opened; full book not read. Useful chapters to pursue concern Hartlib's circle, Boyle's loose notes, Locke, and collective notetaking.
- [Pierre-Marc de Biasi, “What is a Literary Draft?”](https://www.item.ens.fr/articles-en-ligne/?idtexte=what-is-a-literary-draft-towards-a-functional-typology-of-ge&pagename=articles-en-ligne): online article opened and read in relevant sections; the most direct methodological lead for source-to-draft study.
- [Matthew Daniel Eddy, “The Nature of Notebooks” (2018), pp. 275–307](https://doi.org/10.1017/jbr.2017.239): full online article accessible; relevant sections on page-making, writing, and drawing read. A source for investigating instruction through surviving objects.

None of these methods requires pretending that every notebook eventually becomes an essay. Some records teach, remember, coordinate, calculate, or sustain a practice. The question is what transformation occurs in a particular case, and what evidence would let us see it.
