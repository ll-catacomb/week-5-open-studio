# Reading under heads

## When does a collection become writing?

A passage copied into a notebook is already the result of decisions: where to begin and end it, whether to preserve its source, and what future occasion might make it useful. A commonplace heading adds another decision. Putting an anecdote under **Gratitude** turns a particular story into a possible example of a general subject. It does not simply save the story; it proposes what the story can do.

This report asks **how early modern and eighteenth-century collections turned reading into materials for writing, and whose work disappears when we tell that story only through famous authors**. Its argument is that commonplacing organized potential uses, but those uses were plural. Rhetorical handbooks prescribed repositories for future composition. Household books preserved devotional practice, literary exchange, property, and family memory. Some compilations became finished literary objects themselves. Calling all of these “the ancestor of the essay” would conceal the differences that make them instructive.

The local [Locke essay](../locke-commonplace-book/places-before-subjects.md) supplies a substantial starting point. Here Locke is a comparison, not the origin of the practice. Four cases carry the investigation: Erasmus’s educational prescriptions; the Southwell–Sibthorpe household volume; Elizabeth Lyttelton and Dorothy Browne’s shared book; and an anonymous Georgian poetry collection. The evidence is strongest for prescriptions, selection, and surviving arrangements. It is weaker for the complete passage from any particular handwritten note to a later independent work.

## Erasmus: collecting for an occasion

Erasmus’s *De copia* belongs to education in expression, not simply to a history of storage. His dedication to John Colet, dated 29 April 1512, presents the work as a contribution to Colet’s school and its combined Christian and literary education. The dedication distinguishes Erasmus’s project from the accumulation of synonyms alone. This is an author’s statement about the book’s intended institutional use, not a roll call of students who demonstrably used its notebook instructions. I consulted it in M. J. Steens’s Dutch translation, rather than the original Latin. [Erasmus, letter 260, DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/eras001corr03_01/eras001corr03_01_0124.php).

Valentina Lepri’s study places Erasmus alongside Rudolph Agricola and Juan Luis Vives in the genre of study-method manuals. Agricola recommends heads such as virtue, vice, life, and death, with relevant sentences and stories assigned to their corresponding pages. Erasmus’s *De ratione studii* calls for prepared places and arrangements for noteworthy material, referring readers to the second book of *De copia*. Lepri stresses selection and placement as intellectual operations, not mere handwriting. Her discussion also cautions against assuming that surviving student books disclose which manual inspired them. [Lepri, “Note-Taking with Method,” especially pp. 19–20 and 25–26, accessible full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375474558_Note-Taking_with_Method_Remarks_on_the_Theories_of_Knowledge_in_Early_Modern_De_ratione_studii_Manuals).

The useful distinction here is between a subject and a task. A head such as **Gratitude** supplies a subject; a forthcoming speech, letter, or other composition supplies the task. A student can therefore read without knowing the exact work that will eventually need the passage. This is my interpretation of the prescribed arrangement: the notebook maintains possibilities until an occasion selects among them.

That arrangement makes an argumentative demand, too. A story filed under a virtue is not necessarily evidence that the virtue caused its outcome. The heading may make a passage memorable and reusable while simplifying the circumstances that originally gave it meaning. A present-day essay writer borrowing this method should preserve enough context to distinguish an illustrative example from a substantiated claim.

There is a productive tension in this training. Prepared heads can direct attention toward expected moral categories, but the writer still has to choose the extract and determine its usefulness. The book cannot make that judgment automatically. Conversely, a carefully chosen passage cannot guarantee an appropriate later use. Collecting and composing remain separate acts, joined by rereading, comparison, and the demands of an audience.

## A short Locke comparison: address is not argument

In Locke’s published method, a head’s first letter and following vowel determine its index class. Pages are allocated when needed, and reciprocal references connect continuations. The writer still chooses a meaningful head, but the address system groups heads by spelling rather than by their conceptual relationship. These instructions can be checked in the English text reproduced in the Clarendon edition, including the original printed pages 4–10. [Locke, *A New Method*, scholarly-edition extract](https://commons.princeton.edu/hum583-f25/wp-content/uploads/sites/461/2025/08/03_Locke_New-Method-of-a-Common-Place-Book_1686.pdf).

This is a distinction between two kinds of order, not a claim that Locke replaced rhetoric with modern science. A thematic head says what an extract concerns; an index says where it can be found. A table of contents, an alphabetical list, or a collection advertised with Locke’s name cannot by itself demonstrate adoption of his particular letter–vowel procedure.

For essay writing, retrieval is necessary but insufficient. Finding five entries headed **Memory** does not determine which is reliable, whether they agree, or how they belong in a paragraph. The argument begins when the writer tests relationships among retrieved materials, including relationships the original heading did not anticipate.

## Southwell and Sibthorpe: the household inside the book

Folger MS V.b.198 resists a solitary-author model. The Folger’s *Habits of Reading* exhibition describes a blank book with differently sized sheets used by Anne Southwell and her second husband, Henry Sibthorpe. Its book inventory, at ff. 64v–65r, contains 110 items arranged by size, accumulated in stages. Some titles appeared after Southwell’s death, so the list cannot simply be attributed to her. A much earlier workman’s payment receipt shares the opening. The exhibition reads this juxtaposition as evidence of multiple uses and users over time. [Folger, *Habits of Reading*, inventory entry, PDF p. 18](https://folgerpedia.folger.edu/mediawiki/media/images_pedia_folgerpedia_mw/b/bf/HabitsofReading1997Website.pdf).

Lauren Working points to Gerard’s *Herball* and Purchas’s travel compilation among the listed books. Her crucial qualification is that ownership does not establish Southwell’s reading of an individual title. She situates household collecting within access to botanical and geographical knowledge and wider networks of trade and colonial expansion. [Working, “All the world and half a dozen lemons”](https://www.folger.edu/blogs/collation/half-a-dozen-lemons/).

The interpretive consequence is that proximity is evidence of coexistence, not necessarily of a compositional chain. A poem and a property list in the same volume do not prove that the poet extracted the list into a poem. They can instead reveal the material resource shared by several activities: available paper, custody of a book, and a household in which texts circulated.

The inventory’s arrangement also asks us to recognize an output other than an essay. A list sorted by physical format can answer a practical question about a library even if it would be a poor outline for a literary argument. The same notebook may contain several working orders because its users have several tasks. An apparent mismatch between adjacent entries need not be a failed system.

This household case changes the question about labor. Before asking what a celebrated writer composed, we should ask who obtained the books, copied the inventory, maintained the volume, and preserved it after a death. The evidence here does not identify everyone involved. It does make a single name on the catalogue an inadequate account of the object’s production.

## Lyttelton and Browne: recovering the second writer

Cambridge University Library MS Add. 8460 offers an especially clear warning about retrospective authorship. Rebecca Bullard’s study identifies an earlier hand as Dorothy Browne’s through comparison with family letters. Dorothy’s sermon and devotional entries occupy one end of the volume; Elizabeth Lyttelton subsequently wrote from the opposite end. Bullard shows how Geoffrey Keynes valued the book chiefly for its connection to Thomas Browne, while his treatment left Dorothy’s extensive writing unrecognized. The identification changes whose intellectual activity the volume documents. [Bullard, *Cambridge University Library, MS Add. 8460*, PDF pp. 1–3](https://www.english.cam.ac.uk/scriptorium/Bullard_CUL_MS_Add_8460.pdf).

Specific entries establish a wider literary world than family service alone. The Reading Experience Database identifies Lyttelton’s transcription of Katherine Philips’s “A Virgin” at f. 35v, and Edward Tenison’s marriage anagrams and couplets at f. 41r. Another record identifies her copy of Thomas Browne’s translation of Lucan at f. 4r, with a printed source locator in *Religio Medici*. These are documented identifications of copied texts; they do not establish a later essay made from them. [UK RED 22912](https://www.open.ac.uk/Arts/reading/UK/record_details.php?id=22912), [22922](https://www.open.ac.uk/Arts/reading/UK/record_details.php?id=22922), [22762](https://www.open.ac.uk/Arts/reading/UK/record_details.php?id=22762).

Writing from opposite ends is materially significant. Turning the volume creates a second beginning without removing the first writer’s material. In my reading, this makes inheritance a better initial question than linear accumulation: what did a later user preserve, occupy, or repurpose? It does not tell us that every entry was read by both women, nor that either intended a reader to proceed through the whole book in its current order.

The marriage verses make the relation between social occasion and copying particularly visible. Their event preceded their preservation in Lyttelton’s hand. She need not have composed them for their inclusion to represent an intellectual and social choice. Keeping writing about oneself can create a record of exchange, recognition, and identity.

For research on women’s writing, the lesson is methodological. Look for scribal hands before assigning an entire notebook to its most familiar name. Distinguish copying another person’s work from being merely a passive instrument of that person. And ask whether the resulting collection served devotion, commemoration, reading pleasure, future composition, or more than one of these. Without an identifiable draft sequence, preservation is itself the demonstrated output.

## An anonymous eighteenth-century compiler: an anthology as the result

Betty Schellenberg distinguishes manuscript verse miscellanies from topical extract books. Her example Folger MS M.a.174, assembled in the 1720s, contains whole poems copied in a consistent hand. Its compiler numbered 192 pages, made a table of contents, and added a *Mist’s Journal* essay of 9 October 1725 in the front endpapers. Its later morocco binding helps her characterize it as a deliberately finished object. She suggests a male compiler from the contents, but that attribution remains a scholarly inference, not an identified owner. [Schellenberg, “The Eighteenth-Century Manuscript Verse Miscellany”](https://www.folger.edu/blogs/collation/manuscript-verse-miscellany/).

This example extends the local essay’s discussion of A.T.W. without treating every literary collection as the same kind of book. A selected sequence of complete poems does something different from extracting a maxim under **Death**. It preserves the work’s extent and places it among other works. The table of contents permits return to that arrangement without proving that the collector used a Lockean index.

My inference is that the collection itself should be considered among the possible products of reading. Its “drafting” can take the form of choosing pieces, ordering them, presenting their boundaries, and selecting a preface that describes the value of poetry. An anthology can argue through arrangement without turning every entry into the compiler’s continuous prose.

That does not make copying and independent composition interchangeable. Their claims to authorship differ. It does mean that a research account which recognizes only the later essay may overlook the work the compiler actually finished. The collection’s material presentation deserves study alongside the provenance of its verbal contents.

## What the comparison changes

These cases suggest a research method rather than a single developmental story. First identify what kind of intervention an entry represents: excerpting, copying a complete work, recording a heard text, composing, or listing possessions. Then identify its arrangement and users. Only afterward ask what later work it enabled. Reversing that order encourages us to treat every old notebook as a rough draft for a modern genre.

For notes becoming essays, the decisive transition is from an available collection to a warranted relationship among sources. The head **Authority**, for example, might unite a sermon extract, a classical example, and a family poem. Their common heading does not make their evidentiary status equal. A writer must explain which is testimony, which is a literary representation, and which is a prescription. The notebook gives access to materials; the essay must state why those materials support its claims.

The household examples also widen what “useful” means. Something can be useful because it maintains a family’s memory or makes a devotional text available to revisit. Requiring it to produce a publication would impose an outcome the object does not demonstrate. The early modern commonplace book is most illuminating when studied as a set of situated decisions about future use, with authorship and labor distributed across reading, copying, keeping, arranging, and preserving.

## Source → note → arrangement → output walkthrough

**Partly documented historical chain:** UK RED 22762 identifies Lucan, *Pharsalia* IV.519–20, through Thomas Browne’s translation printed in *Religio Medici* (1642), sig. F1r, copied in Lyttelton’s hand at CUL Add.8460, f. 4r. The source identification and notebook location are recorded. I have not independently collated both images. The documented output is a preserved translated excerpt in her collection; no downstream essay is established.

**Reconstruction for an essay, not a claim about Lyttelton’s practice:**

1. Open the printed passage and its surrounding argument. Record edition, signature, original author, translator, and the manuscript recipient. Retain the chain of mediation.
2. Transcribe the short passage accurately, separating its words from your commentary. Add the question: “What changes when another family member preserves a translation?”
3. Put the note under **Transmission**, with a cross-reference under **Authorship**. Mark both heads as ours, not historical manuscript headings.
4. Compare it with the marriage verses at f. 41r. One copied text carries an ancient source through a modern translator; another carries writing occasioned by the compiler’s own social life.
5. Draft this limited claim: “Lyttelton’s collection preserves different kinds of relationships to other people’s words.” Follow with the two entries and their source records. Do not infer that she wrote an essay, that the copies were simultaneous, or that the texts were intended to share a head.

The transformation lies in the new question and comparison. The final paragraph must display their evidentiary limits instead of letting the notebook’s unity stand in for an argument.

## Two week 5 experiments

**1. The same passage under two heads — 45 minutes.** Give everyone four short, fully attributed passages about memory or teaching. Each person copies one under two different heads, then writes a 120-word paragraph from each arrangement. Compare what the head made salient, what context disappeared, and whether the paragraph uses the passage as example or evidence. Finish by restoring an omitted qualification. This tests an adaptation of topical commonplacing; it does not reproduce a documented historical classroom.

**2. A shared book with two beginnings — 60 minutes.** In pairs, use opposite ends of one notebook. One person collects three complete short texts; the other records three excerpts with source locators. Exchange the book and build either a one-page anthology or a 250-word argument. Add a colophon naming selection, transcription, arrangement, and writing labor separately. Compare which output preserves the contributors’ intentions and which changes them. The two-ended form is inspired by Add.8460; the collaborative exercise and attribution scheme are contemporary inventions.

## Open questions and access limits

- **Prescription and practice:** Erasmus’s dedication establishes educational intent; Lepri supplies the notebook prescriptions. A claim about routine implementation needs institutional instructions and actual student manuscripts, with dates and hands checked together.
- **Missing composition chains:** Southwell’s literary entries are a promising route to drafts and revisions, but this report does not establish an extract-to-poem genealogy. Such a claim would require Jean Klene’s edition and comparison of particular manuscript passages.
- **Identity and survival:** Who preserved these books, and whose entries have been lost? The surviving household cases cannot represent all women, all households, or ordinary literacy across Europe.
- **Genre:** An index is not diagnostic of Locke; a compilation is not an unprocessed diary; a household receipt may record a payment rather than a culinary recipe. Determine function at entry level.
- **Access:** Bullard’s PDF and the Locke scholarly extract were read as extracted text. The Folger exhibition PDF yielded text, but its requested screenshot failed. Folger’s catalogue and several old Scriptorium pages failed to open. An institutional mirror of Erasmus’s 1963 English translation returned 404; the original *De copia* section was therefore not directly inspected. Lepri’s full chapter was accessed through ResearchGate’s text after the publisher PDF failed. These limitations restrict object-level and edition-level claims.
