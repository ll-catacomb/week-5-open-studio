# Researching notebooks, notetaking, and notes becoming writing

Marlon's request: investigate the different ways we could do deep research on notebooks, notetaking, research, and notes leading to essay writing, especially their history. Ten independent research agents each investigate one angle. This collection extends the existing week 5 theme-inspiration essays rather than replacing them.

## Ten angles

1. Ancient Mediterranean notes: excerpts, memory, self-writing, dictation, and composition.
2. Medieval Christian and Islamicate compilation: florilegia, scholarly notebooks, transmission, and authority.
3. East Asian traditions: Chinese reading notes and Japanese miscellanies, researched in their own terms.
4. Early modern commonplace books: rhetoric, reading, household collections, and writing under headings.
5. Scientific notebooks: observation, speculation, experiments, collaboration, and published arguments.
6. Literary notebooks and the essay: writers' working papers, diaries, fragments, drafts, and revision.
7. Slips, cards, and indexes: nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship, rearrangement, and authorship.
8. Fieldnotes: ethnography, journalism, situated observation, ethics, and the construction of evidence.
9. Learning to take notes: student notebooks, instructional systems, and empirical evidence about learning and writing.
10. Digital research and AI: hypertext, personal archives, citation managers, provenance, synthesis, and responsibility.

## Other routes worth pursuing

These ten reports are a first research program, not an exhaustive history. Other promising routes include material culture and manufacturing; handwriting, shorthand, and dictation; marginalia and reading marks; visual notebooks, sketchbooks, and diagramming; accounting and administrative records; recipes and domestic knowledge; collective and collaborative notebooks; Indigenous and oral knowledge practices (without treating writing as their inevitable destination); feminist and disability histories of intellectual labor; colonial collecting and archival survival; notebook markets and self-fashioning; legal evidence and laboratory accountability; and comparative studies of unfinished or abandoned notes.

The research methods can also vary: object-level close reading, a documented genetic study from notes to drafts, comparison of classification systems, reconstruction of one historically specified technique, social history of labor and access, empirical classroom study, and a small experiment using the same source pack with different note arrangements.
