# The documentary record of Mead's Trapper Keeper

Every claim below carries one of three tags. **CONTEMPORARY** means a document made at the time (a patent, a trademark filing, a dated ad, a period article). **RETROSPECTIVE** means memory or a company account given decades later. **INFERENCE** means a conclusion drawn here, or by a writer, from other evidence. "Unverified" means it was reported to me but I did not see the source myself.

The short version: the founding story (Crutchfield's research, the West Coast rep, the martini lunch, Wichita in August 1978, the 1981 national launch, the Velcro switch "in the third year") rests almost entirely on retrospective testimony. The contemporary record that survives in free archives is thinner but sharper: four Mead patents from 1973–1976, two trademark filings from November 1978 with a claimed first use of October 30, 1978, and a run of dated store ads in Utah newspapers from August 1979 onward. Those ads show the product on sale in Utah (and, per a lead from the loose-leaf research, in California) two years before the "national" launch, show the snap closure through July 1982 and Velcro from August 1982, and show "Designer Trapper Keepers" in 1985, three years before the company's "Designer Series" date. The one contemporary trace of a school saying no is a 1994 Salt Lake Tribune column; the classroom "ban" itself remains retrospective.

## 1. Patents

All four are Mead patents found on Google Patents. None is a patent "on the Trapper Keeper"; Mental Floss (2024) says no such patent "appears to exist," and I found none either (INFERENCE from searches by inventor and assignee).

Sources: https://patents.google.com/patent/US3833308A/en, https://patents.google.com/patent/US3870223A/en, https://patents.google.com/patent/US3950107A/en, https://patents.google.com/patent/US3968546A/en. Note the inventor order on 3,968,546: Google lists Seaborn first, and spells Wyant "John R." on this one.

**US 3,870,223 in plain words.** One die-cut sheet of card folded twice and glued at two flaps makes a two-pocket portfolio. The inside panels are taller than the outside panels, so the outside of the folder also works as two tabbed file folders. Slits in the inside panels give access to the pockets; claim 3 specifies the front slit is "diagonally oriented" and the rear one "transversely oriented," with round cutouts at the slit ends "to resist tearing." A notepad can be held through the transverse slit. The stated aim is a portfolio "simple and economical to fabricate" and "suited to inexpensive mass production." This is the patent Mental Floss calls "the diagonal folder." It is a paper portfolio, not a binder; its link to the Trapper's angled pocket is INFERENCE (Mental Floss says Crutchfield's Trappers "had angled pockets," and Wyant claims the diagonal pocket as his).

**US 3,968,546 in plain words.** A clip molded in one piece of plastic (acetal resin, e.g. Celcon or Delrin) that grips a pad of paper on a clipboard "or a leaf of the popular three-panel type folios," with a slot behind the spring that holds a pen or pencil. The spring's gripping edge sits below the base so it is preloaded when riveted or ultrasonically welded to the board; little feet seat the top of the pad. This is the "notepad with pencil clip." The 1981 ZCMI ad below lists "note pad, pencil and clip" as a Trapper Keeper feature, which fits, but the identity of the ad's clip with this patent is INFERENCE.

**The Seaborn ring patent.** Mental Floss captions an image "the pinch-less plastic binder rings patented by Seaborn" without a number. Two Seaborn patents fit. US 3,833,308 (1974) is a binder fixture in which half of each ring sits on a fixed base plate and the other half on a plate that slides sideways, driven by a cam bar: rings that slide apart instead of snapping, which matches "pinchless" and Mental Floss's description of rings that "slid open to the side instead of snapping open." US 3,950,107 (1976) is a one-piece molded plastic ring with an integral strap and post for fixing it to a spine. Which one the Trapper Keeper used is INFERENCE; my guess is 3,833,308 for the opening mechanism. The ads confirm the feature itself: "pinchless rings" (ZCMI, 1981, CONTEMPORARY).

Other Wyant patents for Mead, later and not Trapper Keeper parts: US 4,779,791 (portfolio, 1988), US 4,934,584 (portfolio with mirrorized cover, 1990), US 4,991,767 (portfolio with photograph-displaying cover, 1991), design patents D308887, D312275, D314977, D320411 (1990–91), US 5,222,825 (round back binder, filed 1992), US 6,053,382 (zipper expansion gusset for a backpack, filed 1999). Source: https://patents.google.com/?inventor=Wyant&assignee=Mead&before=priority:19900101. Seaborn's earlier patents (egg-crack detectors and conveyors for FMC, a binding machine for Westab in 1971) show an engineer who came to Mead with machinery experience (CONTEMPORARY, from the same search).

Sources: https://trademarks.justia.com/731/94/trapper-73194516.html and https://trademarks.justia.com/731/94/trapper-73194431.html (Justia mirrors USPTO TSDR; I did not open TSDR itself).

What this does and does not show. The applications were filed a month after the claimed first use, which is the normal sequence when a company files once goods have shipped. A claimed first-use date of **October 30, 1978** is sworn evidence from 1978 that the product was being sold under the name that autumn. It does not match the retrospective "August 1978" test-market date (Mental Floss, NYT, ACCO's 2014 timeline). The gap could be ordinary (the first-use date in an application is often the first documented interstate shipment, not the first retail sale) or could mean the Wichita date is remembered a few months early. INFERENCE: the trademark record supports "autumn 1978" more strongly than "August 1978." Neither filing names Wichita.

## 3. This American Life, episode 815, Act One

"How I Learned to Shave," episode 815; the episode page was published 2023-11-10 (https://www.thisamericanlife.org/815/how-i-learned-to-shave); Wikipedia cites the act as airing November 17, 2023; the transcript page is dated for a rebroadcast on 2024-11-29 (https://www.thisamericanlife.org/815/transcript). Act title: "Am I My Father's Trapper Keeper Keeper?" Reported by Phia Bennin, with early interviews by Diane Wu.

What Bennin found (all RETROSPECTIVE testimony unless noted):

- The act opens with a 1980s commercial (CONTEMPORARY artifact, undated in the broadcast): two teenagers collide in a library, papers fall; the girl says "It's my Trapper from Mead," "I've got a Trapper folder for each subject," and "This flap even has a Velcro closure to keep everything inside." The Velcro line dates this spot to 1982 or later (INFERENCE from the ads in section 5).
- Ira Glass: "According to a press release from the time, half of all middle school and high school students had a Trapper Keeper in 1989. I don't know if I believe that." A contemporary Mead press release, cited secondhand; I have not seen it.
- The tip came from Jackie Harris, Jon Wyant's daughter, after the 2022 obituaries. Wyant, then 83, living in South Carolina, worked 36 years at Mead and was director of new product development. His account: Crutchfield "came to him one day and asked him to make a binder that could hold these folders that had vertical pockets," with little more guidance; over a few weeks Wyant designed "the shape of the binder, the shape of the folders, the flap closure, the logo, and even the plastic clipboard in the back with the spot for the pencil," which he had "invented earlier with another guy" (Seaborn; see US 3,968,546). Quote: "It was a full three-dimensional prototype, designed, created with colors, named, the whole works. I can remember sitting at my desk with a tracing pad and tracing out of a typography book the logo." And: "I was very involved in putting that little turkey together." His friends call him "Trapper Jon."
- Wyant appears to hold the prototype on tape ("It was the exact trapper keeper. Here it is."). The broadcast does not describe it further. Mental Floss (2024) adds that it was made "at a Mead-affiliated plant in Missouri."
- Crutchfield's children, Ken and Carol, largely accepted that their father overclaimed. Carol: "Yeah, that makes sense that they were more than one person involved in creating the Trapper Keeper but, yeah, he took all the credit." Carol dates the overclaiming to the last years of his life, after the Mental Floss interview: "He would stop people in the restaurant, say I invented the Trapper Keeper." Ken: "He's always been a talker."
- Bennin says she "found this case study all about Crutch's approach to the project" and "talked to a former boss," and concludes: "I think he does deserve credit, just not all of it." She does not name the case study. A lead from the loose-leaf research names a published marketing case, "Mead Products: The Trapper and the Trapper Keeper"; I could not find its author, publisher, or year (unverified).
- Bennin notes the Mental Floss story "seemed like it was the inspiration for all those obituaries"; both the NYT and Washington Post obituaries linked to it.
- She could not find who painted the cover art and asks listeners to write in.

No memo or document is read on air. The May 1976 Boston brainstorming memo comes from Mental Floss's 2024 reporting, not from TAL.

The arc: company and press credit Crutchfield alone (2013–2022), the obituaries amplify it, Wyant's family contests it (2023), and the record is revised toward joint credit with Wyant as designer (2024). Nothing contemporary from 1976–1981 names either man in connection with the product, apart from the patents, which name Wyant and Seaborn and not Crutchfield (INFERENCE: the patents prove Wyant's and Seaborn's authorship of components, not of the whole, and say nothing about who conceived the product).

## 8. Surviving objects

- **Wyant's prototype**: held privately by Jon Wyant, said to have been made at a Mead-affiliated plant in Missouri (Mental Floss 2024; heard but not described on TAL 2023). RETROSPECTIVE description; no photograph located.
- **Crutchfield's early pieces**: Mental Floss reproduces "Early Trapper Keepers—one with the logo, one without" and a returned feedback card ("Fred's response card"), both "Courtesy of E. Bryant Crutchfield." Private, current location unknown.
- **Museums**: a Smithsonian collections search for "trapper keeper" returns no object, only a 2017 Smithsonian Learning Lab "Back to School" post (https://collections.si.edu/search/results.htm?q=%22trapper+keeper%22). The Strong National Museum of Play site search returns nothing. The Henry Ford and Cooper Hewitt searches did not complete. I found no museum object record for a Trapper Keeper (unverified absence, not proof).
- **ACCO's "Trapper conference room"** in Dayton (photo in Mental Floss) presumably holds company examples. Not documented.
- The market: the NYT (2022) cites an unopened 1992 Trapper Keeper listed on eBay for $699. Not a collection.
