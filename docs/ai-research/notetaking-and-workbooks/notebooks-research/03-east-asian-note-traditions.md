# Gathering, judging, and composing: Chinese notes and Japanese miscellanies

**Central question:** When does a collection of short observations become a work of scholarship or literature—and what would let us establish that transformation historically?

Chinese *biji* 筆記 and Japanese works now grouped as *zuihitsu* 随筆 make this question unusually productive. They invite comparison because both can contain short, heterogeneous passages. But resemblance of finished texts does not establish resemblance of working methods. A published miscellany, a remembered conversation, an author's account of revising notes, and a surviving draft notebook are different kinds of evidence.

The argument here is that gathering becomes composition through **acts of judgment**: recognizing a problem, placing an observation under a category, revising an explanation, drawing a consequence from an anecdote, or deciding what deserves preservation. Those acts can occur inside an individual entry. A miscellany need not be an unfinished essay waiting for a final synthesis. Equally, an apparently spontaneous literary voice can be a constructed effect, amplified by centuries of copying and editing. These are comparisons among particular Chinese and Japanese situations, not a single East Asian system or a genealogy leading to the Zettelkasten.

## First identify what “notes” means

Fu Daiwie's study of Song *biji* emphasizes short entries, *tiao*, within larger divisions, *juan*, and the possibility of implicit subject groupings even without explicit headings. His account connects the growth of these works with woodblock publishing and communities of knowledge. The visible opening of his article is sufficient to challenge the image of a completely unorganized private notebook; it is not sufficient to reconstruct how every author physically stored preliminary material. [Fu, “The Flourishing of Biji,” opening page, 103](https://www.persee.fr/doc/oroc_0754-5010_2007_hos_1_1_1071).

Nor is *biji* simply a synonym for excerpts copied while reading. An NTU thesis studies three different settings: Fan Zhen's experience of official historical compilation and *Dongzhai jishi*; Wang Pizhi's official career and social gatherings behind *Shengshui yantan lu*; and *Lupu biji* as a local official's reading record and communication with other literati. The abstract explicitly treats place, procedure, and product separately. This is a useful research framework, although the full thesis has not been read for this report. [NTU thesis repository, 2018, abstract and contents](https://tdr.lib.ntu.edu.tw/handle/123456789/21850?mode=full).

For the Japanese comparison, *zuihitsu* is often translated “following the brush.” That translation cannot establish that a given work was written without planning. It also cannot tell us whether its writer used an intermediate notebook. The historical application of the genre name is itself a research problem. The following cases therefore name individual works before applying broad labels.

## Shen Kuo: conversation becoming a technical explanation

Shen Kuo's *Mengxi bitan* 夢溪筆談, usually called *Dream Pool Essays* or *Brush Talks from Dream Brook*, belongs to the Northern Song context. A University of Vienna seminar resource reproduces a German translation of the preface: Shen describes remembering conversations with friends in retirement and treating brush and inkstone as conversational companions. This is an author's retrospective presentation of the book's origins, not an extant conversation transcript or a dated working notebook. [Vienna, “Mengxi Bitan,” preface translation credited to Gauler, 1987, p. 45](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/Kamigraphie/Mengxi_Bitan).

The movable-type entry gives a particularly sharp example of what the resulting composition can do. Di Doménico, Mina, and Santillán analyze entry 307, relating Shen's technical interests to his career as an official. The entry names Bi Sheng, describes preparing clay characters and assembling a printing surface, and evaluates when the method is efficient. It also reports that Bi Sheng's types passed into Shen's family's possession. The article cautions against treating similarly named figures as automatically the same person. [“The invention of Movable-Type Printing,” especially pp. 92–96](https://revpubli.unileon.es/ojs/index.php/sinologia/article/download/7230/5700/32747).

The important transformation is from knowledge of a practice to an explanation that a reader can inspect: materials, operations, and conditions of usefulness. My inference is that the entry's explanatory shape matters more for a history of composition than its brevity. A two-hundred-word account can already have a problem, evidence, and a conclusion.

There is also an asymmetry of authorship. In the account, Bi Sheng supplies the invention; Shen supplies the preserved description. Calling the whole episode “Shen's notes” risks making the recording scholar appear to be the source of the technical knowledge. We should ask separately who did the work, who reported it, who selected its features, and whose name later readers learned. We cannot recover those relationships merely by calling the text empirical.

## Gu Yanwu: the note as a revisable claim

Gu Yanwu's *Rizhilu* 日知錄 offers stronger direct evidence for a note-making procedure. Its short authorial preface says that he recorded insights from reading, returned to revise what did not fit, and removed a note if an earlier writer had already made its point. After more than thirty years, a collection resulted. The Chinese text is accessible in Project Gutenberg, ebook **25262**, under **序**. This is a digital transcription, not a facsimile of Gu's handwriting. [*Rizhilu*, preface](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/25262/pg25262-images.html).

Willard J. Peterson translates and discusses that statement in his review of Ian Johnston's selected translations. He recommends understanding the title as *Record of Knowledge Gained Day by Day*, rather than assuming “daily knowledge” means everyday wisdom. His account situates Gu's scholarship in an effort to discover knowledge that could help correct political and social life; he distinguishes the first printed collection of 1670 from the later, posthumous thirty-two-*juan* text of 1695. [Peterson, review, especially pp. 252 and 256](https://doi.org/10.1017/jch.2017.30).

This procedure complicates an attractive contemporary ideal: save everything now and let connections emerge later. Gu's self-description gives deletion a positive role. The criterion is not only whether something is interesting but whether the note contributes something that existing scholarship lacks. Accumulation is paired with an argument about why the accumulated material deserves a reader's time.

There are limits. A preface can prescribe an intellectual identity as well as describe practice. It does not tell us the dimensions of Gu's paper, whether the initial records were bound or loose, how often he revised, or who copied them. Nevertheless, it documents operations that mere fragmentary form cannot establish: capture after reading, reconsideration, comparison with predecessors, and removal. Its strength is procedural specificity, not material completeness.

For present research, the useful distinction is between a **source note** and an **insight note**. Copying a passage preserves access to someone else's words. Recording a claim about it begins responsibility for an interpretation. The latter needs a way to survive disagreement, correction, and eventual replacement. That is my proposed application of the evidence, not a claim that Gu used our modern note categories.

## Sei Shōnagon: lists as finished acts of judgment

The *Pillow Book* belongs to the world of Sei Shōnagon's service at the court of Empress Teishi around the turn of the eleventh century. Columbia's teaching page supplies translated excerpts in which the narrator describes gathering facts, stories, poems, and observations for her own amusement. It also includes the lists “Things That Cannot Be Compared” and “Things That Have Lost Their Power.” In the latter, stranded transport, a fallen tree, and an ineffectual reprimand occupy one category. The heading makes heterogeneous instances comparable through a judgment about lost efficacy. This reading concerns the transmitted literary passage; it does not establish the order in which its examples were first recorded. [Columbia, *Pillow Book* excerpts, translations credited to Morris and Keene](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/special/japan_600ce_pillowbook.htm).

The list supplies a research lesson: a category can be an argument before it becomes a paragraph. Choosing its heading defines the relation among examples. A new example tests that relation. An awkward example can force a revised heading or disclose a concealed assumption. We can study these effects without claiming to have uncovered the author's initial notebook.

Historical reception makes that restraint necessary. Gergana Ivanova explains that Ban Kōkei, an eighteenth-century literary historian, first applied *zuihitsu* to the *Pillow Book*. She emphasizes that no surviving source resolves exactly what Sei wrote, when she completed it, or what happened to her own manuscripts. Later manuscript and print versions differ substantially. The work's conventional generic identity should therefore not be projected back as an authorial decision. [Ivanova, Columbia University Press interview, 2019](https://cupblog.org/2019/08/13/qa-gergana-ivanova-on-unbinding-the-pillow-book-the-many-lives-of-a-japanese-classic/?amp=1).

A chapter summary in *The Cambridge History of Japanese Literature* additionally interprets the work as representing Teishi's salon and potentially commemorating its patron. It identifies overlapping list, essay, and diary modes. This is scholarly interpretation accessed through a chapter summary, not a complete chapter consulted here. [“The Pillow Book of Sei Shōnagon”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-japanese-literature/thepillow-bookof-sei-shonagon/CC0F63282A234D8968FDD685E2C93C3B).

Together these sources make “private notebook” an inadequate default. A claim of personal amusement can coexist with social positioning, patronage, and literary address. Later copyists, commentators, publishers, and translators participate in what becomes available as the author's voice. The question is not whether the work is sincere, but what kinds of judgment and mediation its sincerity cannot explain away.

## Kenkō: the apparent fragment and the constructed encounter

Linda H. Chance's *Formless in Form* treats Kenkō's *Tsurezuregusa* as a deliberate rhetorical work and challenges its routine classification as *zuihitsu*. The publisher's descriptive text, reproduced in the Google Books record, emphasizes the reader's encounter with impermanence through apparently fragmentary form. Only that description has been consulted; it should not substitute for reading Chance's detailed demonstration. [Chance, *Formless in Form*, bibliographic record and description](https://books.google.com/books/about/Formless_in_Form.html?id=kv6a2OplUoUC).

Meredith McKinney's translation provides both a primary passage and a transmission warning. Section 52 tells of a monk who visits subsidiary religious sites, assumes his pilgrimage complete, and fails to reach the destination further uphill. The anecdote closes with a recommendation to seek guidance. In her translation note, McKinney identifies the earliest surviving transcription as the Shōtetsu-bon, dated 1431, and explains her use of the 1613 Karasumaru-bon with occasional variant readings. [*Essays in Idleness and Hōjōki*, translation note and §52](https://www.tlu.ee/sites/default/files/Instituudid/T%C3%9CHI/%C3%B5ppekavad/Liberal%20Arts%20in%20Humanities%20tekstid/Essays%20in%20Idleness_%20and%20Hojoki%20-%20Kenko.pdf).

My reading is that §52 condenses a problem of research itself: reaching something plausible is not proof that one has reached the object sought. The final lesson reorganizes the anecdote retrospectively. That is a source-to-consequence movement visible in the text, even when a prior draft cannot be seen.

A later physical witness reveals another composition process. British Library **16107.g.8**, catalogue record **032-003907180**, is an incomplete illustrated blockprinted *Tsurezuregusa* assigned to the Kyoto edition of 1672. The catalogue describes printed reading marks, thirteen woodcuts, and an opening folio containing biographical notes and a synopsis. These are documented features of an edition that helps readers approach an older work. They are not Kenkō's working papers. The catalogue HTML was accessed; its page images were not inspected. [British Library record](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-003907180).

The popular story that Kenkō pasted preliminary scraps onto walls has not been established through a specialist source in this research. It should remain an unverified lead, not a historical demonstration of movable notes.

## A notes-to-output chain, with its missing links visible

The strongest procedural chain here combines two kinds of evidence without pretending they document one particular draft:

| Stage | Evidence | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Read a source and recognize an insight | Gu's preface explicitly links reading and immediate recording | Which surviving entry began from which particular reading session |
| Make a written claim | Gu says he recorded what he gained from reading | First wording and physical support |
| Reconsider and compare | The preface specifies revision and comparison with earlier writers | Revision frequency and exact changes |
| Remove or retain | Gu describes deleting claims already anticipated | Discarded notes and their quantity |
| Gather into a work | The preface describes a collection; Peterson supplies publication context | Complete sequence of manuscript arrangement and editorial labor |

An **invented classroom reconstruction**, clearly separate from Gu's history, would look like this: read a passage about public libraries; note the claim that access expands with digitization; discover evidence of account, language, or interface barriers; revise “access expands” into “one barrier is lowered while others remain”; group supporting and contradicting examples; write a short essay about what access means. The decisive composition occurs during revision of the claim. Arrangement helps make that revision accountable.

This reconstruction is useful precisely because it can preserve what the historical record lacks: an initial note, revision, rejection reason, and resulting paragraph. It should not imitate imaginary Chinese stationery or rename a modern database *biji*. Its purpose is to investigate an operation suggested by an explicitly documented account.

## Comparison without collapsing the contexts

| Case | Gathered material | Visible compositional act | Evidential boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shen Kuo | Remembered conversation and technical knowledge | Procedure plus evaluation of usefulness | Finished entry and authorial preface; no preliminary notebook inspected |
| Gu Yanwu | Insights from reading | Revision, comparison, deletion, collection | Procedure described; individual note-to-draft sequence unrecovered |
| Sei Shōnagon | Court scenes, observations, poems, examples | A list heading creates a relation | Transmitted literary text; later genre attribution |
| Kenkō | Anecdote and reflection | Narrative generates a consequence | Later textual witnesses and editorial apparatus |

These differences suggest three separate research projects. A material history asks what paper, bindings, copies, and print surfaces survive. A genetic history asks how wording and order changed. A social history asks whose knowledge the writer could gather and whose labor carried it forward. Confusing those projects produces confident workflow stories from insufficient evidence. Combining them carefully could show how an entry acquired authority, not merely how it acquired a location.

## Two feasible week 5 experiments

1. **Make the heading do intellectual work, 40 minutes.** Each participant records eight short observations from a shared studio activity. Keep the original order and timestamps. Then create two lists from the same observations under different headings—for example, “Things that make collaboration easier” and “Things that conceal labor.” Write a 250-word interpretation of each list. Exchange them and identify where a heading overstates its evidence. Preserve the originals, both arrangements, and the revisions. The comparison tests whether categorization generates a claim rather than simply tidying information. The *Pillow Book* supplies a provocation, not a recreated Heian practice.

2. **Revise or withdraw, 50 minutes.** Give pairs one short reading and ask each to write an insight with a passage locator. Supply a second reading that complicates it. Mark each note “retain,” “revise,” or “withdraw,” with a reason; keep withdrawn notes in a separate audit record for the exercise. Draft a 300-word argument and attach its note history. Compare which changes improved the argument and which merely softened it. This modern experiment adapts the operations Gu describes while deliberately making revision observable. No assumption about his original filing apparatus is required.

## Three concrete source leads

- **Gu Yanwu, Gutenberg 25262, 序, immediately after the title.** Collate the brief procedural statement against Peterson's translation at printed p. 256 and a scholarly Chinese edition. Then choose one *Rizhilu* entry for an actual textual-history study. The transcription's edition provenance needs checking before treating it as an authoritative base text.
- **Shen Kuo, movable-type entry 307.** Follow the Spanish article's opening bibliographic note and analysis at printed pp. 89–96 to the Chinese editions. Compare translations' treatment of the inherited types. The cited Chinese editions were not inspected here.
- **British Library 16107.g.8 / 032-003907180.** Examine the unnumbered preliminary folio and the catalogued illustrations, beginning at ff. 3a and 6a, to ask how synopsis, biography, and pictures guide a reader's experience. The physical object or reliable page images are needed to move beyond catalogue description.

## Uncertainties and next work

No autograph working notebook was inspected for any of the four main cases. The report therefore establishes several compositional operations and kinds of evidence, not a complete history of notebook hardware. Chinese philological note traditions such as *zhaji* deserve a separate, historically located investigation; their relationship to *biji* should not be decided by English translations alone. Likewise, a study of later Japanese scholar-collectors could provide closer evidence of working notebooks than the classic literary works chosen here.

Further work should test three questions: Can successive manuscript or print versions expose rearrangement within a particular entry or collection? Can correspondence identify the people who supplied information without receiving authorship? Can a discarded or revised claim be found, so that the history includes failed interpretations as well as successful preservation? The most valuable next discovery would be a documented transformation, not another famous work whose title sounds like “notebook.”
