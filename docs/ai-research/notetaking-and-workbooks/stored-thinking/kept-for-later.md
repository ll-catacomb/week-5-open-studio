# Kept for Later: Six Arrangements for Future Thinking

*Research essay for the Week 5 wall exhibition · TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI · Draft 1 (markdown)*

---

## Wall label (150 words)

**Kept for Later**

Before search bars, people built machines for remembering out of wax, parchment, paper, drawers, and other people's labor. A Roman admiral dictated excerpts to an enslaved secretary riding beside him. A Paris cleric sorted six thousand quotations under 266 headings, each with a letter-address. John Locke indexed his notebooks by the first letter and vowel of a word. Gu Yanwu, travelling North China with his books loaded on mules, deleted any note an earlier scholar had already written. Darwin cut the useful pages out of his notebooks and filed them in portfolios. Niklas Luhmann gave ninety thousand cards permanent addresses and linked them by hand.

None of these is an early computer. Each made some kinds of thinking easy and others hard, and each threw something away. As you build your research pack, ask: what will yours let you find again, and what will it quietly lose?

## Essay

### A framework, and a warning

Ann Blair's *[Too Much to Know: Managing Scholarly Information before the Modern Age](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300168495/too-much-to-know/)* (Yale, 2010) starts from a deflationary point: the sense of having more to read than any one person can absorb is old, and so are the basic responses to it. In an earlier essay Blair reduced those responses to four operations — storing, sorting, selecting, and summarizing — which she argued can be found, in some combination, wherever people take notes ([Blair, "Note Taking as an Art of Transmission," *Critical Inquiry* 31 (2004): 85–107, at 85](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-5f82-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)). The book tests that claim against Islamic and Chinese reference traditions as well as European ones (ch. 1, pp. 11–61) and treats note-taking itself as information management (ch. 2, pp. 62–116).

Blair's more specific argument, made first in "[Humanist Methods in Natural Philosophy: The Commonplace Book](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-8744-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)" (*Journal of the History of Ideas* 53 \[1992\]: 541–551), is that a technique learned in grammar school — copying striking passages under topical headings — did not stay in the classroom. It shaped what late Renaissance natural philosophy looked like: an accumulation of particulars gathered from books and from experience, sorted under heads, and recombined. The notebook was not a neutral container. Its structure pushed the knowledge it held toward a certain form.

That is the premise of this exhibition: a note system is an argument about what matters, and it trains the person who keeps it. Blair also supplies the warning. In a 2010 overview she stressed how rarely notes survive from antiquity and the Middle Ages, and how much the early modern explosion of note-keeping depended on contingencies: paper becoming far cheaper than parchment, and a humanist fear of another collapse of learning ([Blair, "The Rise of Note-Taking in Early Modern Europe," *Intellectual History Review* 20 (2010): 303–316](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/4774908)). The systems below did not evolve toward a destination. They answered local problems with local materials, and several of them were dead ends.

For each of the six examples, four questions organize what follows: what goes in, how it is transformed, how it is found again, and what new work it makes possible. The early modern slot holds a deliberate pair, John Locke's commonplace book and Gu Yanwu's *Rizhilu*, two contemporaries with no documented contact who solved overlapping problems in completely different ways.

### 1. Rome, before 79 CE: Pliny the Elder's 160 notebooks

Almost everything we know about how Pliny the Elder (23/24–79 CE) worked comes from one letter by his nephew (*Epistulae* 3.5; Latin text with commentary in [Mayor, *Pliny's Letters, Book III* (1880)](https://www.archive.org/download/lettersbookiii00plinuoft/lettersbookiii00plinuoft.pdf); English in the Loeb translation by B. Radice, vol. 1, pp. 172–79). The notebooks themselves are gone. Papyrus rarely lasted more than a few centuries, and no copyist had a reason to preserve working notes.

**What entered.** In principle, everything that was read. The nephew reports that his uncle read nothing without excerpting from it, and liked to say that no book was so bad that some part of it was not useful (3.5.10). Reading was continuous and mostly aural: a *lector* read aloud during meals, in the bath while Pliny was being rubbed down, and on the road (3.5.10–14).

**How it was transformed.** Two stages, as modern scholars reconstruct them (Blair 2010, following Tiziano Dorandi): quick marks and extracts (*adnotationes*) on wax tablets, then a second, sorted copy onto papyrus. When travelling, Pliny kept a *notarius*, a shorthand writer, beside him with a book and tablets, whose hands were protected with long sleeves in winter so that cold weather would not cost a minute of study (3.5.15). Both reader and writer were almost certainly enslaved. The nephew includes an anecdote: when a friend at dinner made the reader go back over a mispronounced passage, Pliny asked whether he had understood it, and on being told yes, complained that the interruption had cost them more than ten lines (3.5.12). The system ran on other people's voices and hands, and on the expectation that they would not pause.

**How it was found again.** Pliny left his nephew 160 volumes of *commentarii electorum*, "notebooks of selected passages," written on both sides in a very small hand (3.5.17). We do not know their internal order. We do know how Pliny made his finished work searchable. The *Natural History* opens with a book that is entirely a table: the contents of each of the following thirty-six books, and the authorities used for each. In his preface Pliny says he added it so that the dedicatee would not have to read the whole work, but could look for just what he wanted and know where to find it (*Naturalis historia*, praef. 33).

**What it produced.** By Pliny's own count, about 20,000 facts drawn from about 2,000 volumes by a hundred chosen authors (praef. 17). He approvingly cites Domitius Piso's dictum that what we need are *thesauri*, storehouses, rather than books.

**Expense.** Pliny said he had refused an offer of 400,000 sesterces for the notebooks (3.5.17) — the property qualification for the equestrian order, as Blair notes (2010, n. 15). The notes were valued like an estate.

A useful foil is Aulus Gellius, a generation later, who assembled his *Attic Nights* from his own excerpts and chose to keep them in the accidental order in which he had made them, adding a list of chapter summaries at the front so readers could find their way (*Noctes Atticae*, praef. 2, 25). Pliny sorted toward a total survey of nature. Gellius kept the miscellany. Both depended on a finding list that sat outside the collection.

**Transmission, documented.** Renaissance teachers used this letter. The Jesuits Francesco Sacchini and Jeremias Drexel cited Pliny as warrant for excerpting, and Conrad Gessner repeated the bad-books maxim to justify listing every title in his *Bibliotheca universalis* of 1545 (Blair 2010). The descent is real, but it runs through readers choosing a precedent, not through a continuous technique.

### 2. Paris, 1306: Thomas of Ireland's *Manipulus florum*

A florilegium is a "gathering of flowers," a collection of authoritative passages arranged for reuse. The most widely circulated medieval example is the *Manipulus florum* ("handful of flowers") completed around 1306 by Thomas of Ireland, a former fellow of the Sorbonne who compiled it from that college's library. It survives in more than 180 manuscripts and appeared in at least fifty printed editions between 1483 and 1887 ([Nighman, "The Janus Intertextuality Search Engine," *Digital Medievalist* 2012](https://journal.digitalmedievalist.org/article/id/6985/); [Electronic *Manipulus florum* Project](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/)). The foundational study is Mary A. Rouse and Richard H. Rouse, *Preachers, Florilegia and Sermons: Studies on the "Manipulus florum" of Thomas of Ireland* (Toronto: PIMS, 1979), which edits Thomas's preface (pp. 236–38) and his list of authors and works (pp. 251–310).

**What entered.** About 6,000 excerpts and proverbs from classical, patristic, and medieval authors. Some reached Thomas through earlier anthologies rather than directly from the original works; the electronic edition's source apparatus, which links about 95 percent of entries to their likely sources, has made it possible to track these intermediaries (Nighman 2012, §§1–8).

**How it was transformed.** Each passage was detached, attributed, sometimes abridged, and sometimes merged with neighbors; Thomas often combined several Senecan proverbs into one entry (Nighman 2012, §1). The preface frames the work through the Book of Ruth: the compiler is a gleaner, following the reapers and gathering what they left in the field (preface in Rouse and Rouse, pp. 236–38; English translation on the [project site](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/)). The metaphor is modest and also a claim: what others passed over can be made useful.

**How it was found again.** This is where the *Manipulus* is most interesting. Thomas arranged 266 topics alphabetically, from *Abstinencia* to *Christus* (which falls at the end because manuscripts abbreviate it with Greek letters). He gave every entry under a topic its own reference letter, doubling letters once a topic passed twenty-three entries. At the end of nearly every topic he listed related topics and specific lettered entries filed elsewhere ([project "About" page](https://manipulus-project.wlu.ca/about.html)). An excerpt therefore had an address — *Abstinencia* c, say — that held across copies, and the cross-references made the alphabet into a web. The appended list of authors and works pointed readers back toward whole books.

**What it produced.** Sermons, chiefly, as the Rouses argued; but also any composition that needed authorities, including university writing. Some scholars read it as aimed at students as much as preachers.

**Labor, materials, and scale.** Explicits in some manuscripts say the work was begun by John of Wales and continued and completed by Thomas. How much John contributed is still debated (Nighman, "Revisiting John of Wales's Role in the Creation of the *Manipulus florum*," in Hamesse, Muñoz Jiménez, and Nighman, eds., *New Perspectives on Thomas of Ireland's "Manipulus florum"*, PIMS 2019, pp. 17–30). The text's remarkable stability across manuscripts owes much to the Paris stationers, who rented out exemplars in numbered sections (*peciae*) for professional scribes to copy. Early copies were on parchment: Tours, Bibliothèque municipale MS 35 runs to 277 leaves of about 229 × 160 mm. Later copies were cheaper. The Beinecke Library's fifteenth-century Italian miscellany containing the *Manipulus* is on paper, in a single scribe's italic hand, heavily annotated by that scribe and later readers ([Yale catalogue record](https://collections.library.yale.edu/catalog/2005490)).

**What it made easy and hard.** Finding an authority on a vice in seconds was easy; knowing what the authority meant in context was hard. The electronic edition repairs exactly that loss by setting each quotation beside the passage it came from.

### 3a. Oxford and Amsterdam, 1650s–1706: John Locke's "New Method"

By the seventeenth century, keeping a commonplace book was standard advice to anyone with a Latin education. Printers sold model collections and blank books ruled with predetermined heads. Blair's 1992 article shows how this school routine carried over into natural philosophy; Richard Yeo has shown how it shaped the working lives of English naturalists and philosophers (Yeo, *Notebooks, English Virtuosi, and Early Modern Science*, Chicago 2014; Yeo, "[Thinking with Excerpts: John Locke (1632–1704) and His Notebooks](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bewi.201900024)," *Berichte zur Wissenschaftsgeschichte* 43 \[2020\]).

John Locke kept such books for decades and devised a way of indexing them that he shared with friends before publishing it in French in the *Bibliothèque universelle et historique* (1686). The English translation appeared after his death as *A New Method of Making Common-Place-Books* (London: J. Greenwood, 1706; also in *Posthumous Works*, 1706, pp. 311–336; critical text in the Clarendon Edition of Locke's literary writings, 2019). Harvard's Houghton Library holds a copy (\*EC65 L7934 706n) that has been [digitized](https://curiosity.lib.harvard.edu/reading/catalog/42-990033581460203941); another scan is on the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/13925922180LockeCommonplaceBook) under a Public Domain Mark.

**The problem.** Predetermined heads wasted paper — some heads stayed empty, others overflowed — and the index of a long-kept book became chaotic.

**The method.** A blank bound book with a two-page index at the front: a grid with the letters of the alphabet down the side and the five vowels across. To enter something, you choose a head word (say, *Epistola*), note its first letter and the first vowel that follows it (E-i), write the head in the margin of the next empty page, and record that page number in the E-i cell. When a page fills, you continue on the next blank page and write a pointer forward from the old page and back from the new one.

**What it changed.** Heads now emerged from what the keeper actually read rather than from a printed scheme; the commonplace book became personal in its categories. Retrieval became mechanical but coarse. The grid tells you only which pages might hold *Epistola*; you still have to scan every page listed under E-i. The method saves paper and imposes discipline; it does not do your thinking. Locke's own index pages can be seen in Bodleian MS Locke f. 18, pp. 110–111 (reproduced in the Public Domain Review's [feature on the method](https://brewminate.com/john-lockes-method-for-common-place-books-1685/)).

**Who it was for.** The 1706 title page addresses "all gentlemen, especially students of divinity, physick, and law." The method was published at a friend's request, framed as a letter to Nicolas Toinard. Michael Stolberg argues that it changed what kind of thing a note was, favoring small, self-contained entries filed under a single word (*Early Science and Medicine* 19 \[2014\]: 448–470).

**Transmission, documented.** The method was commercialized. Within a century London printers sold blank books pre-printed with Locke's grid, among them *Bell's Common-Place Book, formed generally upon the principles recommended and practised by Mr Locke*. A personal workaround had become a product.

### 3b. North China, 1650s–1695: Gu Yanwu's *Rizhilu*

Gu Yanwu (1613–1682) was born into a landed family in Kunshan, near Suzhou. After the Manchu conquest of 1644 he remained loyal to the fallen Ming, refused office under the Qing, and from the late 1650s spent most of the rest of his life travelling through North China. His notes became the *Rizhilu* 日知錄, usually translated *Record of Daily Knowledge*; the title comes from *Analects* 19.5, where a disciple says that one should learn each day what one lacks and each month not forget what one has mastered. The work is usually placed at the head of Qing "evidential scholarship" (*kaozheng*), a movement that prized verifiable textual and material evidence over speculative philosophy (Benjamin A. Elman, *From Philosophy to Philology: Intellectual and Social Aspects of Change in Late Imperial China*, Harvard Council on East Asian Studies, 1984; rev. ed. 2001). English selections: Ian Johnston, trans., *Record of Daily Knowledge and Collected Poems and Essays* ([Columbia University Press, 2017](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/record-of-daily-knowledge-and-collected-poems-and-essays/9780231170482/)).

The *Rizhilu* belongs to a Chinese genre with its own long history, the *biji* 筆記 or *zhaji* 札記 — "brush notes," "jotted notes" — and it should be read in that tradition rather than as an exotic commonplace book.

**What entered.** Readings in the classics, histories, legal codes, and local gazetteers; inscriptions; and what Gu saw and heard on the road. His biographer Quan Zuwang (1705–1755) describes him travelling with two horses and three mules carrying books. At a frontier pass he would question old soldiers about the terrain, and if what they said did not match what he had read, he would open his books on the spot and compare (Quan Zuwang, "Tinglin xiansheng shendao biao," in *Jieqiting ji*). Gu's own essays show the same habit. In one, on the location of the sacred Northern Peak, he faults earlier officials for arguing from texts without going to the place, and reports that he went to both candidate sites and wrote down what he saw (*Tinglin wenji*, juan 1, [Sibu congkan text on Wikisource](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E4%BA%AD%E6%9E%97%E8%A9%A9%E6%96%87%E9%9B%86_%28%E5%9B%9B%E9%83%A8%E5%8F%A2%E5%88%8A%E6%9C%AC%29/%E6%96%87%E9%9B%86%E5%8D%B7%E7%AC%AC%E4%B8%80)).

**How it was transformed.** Each entry begins with a short title, often a phrase from a classic or a named institution, followed by assembled evidence — quotations from sources across many centuries — and Gu's judgment. The transformation that distinguishes the *Rizhilu*, however, is subtraction. In the short note Gu placed at the head of the work, he says that since youth, whenever reading gave him something, he wrote it down; where it proved wrong he revised it; and where he found that the ancients had already said it before him, he deleted it — and that it took thirty-some years to make one volume (paraphrased in the *Siku quanshu* abstract; see also the [Ricci Institute catalogue note](https://web.bc.edu/ricci/indexAuthor.php?id=13532) citing Liang Qichao). A note survived only if it was both correct and new.

Gu explained why in a letter to a friend who had asked how many more chapters he had finished. Compiling books, he wrote, has become like minting coins. The ancients gathered copper in the mountains; people now buy old coins, call them scrap, and melt them down to recast. Since they last met, a year of reading morning and night had yielded only some ten-odd entries ("Yu ren shu" no. 10, *Tinglin wenji*, juan 4; my translation). The image targets exactly the practice the *Manipulus florum* made efficient: recombining what others have already struck.

**How it was found again.** Through titled entries grouped into thematic *juan* (chapters) — classics first, then government, finance, ritual, examination, geography, literature, and so on — with a table of contents listing every entry title (see the [*Siku quanshu* text on Wikisource](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%97%A5%E7%9F%A5%E9%8C%84_%28%E5%9B%9B%E5%BA%AB%E5%85%A8%E6%9B%B8%E6%9C%AC%29)). The arrangement is a classified sequence, not an index, and it assumes a reader trained in the same canon who knows where a question about salt monopolies or mourning grades belongs.

**What it produced.** Arguments about institutions. Gu used his entries to diagnose why the Ming had fallen and to argue for decentralizing authority. The notes were meant to be acted on, not just consulted.

**Labor, money, and editing.** Gu paid for the first printing, eight juan, in 1670, from his own funds (the Fushantang edition). He kept revising and circulated drafts to friends for correction, and in a preface to the first printing he wrote that past gains were nothing to boast of and future achievements should not be limited by them (*Tinglin wenji*, juan 2, [Wikisource](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E4%BA%AD%E6%9E%97%E8%A9%A9%E6%96%87%E9%9B%86_%28%E5%9B%9B%E9%83%A8%E5%8F%A2%E5%88%8A%E6%9C%AC%29/%E6%96%87%E9%9B%86%E5%8D%B7%E7%AC%AC%E4%BA%8C)). In the same juan, his postface to his phonological work records that he carried that manuscript with him everywhere for over thirty years, recopied it by hand three times, and paid the carvers with money from selling property. The full thirty-two-juan *Rizhilu* appeared only in 1695, thirteen years after his death, edited — and cut — by his student Pan Lei and carved at Jianyang in Fujian (the Suichutang edition). A copy at Tunghai University Library — eleven columns per half-leaf, a block frame of about 15 × 20 cm — carries red-ink reading marks dated 1897–98 by a later owner ([Tunghai University Library Bulletin, n.s. 86, pp. 60–69](https://dcollection.lib.thu.edu.tw/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/86%E6%9C%9F60-69.pdf)).

**What disappeared.** The printed *Rizhilu* is not quite Gu's *Rizhilu*. Manuscript copies of the thirty-two-juan text survive, including one formerly owned by Zhang Ji and printed in Taiwan in 1958 as the "original manuscript" text; collating them against the printed tradition has shown passages softened or removed, some of them touching on politically sensitive matters under Qing rule. A modern collated edition (Zhang Jinghua, *Chaoben Rizhilu jiaozhu*, East China Normal University Press, 2021) rests on these comparisons. Gu's discipline of deletion was followed by other people's deletions, which he did not choose.

### 4. London and Down, 1837–1856: Darwin's Notebook B, then the portfolios

In July 1837, nine months after returning from the *Beagle*, Charles Darwin opened a small notebook labelled "B" for his thoughts on the transmutation of species. It is roughly 10 × 17 cm, about the size of a postcard, and had 280 numbered pages (Cambridge University Library, [MS DAR 121](https://archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/753701); facsimile on [Darwin Online](http://darwin-online.org.uk/content/frameset?itemID=CUL-DAR121.-&pageseq=38&viewtype=side); transcription by Paul H. Barrett, [1960](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1960_BarrettBnotebook_F1575/1960_BarrettBnotebook_F1575.html); standard edition in Barrett, Gautrey, Herbert, Kohn, and Smith, eds., *Charles Darwin's Notebooks, 1836–1844*, Cambridge 1987).

**What entered.** Questions, reading notes, facts reported by breeders and correspondents, half-formed laws, reminders to himself. On page 36 Darwin wrote "I think" and drew a small branching diagram of descent with lettered terminal species — the most reproduced page in his archive.

**How it was transformed.** First, by being written in sequence. A notebook in chronological order preserves the order of thought, including its wrong turns. Then, much later, by being dismantled. On the first page of Notebook B Darwin wrote that all useful pages had been cut out on 7 December 1856, the year he began the long "big species book" from which the *Origin* was later abridged. Sixty-three of the 280 pages were excised (Barrett 1960). Darwin marked many of the removed pages with large figures in pencil corresponding to the topics under which he refiled them (Gavin de Beer et al., "[Pages Excised by Darwin](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1967_Notebooks_F1574f.html)," 1967). The notebook was a stage, not a destination.

**How it was found again.** By the portfolio system Darwin described late in life. He kept thirty to forty large portfolios in cabinets with labelled shelves, into which he could at once drop a detached reference or memorandum; he indexed the facts relevant to his work at the back of books he owned and wrote separate abstracts of books he did not; and before starting a subject he consulted all the short indexes to make one general classified index (*Autobiography*, ed. Nora Barlow, 1958, p. 137; quoted in [Stauffer, ed., *Charles Darwin's Natural Selection*, 1975](https://darwin-online.org.uk/content/contentblock?basepage=1&itemID=F1583&hitpage=701&viewtype=side)). He also kept a running record of every book he read.

**What it produced.** The theory of natural selection — formulated in a later notebook in the series — and the evidence for the *Origin* and the books that followed. Darwin also says he made a habit of writing down at once any fact that contradicted his conclusions, because unfavourable facts slipped from memory more easily.

**Labor and expense.** Darwin's independent income paid for a house, a library, and household staff; his wife Emma read aloud to him; copyists made fair copies; and hundreds of correspondents fed him observations. The "detached memorandum" was often a letter from someone else. Notebooks B and C vanished from Cambridge University Library around 2000 and were returned anonymously in 2022 — a reminder that a single-copy paper system is also a single point of failure.

**What it made easy and hard.** Sequence made it easy to think forward. Excision made it easy to assemble a book. Together they made it hard for anyone, including Darwin, to see the original order again; it took twentieth-century editors searching archives to restore the pages to their places.

### 5. Bielefeld, 1951–1997: Niklas Luhmann's *Zettelkasten*

The sociologist Niklas Luhmann (1927–1998) began his card index in the early 1950s, as a legal clerk with no academic post, because folders of notes kept failing him. By his death it held about 90,000 handwritten A6 cards in two collections: about 23,000 cards from 1951–62 in 108 subject sections, and about 67,000 from 1963–97 in eleven top-level areas. The best account is by Johannes F. K. Schmidt, coordinator of the Bielefeld edition ("[Niklas Luhmann's Card Index: The Fabrication of Serendipity](https://www.uni-bielefeld.de/fakultaeten/soziologie/forschung/luhmann-archiv/pdf/jschmidt_niklas-luhmanns-card-index_-sociologica_2018_12-1.pdf)," *Sociologica* 12, no. 1 \[2018\]: 53–60; longer version in Alberto Cevolini, ed., *Forgetting Machines*, Brill 2016, pp. 289–311). The cards are being digitized and edited by the [Niklas Luhmann-Archiv](https://niklas-luhmann-archiv.de/nachlass/zettelkasten) in a project that runs from 2015 to 2030. Luhmann's own short account is "Kommunikation mit Zettelkästen: Ein Erfahrungsbericht," in H. Baier et al., eds., *Öffentliche Meinung und sozialer Wandel* (Westdeutscher Verlag, 1981), pp. 222–228.

**What entered.** Not excerpts, mostly. Luhmann read with a running set of rough notes and, in the evening, rewrote them into new cards, asking of each book how it could be connected to cards he had already written (Schmidt 2018, p. 55). About 75,000 cards record readings, his own arguments, and concepts; the rest are bibliography and index.

**How it was transformed.** Each card received a permanent number that never changed. A card developing a side-point of card 1/1 became 1/1a and was physically inserted between 1/1 and 1/2; a branch off that became 1/1a1, and so on, producing addresses as long as 21/3a1p5c4fB1a (Schmidt 2018, pp. 56–57). The collection grew inward, by insertion, rather than only at the end. Luhmann also wrote the numbers of distant cards onto existing ones: about 20,000 links in the first collection and 30,000 in the second, roughly one on every card or every second card (p. 57).

**How it was found again.** Not through a table of contents — Luhmann never made one — but through a keyword index of about 1,250 entries for the first collection and 3,200 for the second, rewritten from scratch four times as it became disordered (p. 58). The index deliberately listed only one to four entry points per term. From there, the hand-written links carried the reader outward into regions she had not meant to visit. Schmidt notes a corresponding risk: cards or small sections that were never linked became effectively unreachable, and some appear never to have been touched again.

**What it produced.** More than 500 publications and a theory of society built from interdependent concepts, which the box's lack of hierarchy suited. Luhmann described it as a communication partner, not a store. A card in the section where the index reflects on itself states the principle bluntly: "without writing, there is no thinking" (ZK II, card 9/8g, trans. Schmidt 2018, p. 59).

**What it kept.** Errors. Luhmann did not remove superseded cards; he added new ones beside them. The box is therefore a record of how his ideas changed — Schmidt calls it an intellectual autobiography — in a way that a deleted-and-rewritten digital file is not.

**Materials and labor.** Cheap paper, often the backs of copies and other scrap; wooden cabinets; and enormous amounts of one man's time, made possible by a professorship. The present digital edition depends on a second collective labor of scanning, transcription, and encoding, funded by a state academy.

### 6. Now: a Zotero library and its folder

For the contemporary case, take a concrete and common tool: Zotero, a free, open-source reference manager first released in 2006 by the Center for History and New Media at George Mason University.

**What enters.** Bibliographic records captured from library catalogues, publisher pages, and databases with a browser click; the PDFs and web snapshots attached to them; highlights and comments made in Zotero's reader; free-standing notes. Very little of this is typed by the user. The metadata was produced by cataloguers and publishers, and the "translators" that scrape it from websites are written and maintained by a volunteer community.

**How it is transformed.** Records are normalized into a database. A single item can sit in many collections at once without being copied — Zotero's documentation compares collections to playlists rather than folders — and can carry any number of tags ([Zotero documentation, "Collections and Tags"](https://www.zotero.org/support/collections_and_tags)). Highlights in a PDF can be pulled out into notes, keeping a link back to the page.

**How it is found again.** Full-text search, tag filters, saved searches, and the collection tree. Retrieval is fine-grained and almost free, which is new. Note that the same documentation warns that tags travel with an item when it is copied into another library, but collection placements do not. The arrangement — the part that reflects the user's thinking — is the least portable layer.

**What it produces.** Citations, most visibly. Whether it produces arguments depends on whether the user writes notes; the tool makes collecting easier than writing.

**Expense, literacy, labor.** The software is free; synced storage beyond a small allowance is paid. Access to most of the PDFs depends on institutional subscriptions, which means a Harvard student's research folder is partly a library's licensing budget. Durability is uncertain: Blair ends her 2010 survey by asking whether anyone, including us, will be able to read today's notes once they stop seeming worth migrating to new hardware and software.

### The operations compared

**Copying** was the only way to make a second instance, and Renaissance teachers valued it as discipline: it slowed reading and pressed the passage into memory (Blair 2010). In Zotero copying is costless and does none of that work; the friction has to be designed back in.

**Excerpting** means cutting a passage out of its setting. Pliny and Thomas excerpted others' words to be reused as authorities. Luhmann mostly refused to excerpt, rewriting readings in his own terms. Gu excerpted heavily but subordinated each quotation to his own argument and treated recombination without new evidence as recasting old coins.

**Annotating** keeps notes attached to their source. It preserves context but scatters knowledge across objects; Darwin's back-of-book indexes are a compromise, annotation gathered into a list.

**Indexing** separates finding from order. Pliny's Book 1 sits outside the collection; Locke's grid needs no prior categories but is coarse; Luhmann's index is deliberately incomplete, a door rather than a map.

**Cross-referencing** makes an order into a network. Thomas's lettered addresses, Luhmann's card numbers, and Locke's forward-and-back page pointers all depend on items having stable addresses. Without fixed locations, there are no reliable links.

**Rearranging** is what slips and excised pages allow and bound books resist. Darwin used both: the bound notebook for thinking in order, the knife for building a book. Luhmann rejected rearrangement — cards never moved — and got flexibility from insertion and links.

**Discarding** is the operation the systems disagree about most. Pliny and Gessner wanted to waste nothing. Luhmann discarded nothing, even his mistakes. Gu made deletion the criterion of value. Darwin's "useful pages cut out" is a discard in reverse: what was left behind was the residue. Every one of these systems discarded context, and most never acknowledged it.

---

### Transmission and resemblance

It is tempting to draw a line from Pliny to Thomas to Locke to Luhmann to Zotero and call it the history of the database. Some links in such a line are documented. Renaissance teachers explicitly took Pliny as their model; the *Manipulus florum* was printed into the nineteenth century; London printers sold Locke's grid; Schmidt places Luhmann within the card-index tradition that grew out of early modern excerpting; and Luhmann's numbering has been explicitly adopted by some contemporary note-taking methods and apps.

Other links are resemblances that a modern observer notices. Thomas's letter-addresses and cross-references look like hyperlinks, and Luhmann's links look like a web; Schmidt himself calls the card index an analog anticipation of the database. These are illuminating analogies made in hindsight. Thomas was not building hypertext; he was helping a preacher find three authorities on avarice before Sunday.

The pairing of Locke and Gu is the clearest case. They were near contemporaries working on bound notebooks of collected passages and their own judgments. Nothing suggests either knew of the other. Their systems answer different questions. Locke's asks how to find something again with minimal waste. Gu's asks whether something deserves to exist at all. Treating the *Rizhilu* as "a Chinese commonplace book" would erase precisely the feature — deletion of the already-said, testing texts against places — that makes it worth studying. It also sits in a tradition of compendia and classified encyclopedias (*leishu*) older than the European printed reference book, which Blair treats as a parallel development, not an ancestor.

A further reason to resist the evolutionary story: many of these systems were idiosyncratic and hard to inherit. Blair notes that note collections were often obscure even to the heirs who received them, and that the note-closet Thomas Harrison designed in the 1640s is known to have been built only twice (Blair 2010; *Too Much to Know*, pp. 93ff.). Luhmann's box worked for Luhmann; its afterlife as a productivity method is a new practice, not a continuation.

---

### What detachment costs

Every arrangement here starts by pulling something out of where it was. That makes the thing mobile, comparable, and combinable. It also severs the thing from the conditions that gave it meaning: the paragraph around a sentence, the voice of a reader, the mountain pass where a soldier's account contradicted the gazetteer, the order in which Darwin thought something before he knew what it meant.

Some systems kept a thread back. Pliny named his authorities; Thomas listed his; Gu set evidence beside judgment; Zotero keeps a link from a highlight to its page. Others cut the thread on purpose, because the point was to make something new. Neither choice is neutral. A research pack built for a creative project should decide, for each item, whether its value lies in being able to return to its source or in being free of it.

## Three historical design choices to test in a research pack

1. **Permanent addresses and links written at entry time** (Thomas of Ireland, Luhmann). Give every item in the pack a fixed ID that never changes, and require at least one hand-written link to an existing item before a new one can be added. Test: after two weeks, does following links surface connections that browsing folders would not, and how many items end up unlinked?
2. **A deletion rule** (Gu Yanwu). Before an item stays in the pack, ask whether it adds evidence or a judgment that is not already in a source you have. If not, cut it and record the cut in a short log. Test: is the pack smaller and more pointed, and does the deletion log itself become useful material?
3. **Write in sequence, then cut and refile at a deadline** (Darwin). Keep a single chronological notebook for the first phase of the project. At a fixed date, physically or digitally cut out the useful entries and refile them by the parts of the final work, marking each with where it came from. Test: what do you learn from what was left behind, and can you still reconstruct how your thinking moved?

---

## A question these precedents do not settle

Every system here had hidden collaborators who selected and shaped material — Pliny's enslaved secretary, the Paris stationers, Gu's correspondents and carvers, Pan Lei with his editing knife, Darwin's correspondents. But their selections were made of the same stuff as the collection, on wax or paper, at an address, where someone could in principle check them. When an AI system retrieves for you, it is also a collaborator that has absorbed an excerpt collection far larger than any of these, without keeping the addresses, and it can produce a fluent passage that was never in any source. **If the thing that finds material for you can also compose what it "finds," what would it mean to find something again, and who is answerable for the selection?**
