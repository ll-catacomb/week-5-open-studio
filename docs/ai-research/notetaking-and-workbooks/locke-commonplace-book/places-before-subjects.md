# Places Before Subjects

**John Locke's "New Method" and the eighteenth-century commonplace book**

*Illustrated essay for TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI. Week 5 wall exhibition: research, workbooks, and gathering the materials for creative production. Draft for iteration.*

## Wall label (150 words)

**Places Before Subjects** John Locke, *A New Method of Making Common-Place-Books* (London: J. Greenwood, 1706). Wellcome Collection; digitized by the Internet Archive.

Locke's problem is yours. Material arrives in the order you find it, but you need it later by subject. His solution, first printed in French in 1686, fits on two ruled pages. Name each note by a key word. Take that word's first letter and the vowel that follows it, and look up that cell. If the cell is empty, claim the next blank left-hand page and write its number there. Unrelated notes sharing a key end up side by side.

Eighteenth-century buyers could purchase this structure ready-made, as in *Bell's Common-Place Book* (1770). Many collectors ignored it, keeping books arranged by sequence, taste, and later regret.

Try it in your workbook this week. Then ask what your search bar, your tags, and your AI summaries decide for you.

## Essay

### A pamphlet that is its own example

In 1706 the London bookseller J. Greenwood, "at the End of Cornhill next Stocks-Market," issued a small octavo titled *A New Method of Making Common-Place-Books*, "written by the late learned Mr. John Lock" and "translated from the French." The copy digitized from the Wellcome Collection has sixty numbered pages. After a dedication to a young Edward Northey of Hackney and a short essay of advice by Jean Le Clerc (pp. i–v), the reader meets a double-page grid printed in red and black and headed *The Index*. Locke's letter to his friend Nicolas Toinard follows (pp. 2–24). The rest of the volume reprints John Wallis's letters on teaching deaf people to speak and write, added by the bookseller for their public usefulness rather than any connection to note-taking ([Open Library record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL26873677M/A_new_method_of_making_common-place-books); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/b30540434)).

The pamphlet does not only describe a commonplace book; it is typeset as one. The instructions run beside the marginal head *Adversariorum Methodus*. A passage on the Ebionites breaks off at the foot of p. 3 and resumes on p. 14, whose page number carries a small "3" pointing back. Four pages, 15, 17, 19 and 23, are blank. They are not mistakes. They are the method holding space open.

### Where the text came from

Locke tells Toinard he had used the method for "above Twenty Years," and a few pages later for "Five and Twenty." Its first printed form was an anonymous article, "Méthode nouvelle de dresser des recueils," in the second volume of Le Clerc's *Bibliothèque universelle et historique*, the volume for 1686, pp. 315–340 ([Yeo 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bewi.201900024); [Stolberg 2014](https://doi.org/10.1163/15733823-00195p04)). Popular accounts often date it 1685; catalogers note that the article grew out of a Latin letter to Toinard written that year ([Open Library record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL26873677M/A_new_method_of_making_common-place-books)). Three manuscript drafts survive, including an English draft published by G. G. Meynell in 1993 ([Stolberg 2014](https://doi.org/10.1163/15733823-00195p04)).

Two English versions appeared in 1706, two years after Locke's death: Greenwood's pamphlet, and "A New Method of a Common-Place-Book" in the Churchills' *Posthumous Works of Mr. John Locke*, reprinted in the 1714 *Works*. The Clarendon Edition (2019) collates the French text with those two. The translations are not interchangeable. The Churchill text keeps Locke's Latin example head, EPISTOLA; Greenwood renders it "Epistle," quietly abandoning Locke's own advice to write heads in Latin. The method then travelled without Locke's name on the title page. Ephraim Chambers reprinted the grid in the "Common-Place-Book" entry of his *Cyclopaedia* (2nd ed., 1738) ([Walker 2001](https://www.liverpooluniversitypress.co.uk/doi/pdf/10.3828/indexer.2001.22.3.2)), and in 1770 John Bell sold blank books "Formed generally upon the Principles Recommended and Practised by Mr Locke." Lucia Dacome has traced how widely the letter–vowel rule was taken up by eighteenth-century readers, students, and observers ([Dacome 2004](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/184066)).

### The problem: placing before knowing

Material arrives in the order you happen to read it, not by subject. Assign pages to subjects in advance and you guess wrong: some sections overflow, others stay empty. Write straight through and the book becomes a heap that can be searched only by rereading.

Locke's answer separates *where an entry is written* from *how it is found*. Space is allocated on demand, when a kind of head first appears. Finding runs through a fixed address computed from the head word itself. So the index never grows: two pages serve, in Greenwood's words, "the whole Volume, be it of what Size soever" (p. 4).

### The method, carefully (Greenwood 1706, pp. 4–12)

Rule the first two facing pages into twenty-five equal horizontal bands, inking every fifth line, and cross them with vertical lines. Each group of five bands takes one letter; each band within the group takes a vowel, *a e i o u*, in order. That makes one hundred cells. Locke uses twenty letters. He drops K, W and Y, whose work C, U and I can do, and lodges Q, which is always followed by *u*, in the *u* band of Z. The count reaches twenty only if I/J and U/V each count as one letter, as they commonly did in the period's alphabets. Then rule an inch-wide margin on every other page.

To make an entry, choose a head, "some Considerable Word that is Essential to the Matter." Take its first letter and the vowel that follows it. *Epistola* gives E-i; *Adversaria* gives A-e. A one-syllable head that begins with a vowel uses that vowel twice, so *Art* goes under A-a. Locke prefers Latin heads in the nominative, so that shifting case endings never move a word to another cell, though any language will do if you never mix languages.

Look up the cell. If it holds a page number, write your entry on that page. If it is empty, find the first blank left-hand page (verso), write its number in the cell, set the head in large letters in the margin, and write the entry beside it. That verso and the right-hand page facing it now belong to the whole class. Every later head whose key is E-i (Locke lists Epicurus, Ebionites, Epigram, Edict) goes on that opening, whatever its subject. Starting on a verso keeps a whole class visible without turning a leaf.

When an opening fills, look for the next blank verso. If it is the very next page, write "V." (*verte*, turn over) at the foot of the full page and at the head of the next, and leave the index alone. If other classes have taken the intervening pages, write the new page number at the foot of the full page, repeat the head at the top of the new verso with the number of the page it continues, and add the new number to the index cell. These reciprocal numbers let you read a scattered class as if it were continuous, which is why p. 14 of the pamphlet points back to p. 3. When every verso is used, the right-hand pages still blank may be given to new classes; a marginal note cites the pamphlet's empty pp. 15, 17 and 19 as examples.

Two refinements complete the system. A finer index is possible, since adding a second vowel yields five hundred classes, but Locke preferred the coarse one and advised separate books instead: one for moral philosophy, one for natural philosophy, and perhaps a third for the "Knowledge of Signs." Sources get their own economy. Before extracting from a book, enter its author, title, edition, place, date, and total number of pages; then cite each passage as a fraction. An entry on Acherusia, a passage of Diodorus that Locke read in Marsham's *Canon Chronicus* (London, 1672), is marked 259 over 626, so that the Rule of Three will bring a reader within a few pages of the same passage in any other edition (pp. 10–12).

### What the method costs, and what it buys

In computing terms, Locke built a hash table with chaining. The letter–vowel pair is a cheap hash, the opening is a bucket, and collisions are resolved by placing different heads side by side. The cost shows on the page: an opening mixes unrelated matter, so that a gospel variant used by the Ebionites shares space with the letter that introduces the method. Retrieval takes two steps, index to opening and then eye down the margin, which is why Locke insists that the margin hold nothing but heads.

The deeper cost is that the head is chosen once, at entry, in ink. Le Clerc's preface pushes judgment earlier still. Copy only what is "Choice and Excellent," he advises; do not break off reading to copy; mark passages on a slip of paper and extract them after a second reading (pp. ii–v). He expects a beginner's extracts to be despised once judgment ripens, yet insists they teach "the Art of putting Things in Order." A head, in other words, is a decision about what a passage is *for*, made before you know what you will need. Headings train attention. Once you own a cell for *Light*, you begin to read for light.

### Two eighteenth-century books against the instructions

**Purchased structure: Bell's Common-Place Book and Erasmus Darwin.** Bell's 1770 product sold Locke's authority as a finished object. The copy Erasmus Darwin used from 1776 to 1787, owned by English Heritage and shown at Erasmus Darwin House in Lichfield, is a vellum-bound quarto of about three hundred sheets. Its title sets each line in a different typeface, and printed hints and lettered index pages precede the blank leaves. Darwin made 136 index entries and filled 160 pages with medical records, meteorology, and drawings and notes for inventions such as an artificial bird and a canal lift ([Revolutionary Players](https://www.revolutionaryplayers.org.uk/the-scope-and-nature-of-darwins-commonplace-book/)). By one count, sixty-three pages concern inventions and sixty medicine; Darwin numbered the pages himself and skipped 134 ([Erasmus Darwin House](https://www.erasmusdarwin.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Commonplace-Book.pdf)). After his death his widow added family history, and later Darwins added more.

Does it follow Locke? The publisher's frame does. Whether Darwin worked the letter–vowel cells, gave each class a facing opening, or kept up the reciprocal page numbers cannot be settled from the published descriptions. Topics that recur, such as the artificial bird on pp. 32 and 38, fit Locke's continuations, but they also fit a man simply returning to an idea. Even the product is described inconsistently: a dealer counts eight printed index pages in a folio copy; popular accounts call them instructions ([Antipodean Books](https://www.antipodean.com/pages/books/21917/bell-s-common-place-book-formd-generally-upon-the-principles-recommended-and-practised-by-mr-locke?soldItem=true); [History of Information](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=3291)). The verdict: Locke's name was bought; Locke's method is unproven until someone reads the index pages. Bell supplied the grid, but the pagination on which the whole system depends was Darwin's own labor, and his slip is still there.

**No Lockean method at all: A.T.W.'s miscellany (Folger MS M.a.182, begun 1793).** A compiler who signed "A.T.W." (probably, Betty Schellenberg suggests, a woman of the Wharton family) pasted in a title page announcing "A miscellaneous collection in prose and verse in which are included several original pieces 1793." The first entry is an allegory written at fifteen, later annotated by its older author as excusable only because a fond father would not let it be corrected. Poems by Johnson, Hester Thrale Piozzi, Garrick, Mehetabel Wright, the Della Cruscans and Burns follow, with anecdotes and quotations, and one poem was written for the collection by a male Wharton. A courtly love poem is crossed out with the note "grand nonsense, scratch it out," and an epitaph's "great, the good Rousseau" becomes "the Infidel Rousseau" ([Schellenberg 2020](https://www.folger.edu/blogs/collation/manuscript-verse-miscellany/)).

Nothing in the published description suggests heads or a Lockean index, and an index alone would not prove one. Schellenberg argues that such books, though often catalogued as commonplace books, are a different kind: whole poems rather than excerpts under heads, usually in one hand, designed as a coherent object. A.T.W.'s book is organized by sequence, title page, and revision; it is authored. Yet it borrows from every neighboring form: quotations a commonplacer would have filed, a contribution solicited from a friend as an album would solicit it, and a second, retrospective voice that reads like a diary kept against its younger self.

A third object sharpens the point. Houghton Library's *Collection of Various Kinds of Poetry* (MS Eng 768, 1749–50) claims on its title page to be the work of Melesinda Munbee, aged five and a half, for her father. Susan Halpert, who researched it, found the hand too practiced for a child and the poems, copied whole from printed anthologies, sometimes far from suitable for one ([Halpert 2017](https://sites.harvard.edu/houghton-library/2017/08/07/a-curious-manuscript)). Whoever held the pen, its choices describe a household more than a girl.

### Names for things that will not stay put

The categories are useful precisely because objects cross them. A commonplace book gathers excerpts under heads for later retrieval and reuse; its order is topical and its purpose is future writing. A diary is ordered by date and records the keeper's days. A recipe or receipt book gathers procedures for cookery, medicine, and household management, and often passes through several hands and generations. A miscellany, in Schellenberg's sense, copies whole pieces in a designed sequence as an aesthetic object. An album, a form that flourishes later, collects contributions written by others in their own hands. Darwin's book is a commonplace book that becomes a laboratory notebook and then a family record. A.T.W.'s is a miscellany carrying a commonplacer's quotations, an album's gift, and a diarist's hindsight. Classification should describe use, not police it.

### Copying, composing, and the compiler's portrait

Is copying composition? For her compilers, Schellenberg's answer is yes. Copying by hand was slow, physical work, and selection, juxtaposition, and arrangement were how socially obscure readers made the period's poetry their own. Robert Darnton made a stronger claim for earlier commonplacers: that breaking texts into fragments and reassembling them under heads was itself a way of thinking ([Darnton, via History of Information](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=3291)). Locke's grid is indifferent to meaning, but the person choosing heads is not, and a selection of other people's words records what its compiler noticed, valued, and later disowned.

The material evidence matters as much as the words: Munbee's faint hand-ruled lines; the 192 pages a 1720s compiler numbered in Folger MS M.a.174, with a preface added last, outside the pagination; Darwin's missing page 134; A.T.W.'s cancellations; Greenwood's designed blanks. Blank space in these books is never neutral. It is reserved, as in Locke, or abandoned, or left for someone else.

### An invitation, and a comparison

For your Week 5 workbook, keep a Locke book for three weeks. Use a bound notebook, number every page, rule the two-page index, and give every entry a single head chosen at the moment you write. Cite sources Locke's way, adapted to media: a timecode over total runtime (12:40 / 92:00) works the way his 259 over 626 did. Then open it at random and read one opening as a collage made by the alphabet.

Then compare it with your digital tools. Full-text search defers the decision Locke forces: you need not name a thing to find it later, and you can find words you never thought of as heads, but only words that are actually there. Tags allow many heads per item and can be revised; Locke allows one head per entry, in ink, and can only add. Neither search nor tags will put *Light* beside *Listening* because of a spelling accident. AI summaries and semantic search change the most. They retrieve by meaning rather than by letters, across material you never read; researchers are now testing such search across an eighteenth-century corpus of more than thirty-two million pages to trace Locke's own reception ([arXiv preprint, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.09236)). They will also propose heads for you. What they cannot do is make the selection yours. A summary records a model's attention; a commonplace book records yours, mistakes included. Use both, and notice which one you reread.

## Worked example: a Locke index for a short documentary

*Hypothetical, for demonstration. A student is gathering material for a short documentary about a city river. Heads are in English (Locke's rule only requires that you not mix languages). The index occupies the first opening; pages after it are numbered so that the first left-hand page is 2, as in Greenwood's printing.*

**Entries, in the order they arrive**

| # | Entry (what arrived) | Head chosen | Key (first letter + next vowel) | Index cell before | Action | Index cell after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Note on golden-hour exposure from a cinematography manual | Light | L-i | empty | First blank verso is p. 2. Write head in margin of p. 2; pp. 2–3 now belong to L-i. | L-i: 2 |
| 2 | Quotation on cutting from an editor's interview | Montage | M-o | empty | Next blank verso is p. 4; pp. 4–5 go to M-o. | M-o: 4 |
| 3 | Field-recording note: the weir is loudest at dawn | Listening | L-i | 2 | Cell already has a number. Write on p. 2 under a new margin head, below *Light*. | L-i: 2 (unchanged) |
| 4 | City archive photo, 1911 flood | Archive | A-i (A, then first following vowel *i*) | empty | Next blank verso is p. 6. | A-i: 6 |
| 5 | Idea for the film's overall shape | Arc | A-a (one syllable beginning with a vowel) | empty | Next blank verso is p. 8. | A-a: 8 |
| 6 | Note on a hand-cranked viewer at the museum | Kinetoscope | C-i (Locke drops K; C stands in) | empty | Next blank verso is p. 10. | C-i: 10 |
| 7 | More *Light* and *Listening* notes fill pp. 2–3 | (continuation) | L-i | 2 | Next verso, p. 4, belongs to M-o, and pp. 6, 8, 10 are taken. First blank verso is p. 12. Write "12" at the foot of p. 3; at the top of p. 12 write "3" and repeat the head. Add 12 to the index. | L-i: 2. 12 |
| 8 | L-i pages 12–13 fill | (continuation) | L-i | 2. 12 | The very next verso, p. 14, is blank. Write "V." at the foot of p. 13 and the top of p. 14. Index unchanged. | L-i: 2. 12 |

**What the example shows.** *Light* and *Listening* share pages because of spelling, not meaning. That is the cost of a two-page index and, in a creative workbook, a possible source of unplanned juxtaposition. Finding *Listening* later takes two steps: the L-i cell sends you to pp. 2 and 12, and you scan the margins.
