# Ancient notes — what happens on the return?

**Central question:** When ancient Mediterranean writers saved someone else's words, what made those words become a new composition, a resource for memory, or a way of living?

The consequential action is often the return. A passage first copied out can subsequently be retrieved, compared, rehearsed, rejected, or rewritten for another person. These operations produce different kinds of authority. A collection can offer information; an argument must establish relations between selected items; an ethical exercise asks its reader to act differently. Treating all three as “keeping a notebook” makes their histories look more uniform than the evidence allows.

This report concentrates on Greek and Latin evidence from the Roman imperial world, with an earlier Greek visual comparison. It extends the local `ancient-notebooks/` essays by checking passages afresh and giving greater weight to disagreements between practices. It is not a comprehensive account of the ancient Mediterranean, which would also require substantial work on Egyptian, Jewish, administrative, commercial, and other traditions. The witnesses here are unusually literary, and often unusually privileged.

Four cases establish the argument: Pliny's excerpting, Seneca's assimilation, Quintilian's revision, and Philodemus's materially surviving draft. Plutarch supplies a particularly explicit, though incomplete, note-to-composition chain. Gellius provides an alternative outcome: a published miscellany can retain diversity rather than fuse everything into a continuous argument.

## Pliny: accumulation is organized labor

Pliny the Younger's *Letters* 3.5 answers a request for a list of his uncle's works by describing the elder Pliny's habits. Books were read aloud; he selected extracts; a shorthand writer accompanied him on journeys. The nephew reports inheriting 160 densely written collections of selections, using both sides of their writing surfaces. He connects these habits to the uncle's extensive output. This is testimony about a workflow, transmitted in a crafted commemorative letter, rather than a surviving workshop record. [Pliny, *Letters* 3.5](https://www.attalus.org/pliny/ep3.html#5).

The old Firth translation calls these “commonplace books” and specifies scrolls. Neither expression should silently become an archaeological description. The Latin phrase *electorum commentarios* names collections of selected material; *opisthographos* indicates writing on both sides. It does not establish a modern bound notebook with headings, an alphabetical index, or detachable entries. The containers and retrieval scheme remain uncertain. [Latin text, 3.5.17](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pliny.ep3.html).

Pliny's own *Natural History* preface gives complementary evidence. At §17 he claims an immense reading base; at §§21–23 he presents acknowledgment of source authors as an obligation; at §33 he explains his contents list as a way for readers to find particular matters without reading everything. These are authorial claims about scope, attribution, and access, not independently audited statistics. [*Natural History*, preface](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/praefatio*.html).

My inference is that the finished compilation changes the audience's relation to the accumulated material. Selection initially serves the collector's future work. A contents list and named authorities make the result useful to readers who were absent from that work. An essay makes a comparable change when it turns private reminders into evidence that another person can locate and assess. A useful passage is not yet a usable citation, and neither is automatically an argument.

The social question follows immediately: whose time does “the author's productivity” measure? The reading voice and recording hand matter even when the title names one author. This letter does not give the legal status or names of every assistant, and those details should not be supplied by assumption. It nevertheless makes a solitary-writer reconstruction untenable. Selection, vocal delivery, transcription, and final responsibility need not belong to the same person.

## Seneca: reading must undergo a change

Seneca's Letter 84 recommends alternating reading and writing. His bees collect from different flowers, keep gathered material distinct, and turn it into something with a unified character. Digestion offers a second image: remembered material must become the reader's own intellectual capacity. These are prescriptions for assimilation, expressed in an ethical letter, not observations of a universal ancient note system. No particular notebook artifact is described. [Seneca, *Letters* 84.1–8, Gummere translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_84).

This gives a sharper question than how many highlights someone has saved: what can the reader now judge or do? My interpretation is that successful incorporation has a double movement. First, keep enough difference between sources to recognize what each contributes. Then construct a relation that none of those sources supplies individually. The latter operation cannot be measured simply by how thoroughly borrowed wording has been paraphrased. A smooth restatement might still reproduce somebody else's conceptual sequence without testing it.

There is also a productive tension with Pliny. Assimilating a source and making its origin available to a reader are different requirements. Contemporary research writing benefits from both: the argument should have its own organization, while its evidence remains traceable. Seneca's literary ideal does not authorize a modern researcher to erase provenance. We can borrow the demand for transformation without borrowing every implication of the metaphor.

Michel Foucault's 1983 “Self Writing” interprets collections of already existing sayings as instruments for forming an ethical self. His account connects writing with rereading and practice, and distinguishes such collections from a search for hidden inner thoughts. This is an influential twentieth-century arrangement of ancient evidence. It should be read alongside the particular texts it brings together. [Foucault, “Self Writing”](https://foucault.info/documents/foucault.hypomnemata.en/).

For a studio, its strongest invitation is to ask what a saved passage changes beyond the page. Its historical limitation is the temptation to imagine *hypomnemata* as a standardized device with a single function. A functional interpretation is not a manufacturing specification. Seneca writes Latin; applying the Greek label to him is already an interpretive move.

## Quintilian: the writing surface helps determine the revision

In *Institutio oratoria* 10.3.19–22, Quintilian objects that dictating before an amanuensis can make a writer reluctant to pause or alter the wording. At 10.3.31–33 he prefers wax for easy erasure, allows parchment for weak eyesight, and recommends room for additions and thoughts arriving out of sequence. His advice concerns composition and training an orator. It should not be generalized into a survey of everyone's daily habits. [Quintilian, 10.3](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Quintilian/Institutio_Oratoria/10B*.html#3).

This is unusually precise evidence about the encounter between a body, a surface, and a developing text. My inference is that empty space participates in thinking: an addition that has somewhere to go is easier to make than one that threatens the legibility of the whole. The room reserved for an irrelevant but promising thought also separates two tasks. One can preserve a possibility without forcing it into the current argument.

The dictation passage deserves equal attention. A recording assistant is a social presence, not merely an extra writing speed. Hesitation may feel different when another person waits. That observation suggests a present-day experiment with speech transcription, but it does not prove that people experience software as they experienced an ancient attendant. The useful comparison concerns the pressure to keep producing language; the relationships and conditions of labor differ.

Quintilian and Pliny therefore resist a single productivity doctrine. Continuous collection can serve one kind of work; deliberate resistance to fluency can serve another. Before adapting an ancient method, ask what output it was intended to support. Notes for a factual compilation and a speech that must persuade a particular audience do not face identical tests of success.

## Philodemus: a draft that retains its alternatives

PHerc. 1691/1021, associated with Philodemus's *History of the Academy*, offers a rare material check on literary descriptions of working texts. Tournié and colleagues describe a preliminary draft with marginal additions, transposition signs, poetic excerpts and prose paraphrases, and supplementary columns on the reverse. They regard identification as Philodemus's actual working manuscript as probable. Some remains of a final version survive as PHerc. 164. Their 2019 imaging study recovered portions of previously concealed reverse-side writing. [Tournié et al., *Science Advances* 5, eaav8936](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6777967/).

The important distinction is between an observed feature and a reconstructed operation. Added writing outside the main columns can be documented. Its intended insertion into a later text is an interpretation supported by the manuscript's structure. The claim that a particular person marked a source for a particular scribe to copy requires another evidentiary step. It is not guaranteed by the presence of a quotation.

A smaller example shows why layout matters. Fleischer's analysis of the Philo passage identifies an intercolumnar supplement that earlier editors partly treated as main-line text. The suggested relation between that supplement and removal of a duplicated report remains tentative. [Fleischer, “New evidence on the death of Philo,” author manuscript, pp. 3–5](https://www.iliesi.cnr.it/philodemus/documenti/NEW_EVIDENCE_ON_THE_DEATH_OF_PHILO_OF_LA.PDF).

My inference is that a draft preserves decisions in a way the finished prose often suppresses. It can display multiple destinations for the same material, conflicts between versions, and additions waiting for integration. This makes the manuscript especially useful for a lesson about essay writing: revision is not merely polishing sentences after an argument exists. It can determine what the argument contains and where its parts belong. Yet this object does not reveal the complete original sequence of operations, much less the whole working life behind it.

## A documented chain, with the missing links exposed

Plutarch opens *On Tranquillity of Mind* by explaining that Paccius requested a discourse and a departing friend created a deadline. He says he gathered observations previously made for his own use from his *hypomnemata*. The surviving discourse then develops advice through examples, quotations, comparisons, and reasoning. This unusually explicit statement establishes reuse for an addressee; it does not preserve the earlier notes. [Plutarch, 464E–465C](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/De_tranquillitate_animi*.html).

The chain can be described without filling that gap:

| Stage | Evidence and limit |
|---|---|
| Source encounters | The finished discourse names earlier writers and uses quotations. Which entries entered the notes first is unknown. |
| Notes | The opening reports a preexisting collection for personal use. Its physical format does not survive here. |
| Selection | The author reports gathering material appropriate to the requested subject. No selection marks survive. |
| Arrangement | The transmitted discourse has a sequence. Whether it reproduces or changes the collection's order is unknown. |
| Output | A discourse addressed to a specific person survives in the textual tradition. Its opening frames usefulness as the purpose. |

This is a partially evidenced historical chain, not a manuscript-to-manuscript genetic demonstration. It suggests a practical principle: an audience and a need can give old notes a new organization. A research essay similarly becomes possible when the writer can say what problem these selections jointly address. The problem may emerge after collection, but the subsequent arrangement must answer it.

Gellius prevents that principle from becoming a rule that every collection should become an essay. His *Attic Nights* preface describes selective jottings from reading and hearing, retained for future retrieval, and claims that the work preserves their varied order. He also supplies summaries to make material findable. [Gellius, preface §§2–3, 11–12, 25](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Gellius/Praefatio*.html). A miscellany can organize access without enforcing a single thesis. Its arrangement need not be mistaken for a failed continuous argument.

## What “notebook” obscures

The cases concern multiple things: a collection described by another writer, a metaphor for intellectual transformation, directions for drafting, an actual worked-on roll, and the reported reuse of personal observations. Their coexistence is more informative than a linear history leading toward today's apps.

Matthew Larsen and Mark Letteney distinguish material formats from ancient judgments about a text's purpose, finish, and authorship. Their discussion includes *hypomnemata*, *commentarii*, social status, and Lucian's distinction between preparatory records and historical composition. It offers useful terminology while also advancing a particular, larger theory of early gospel textuality that this report does not establish. [Larsen and Letteney, “Christians and the Codex,” especially pp. 395–400](https://history.washington.edu/sites/history/files/documents/research/Larsen%20and%20Letteney%20-%202019%20-%20Christians%20and%20the%20Codex%20Generic%20Materiality%20and%20Early%20Gosp.pdf).

Surviving tablets complicate the assumption that a principal's name identifies the writing hand. The edition of the Vindolanda birthday invitation, Tab. Vindol. 291, identifies a main hand and Claudia Severa's personal closing. It is a wooden ink tablet, now British Museum 1986,1001.64. This directly supports shared inscription of a letter; it does not disclose an excerpting practice or securely establish the main writer's legal status. [Roman Inscriptions of Britain, object record and commentary](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/TabVindol291).

The lesson for research is to record roles as carefully as sources. Who located a passage? Who selected it? Who physically recorded it? Who supplied the interpretation? Who could revise the output? Evidence of assistance should reopen those questions even when it cannot answer all of them. Conversely, an unnamed hand should not automatically be assigned either servile status or modern coauthorship. The unequal social world of ancient writing requires investigation, rather than a flattering story of either solitary genius or frictionless collaboration.

| Plutarch | Select for a recipient | Practical discourse | Author's opening statement | Preexisting collection |

## Two feasible week 5 experiments

**1. One source pack, two returns — 50 minutes.** Give pairs three short, cited passages that disagree about a practical problem. Each person records six brief notes, distinguishing quotation, paraphrase, and inference. One makes a retrievable miscellany of independent entries; the other writes a 250-word argument for a named audience. Exchange outputs and trace every consequential assertion back to its source. Record what was omitted, reordered, and added. The contrast tests whether useful storage and persuasive synthesis require different arrangements. It is a modern reconstruction inspired by the cases, not a reproduction of an attested ancient assignment.

**2. Room to revise, room to hesitate — 35 minutes plus a next-day return.** Draft the same kind of 150-word explanation in two rounds: by hand on a sheet with a generous blank column, and through speech transcription. Counterbalance the order across participants. Save both first versions; revise each the next day for ten minutes. Mark added claims, deleted claims, reordered passages, and factual corrections. Compare how the medium affected hesitation and recovery of out-of-sequence ideas. Participants should describe their experience rather than rank handwriting as inherently superior. Modern recognition errors and motor or accessibility differences are part of the observation.

## Three artifact and source leads

1. **PHerc. 1691/1021, Officina dei Papiri Ercolanesi, Biblioteca Nazionale di Napoli.** Begin with the “Papyrus roll” and “Marginal annotations” sections and Figures 2–3 of the [2019 imaging article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6777967/). Compare its wording of probability with the features actually identified. The article text and captions were accessed; the images were not visually inspected for this report. A later object-level study should inspect images and current editions together.
2. **Tab. Vindol. 291; British Museum 1986,1001.64; inventory 85.057.** The [digital edition](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/TabVindol291) marks the second hand in the closing. Use the diplomatic text and commentary together to study how an editor distinguishes authorial responsibility from inscription. The record and text were accessed, not a physical tablet or high-resolution photograph.
3. **Metropolitan Museum, 17.230.10, terracotta kylix, approximately 460 BCE.** The [museum record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/250548) identifies a schoolboy carrying writing tablets, with rolls elsewhere in the decoration. This is a representation of materials in an educational setting, not a surviving notebook. The catalog identifies the images as public domain. The record was accessed; a visual inspection remains a separate next step.

## Uncertainties and further research

- A true source → notes → successive drafts → finished output comparison remains incomplete. Philodemus is the strongest route, but it requires specialist editions and scrutiny of individual corresponding passages, not just a general description of drafting.
- Pliny's selection containers, retrieval procedures, and assistants' exact contributions remain unresolved. An English translation's confident vocabulary is insufficient evidence.
- The local essay's British Library school tablet, Add MS 34186, is a promising lead, but the catalog and story pages could not be opened successfully during this run. Its details are therefore not used as verified evidence here.
- P.Oxy. IV 724 is an important lead for an enslaved shorthand apprentice and training conditions. The document frontend yielded no usable edition and the source endpoint presented an access challenge. Inspect the original publication or an accessible scholarly edition before using detailed contract claims.
- Literary self-description can advertise diligence, judgment, refinement, or helpfulness. Neither a preface nor a commemorative letter is a neutral time-and-motion record.
- These sources cannot establish how typical such practices were among women, nonelite readers, or people outside the Greek and Latin literary networks represented here. A broader study should seek documentary archives and schooling evidence without forcing their purposes into literary composition.
