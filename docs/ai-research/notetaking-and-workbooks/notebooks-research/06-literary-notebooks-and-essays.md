# Literary notebooks and essays: where fragments become arguments

## The question: what changes between recording and writing?

An essay needs something a notebook entry can usually leave unresolved: a reason for another person to follow its movement. A reading extract may preserve an attractive sentence; an essay must decide what that sentence establishes. An observation may summon a private memory; an essay must give the reader enough of a world to enter it. **The important transformation is the construction of relationships among fragments, sources, a speaking voice, and an audience.** Capture supplies possibilities. Rereading, selection, arrangement, and revision make those possibilities consequential.

This report follows four working environments rather than four versions of a universal method: Montaigne's annotated printed *Essais*; the circulating manuscripts of Mary Moody Emerson and the journals of her nephew Ralph Waldo Emerson; Virginia Woolf's reading notes and article sketches; and Joan Didion's account of notebook memory. Woolf provides the closest inspected source-to-essay dossier. The other cases test its limits: revision can follow publication, a notebook can already address readers, and a private fragment can become an essay without becoming reliable evidence about an external event.

Genetic criticism gives us a useful discipline for studying these changes. Pierre-Marc de Biasi distinguishes documentary research, outlining, composition, and preparation for publication, while allowing that their functions overlap. His distinction between a documented external source and a merely probable influence is especially valuable here. A resemblance is a research lead; a located reading note supplies a different kind of evidence. His model also accommodates reversals and abandonment, rather than assuming that each page advances toward the book eventually published. [De Biasi, “What is a Literary Draft?”](https://www.item.ens.fr/articles-en-ligne/?idtexte=what-is-a-literary-draft-towards-a-functional-typology-of-ge&pagename=articles-en-ligne).

That changes our unit of research. “Woolf used notebooks” is a starting observation. “This identified source is compressed on this page, placed beside these other readings, and recast in this article sketch” is an investigable process. We should also ask what the final essay excludes. A surviving notebook's abandoned possibilities can tell us more about composition than an anthology of its most polished sentences.

## 1. Montaigne: the printed essay becomes a working surface

The Bordeaux Copy is a copy of the 1588 *Essais*, extensively corrected and enlarged in handwriting during Montaigne's remaining years. It is a hybrid object: printed prose supplies the existing structure, while margins and interlinear spaces reopen that structure. Additions, substitutions, deletions, and punctuation changes make revision physically visible. The Chicago Montaigne project's description is therefore evidence for an author working on already published essays, rather than evidence for a lost notebook from which the original essays supposedly emerged. [Montaigne Studies, Bordeaux Copy](https://montaignestudies.uchicago.edu/h/bordeaux_copy/).

This reverses the familiar capture-to-output diagram. The output becomes a source for further writing. A later thought has to locate itself against an earlier passage; the printed page makes the writer's previous commitments available for interrogation. My inference is that this arrangement can make an essay accumulate disagreement with itself: the new fragment need not become a new chapter, but can alter the pressure of an existing argument.

The material organization was consequential. Bordeaux's nomination dossier identifies insertion signs connecting marginal additions to their intended positions. It also records additions in Marie de Gournay's hand, including at folio 42v. Her participation prevents us from equating every act of inscription with the named author's hand. The same dossier distinguishes the annotated copy from the posthumous 1595 edition: the latter's differences indicate another, now lost, copy in the printing history. We cannot draw a simple arrow from the surviving Bordeaux object to every word printed in 1595. [Bordeaux Library's UNESCO dossier, §§6.3 and 6.5.2–6.5.3](https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/21_70%252B.pdf).

The MONLOE edition makes a further distinction legible: handwritten material can itself be rejected. Its editors discuss a crossed-out comment in “Sur des vers de Virgile,” at their locator III.v, f.390r = 398r, which did not appear in the 1595 edition. Their display separates additions, substitutions, cancellations, and editorial restorations. For research, that separation matters more than a smooth modern transcription. An addition proves that a possibility was written; a cancellation prevents us from automatically counting it among the author's published positions. [MONLOE's editorial account](https://montaigne.univ-tours.fr/essais-1588-exemplaire-bordeaux/).

Montaigne consequently supplies a practical possibility for week 5: notes can attach to an existing argument at the point where it needs complication. The margin is a location for renewed thought, not merely a storage space for quotations.

## 2. Mary Moody and Ralph Waldo Emerson: manuscripts circulate, authorship changes

Mary Moody Emerson's *Almanacks*, approximately 1804–1855, combine reading extracts, spiritual reflection, original writing, and correspondence. Noelle Baker and Sandra Petrulionis describe handmade fascicles of letter paper bound with thread, portable enough to circulate among readers. Mary's intellectual work belongs in the account in its own right; it was not simply preparatory material awaiting her nephew's literary use. The editors also describe her household obligations and constrained resources. The conditions under which someone can read and write belong to notebook history. [*Almanacks*, general introduction](https://wwp.northeastern.edu/research/projects/manuscripts/emerson/general_introduction.html).

The editors' earlier conference paper identifies a more specific transmission practice: Waldo excerpted Mary's letters and *Almanacks* into his journals and eventually made indexed transcription notebooks. This is an arrangement across persons as well as across pages. Their account includes Mary's objections to his retaining her manuscripts and an instruction to respect folded pages. Circulation could coexist with restricted access. A manuscript offered to a correspondent was not therefore offered without limits to every future reader. [Baker and Petrulionis, “I defy tomorrow”](https://wwp.northeastern.edu/outreach/conference/wia2009/papers/baker_petrulionis.html).

The interpretive consequence is substantial. When a thought passes from Mary's manuscript into Waldo's notebook, and then into a work bearing his name, it can acquire a different speaker and a different audience. We need to track attribution and framing along with wording. The general introduction identifies later essays and lectures, including “Fate,” as areas for cross-reference; this report has not inspected the corresponding leaves and drafts and therefore does not claim a particular sentence transfer into that essay.

Waldo's own journal-to-essay practice is well attested at the editorial level. The description of *Journals and Miscellaneous Notebooks*, volume 8, covering 1841–1843, identifies passages eventually incorporated into “Experience,” alongside early drafts of “Threnody,” in the period of his son's death. This establishes a promising dossier, not a license to treat every private expression of grief as an early version of the published essay. [Volume 8 edition record and description](https://books.google.com/books/about/Journals_and_Miscellaneous_Notebooks_184.html?id=POFDAAAAYAAJ).

There is another transformation after the author's death: selection for publication. Lawrence Rosenwald describes keeping original journal volumes distinct and preserving paragraphs in his Library of America selection, while cutting most early and late writing. A selected diary is therefore a shaped reading experience. My inference is that an edition favoring powerful paragraphs may make the working papers seem more consistently finished than the full archive. His account lets us study that shaping without mistaking the selected publication for the untouched journal. [Rosenwald, “Two Perspectives on Emerson's Journals”](https://cswr.hds.harvard.edu/news/2026/09/22/two-perspectives-emersons-journals).

## 3. Woolf: reading becomes an argument through comparison

WoolfNotes presents facsimiles alongside Brenda Silver's guide to 67 reading notebooks. Michèle Barrett explicitly warns that the site does not provide complete transcriptions. Its cataloguing also records bindings and rebinding. Thus the digital encounter has several layers: manuscript image, modern description, selected transcription, and interpretation. Silver's numbering is a scholarly access system, not a chronology we should silently attribute to Woolf. [Barrett's introduction](https://www.woolfnotes.com/introduction-to-the-reading-notebooks/), [Silver's account of making the guide](https://www.woolfnotes.com/background-silver-2023-talk/).

RN31, *Modern Novels (Joyce)*, is particularly useful because it contains source notes and a sketch for an essay within one material object. The catalogue places it in 1919, while describing the manuscript itself as unsigned and undated. It records use from both ends and identifies an associated typescript at Sussex, MH/B.3f. Those distinctions caution against assuming that all writing in the volume belongs to one project or that physical page order gives an exact writing sequence. [RN31 record](https://www.woolfnotes.com/notebook-display/?pdb=31).

On facsimile image 3, I inspected a marginal attribution to May Sinclair and *The Little Review*, compressed notes on reality and literary method, an episode/color list for *Ulysses*, and the start of Joyce notes. The blue page rule divides a margin used for sources and retrieval cues from the main writing area. On image 11, the heading “Sketches of article” introduces writing that puts authors into a comparative argument; image 12 questions approaching Joyce through his method. Crossings-out and marks remain visible. These are observations about the artifact, not claims to have deciphered every word. [RN31 facsimile, PDF images 3 and 11–12](https://www.woolfnotes.com/WN_DATA/WNimages/Woolfnotes_RN31-images.pdf).

What becomes visible is a change in intellectual scale. A note on one critic's argument joins notes on a novelist; the sketch asks how several writers represent life. The finished critical argument is not simply the extracted sentences in a new container. Comparison supplies a problem the sources separately did not answer. The dossier below follows that change while retaining the missing stages.

This is an interpretation of an evidenced relationship, not a claim that one sentence traveled unchanged through every stage. ## 4. Didion: the notebook returns a self to the writer

“On Keeping a Notebook” opens with Didion rereading an entry about a Wilmington hotel bar. The short record generates an expanded scene and a question about why she recorded it. Her account distinguishes a notebook's recovery of felt experience from an accurate diary. This is a published essay staging a return to notebook material; the entry as printed inside it is not an independently inspected raw page. [Didion's essay, Newberry teaching PDF, pp.1–3](https://www.newberry.org/uploads/files/OnKeepingaNotebook.pdf).

The essay matters for notes becoming writing because it makes rereading generative. A fragment does not merely retrieve a stored event; the writer gives it new narrative duration and significance. But Didion's own uncertainty about memory is part of the evidence. For a research essay, an emotionally convincing reconstruction cannot by itself verify what happened. We can learn from the compositional operation while retaining a separate standard for factual claims.

There are also notebooks that do not produce their originally intended article. The publisher describes *South and West* as extended excerpts from 1970s notebooks, including California notes begun for an assignment on the Patty Hearst trial that Didion did not write. A later publication of selected notes should not be retroactively turned into successful completion of that assignment. The notebook had a publishing afterlife, and the intended output remained absent. [Publisher's description](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/607609/sur-y-oeste--south-and-west-by-joan-didion/).

NYPL's collection description identifies distinct research, writing, and personal materials, and records Didion and John Gregory Dunne's exchanges of research and feedback. That is an archival lead for testing the solitary notebook image against collaborative work. The collection is MssCol 186276; its Series IV description identifies notebooks including work related to *Where I Was From*. I have read the catalogue, not those notebooks or a matched draft. [NYPL collection description](https://archives.nypl.org/admin/collections/11838).

## Comparing the transformations

| Working environment | What the surface enables | Relation to finished prose | Research risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Montaigne's annotated print | Insert, revise, or cancel at a specific passage | Publication becomes a base for more writing | Equating Bordeaux handwriting with the whole 1595 text |
| Mary and Waldo Emerson's manuscript exchange | Circulate, extract, index, repurpose | Another person's writing enters a new authorial frame | Erasing the original writer or ignoring access limits |
| Woolf's reading notebook and sketch | Attribute, compare, move from source response to article problem | A documented intermediate composition stage | Treating adjacency as exact chronology or influence as verbatim copying |
| Didion's reread fragment | Recover an earlier felt self and expand a scene | The essay interprets the act of keeping notes | Treating a printed recollection as raw evidence or factual verification |

These cases suggest a better practical question than how much to capture: **what operation will make a retained fragment answerable to a reader?** Sometimes that operation is comparison; sometimes contradiction, contextual expansion, or a change of speaker. The archive helps us recognize the operation only when we keep the source, the working page, and the edited output distinct.

## Two week 5 experiments

1. **Build a small genetic dossier, 60–90 minutes.** Give participants Sinclair's printed p.4, RN31 images 3 and 11–12, Silver's two-page RN31 guide, and the relevant published essay paragraph. Use four colors to distinguish quotation, paraphrase, interpretation, and uncertainty. Produce a 250-word account of what changed, followed by two missing pieces of evidence. Assess source fidelity and whether the account overstates the chain. This studies a documented practice; it does not require imitating Woolf's handwriting or guessing her mental state.

2. **Reopen a paragraph through its margins, 45 minutes.** Write a 300-word argument from a short source packet, then print it with generous margins. Add three notes at particular sentences: a counterexample, an alternative interpretation, and a source limitation. Rewrite to 400 words, retaining the first version and canceled additions. Exchange drafts and ask which marginal intervention changed the argument, which merely decorated it, and which was rightly discarded. This is a modern reconstruction inspired by annotated print, not a reproduction of Montaigne's historical workflow.

## Gaps and further research

- **The Woolf chain needs a collation.** The inspected sketch establishes an intermediate state, but it does not supply every revision. Which propositions changed between the 1919 essay and the 1925 collection? What reading note failed to enter either version?
- **Mary Emerson deserves her own dossier.** Match an *Almanack* passage to Waldo's transcription and one essay or poem. Record omissions, attribution, and both writers' framing. The scholarly accounts establish the exchange; they do not substitute for passage-level comparison.
- **Survival distorts the sample.** The *Almanacks* editors describe damaged manuscripts and later transcriptions; Montaigne's printing history includes a lost copy. Work that survived through a famous author's household is not representative of everyone who kept literary notes.
- **Genre names need auditing.** Raw manuscript, partial transcription, selected diary, published essay about notebooks, lifetime publication of notebook excerpts, and posthumous edited notebook are different evidence types. Publication date also differs from entry date and inferred composition date.
- **Unfinished work matters.** Didion's unrealized assignment suggests studying unsuccessful or redirected projects alongside famous finished essays. The research question becomes what a notebook made possible, rather than what proportion of it was efficiently consumed.
