# "I think": Notebook B and the shape of research before an argument

## Wall label (150 words)

In July 1837, nine months after the Beagle came home, Charles Darwin opened a small leather pocket notebook lettered B. Over about seven months he filled it with reading notes, questions for breeders and naturalists, arithmetic, reminders to himself, and a few quick sketches. On page 36 he wrote "I think," stopped, and drew. The branching figure that follows is now celebrated as an early tree of life. In 1837 it was a working test: could descent plus extinction produce the gaps naturalists saw between groups? Natural selection was still about a year away. The five pages on this wall show guesses, reversals, a warning to himself (*Cuidado*: careful), and stubs where Darwin later cut pages out for a book he never finished. None of it was meant for print. Look closely for what is unfinished. That is what your research pack is for. *Notebook B, Cambridge University Library, DAR 121.*

## Essay

### 1. A small book with a date problem

Notebook B is a 17 × 9.7 cm notebook in brown leather with a brass clasp; Darwin numbered its 280 pages himself (CUL DAR 121). Its dates come from Darwin, but after the fact. Inside the front cover he wrote that the book "was commenced about July, 1837," that page 235 "was written in January 1838," and that it "probably ended in beginning of February." His Journal, begun in August 1838, records: "In July opened first note Book on 'transmutation of Species'."

No individual entry is dated. Citations give some purchase: B30e refers to Loudon's *Magazine of Natural History* for July 1837, and B138 to a Gould paper from "September or Octob 1837." If Darwin filled the pages roughly in order (an assumption, not a fact), the "I think" page belongs to the second half of 1837, probably summer or early autumn, which is where historians usually place it. No document gives its day. The page is usually presented as a moment; it is better read as a place in a sequence.

### 2. The pages around "I think"

Fifteen pages before the famous sketch, Darwin writes: "Organized beings represent a tree irregularly branched … As many terminal buds dying as new ones generated" (B21). Two pages later the tree branches three ways, for "air, land & water" (B23). Then he doubts his own figure: "The tree of life should perhaps be called the coral of life, base of branches dead; so that passages cannot be seen" (B25). On B26 he draws two small figures, the notebook's first sketches.

At B36 a sentence stops after two words. Below "I think" a branching figure grows from a circled 1; some branches end in a short crossbar, others end bare. Beside it, in two enclosed blocks: "Case must be that one generation then should be as many living as now" and "To do this & to have many species in same genus (as is) requires extinction." Underneath: "Thus between A & B immense gap of relation. C & B the finest gradation, B & D rather greater distinction. Thus genera would be formed." On the next page he counts: if one ancient form "is capable of making 13 recent forms," then "Twelve of the contemporarys must have left no offspring at all" (B37). Paul Barrett's 1960 edition notes thirteen barred endings and twelve unbarred ones in the drawing.

Here the diagram does work a paragraph could not. Every line must end somewhere, so drawing forces a decision about every lineage: continue or stop. The crossbars make extinction countable, and the count is what yields "requires extinction." Yet the drawing commits to a shape without committing to a cause. Kees van Putten observes that B, C and D sit the same number of branchings from their common stem, yet Darwin grades their distances differently; the spacing on the page carries a meaning the branching does not, and the ambiguity is unresolved (van Putten 2020). A polished paragraph would have to choose. The sketch lets two readings coexist long enough to be useful.

The following pages show what the diagram did not settle. Darwin attributes extinction to "non-adaptation of circumstances" (B38–39), tries an analogy with twelve siblings whose lines die out over ten thousand years (B40–41), and then writes: "Heaven know whether this agrees with Nature: Cuidado" (B44). *Cuidado* is Spanish for "careful"; I suspect a word from the Beagle years. Whatever its source, it is a warning addressed to himself.

### 3. Four entries that complicate the discovery story

**The book opens on reproduction, not trees.** Page 1 is headed "ZOONOMIA," the title of his grandfather Erasmus Darwin's treatise, and begins "Two kinds of generation." The notebook's first theory is that sexual reproduction produces variation, adapting "the race to changing world" (B4), and that isolation then fixes differences: "Now Galapagos Tortoises, Mocking birds, Falkland Fox, Chiloe fox" (B7). Cambridge's catalogue notes that Darwin later rejected this account. A story that starts at the tree starts thirty-five pages late.

**The monad that dies, and then doesn't.** On B22 Darwin supposes that a "monad," the simple living particle from which a lineage descends, has a "definite existence": a fixed lifespan that would explain whole groups dying out. On B29e he writes, flatly, "Monad has not definite existence." On B35 he argues it through: if the monad had definite life, related animals "all die at one period which is not the case, ∴ monucule not definite life." Within thirteen pages he builds a mechanism and dismantles it. Nothing later rescued the idea. It is evidence of a willingness to test and discard, not a hidden road to 1859.

**Revisions on the line.** The transcription of B5 records Darwin striking a word: "beautiful law of intermarriages ~~separating~~ partaking of characters of both parents." On B74, beside "It is absurd to talk of one animal being higher than another," he trades "cerebral structure" against "intellectual faculties," and the 1960 and online editions print the alternatives in opposite order. The page records a decision; neither edition can say how long Darwin hesitated.

**The sentence that sounds like the answer.** B90 reads: "Whether every animal produces in course of ages ten thousand varieties (influenced itself perhaps by circumstances) & those alone preserved which are well adapted." To a modern ear that is natural selection, and Barrett's 1960 footnote claims that the "I think" pages already "to all intents and purposes" formulate it. I read both passages differently. B90 begins with "Whether": it is a question. And Notebook B lacks the pressure Darwin credited to reading Malthus in late September 1838, populations outrunning their means, which is why Cambridge describes Notebooks B and C as his thinking *before* natural selection. Barrett's footnote is a useful warning: editors, not only publicists, can close a question the page leaves open.

### 4. A notebook made of other people

Notebook B is less a monologue than a switchboard. Darwin reads Lyell, Lamarck, Cuvier, Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, von Buch, Humboldt, and judges: Swainson's opening chapter is "trash" (B92); Kirby "errs greatly" (B141). He records the specialists describing his Beagle collections and the breeders he questions: John Gould ("the beauty of species is their exactness," B171), Richard Owen ("Owen says nonsense," B163), George Waterhouse, the army surgeon and zoologist Andrew Smith, and his second cousin W. D. Fox on apple pippins and bloodhounds (B83, B175–76). He writes himself instructions: "Pray ask Dr Smith" (B83), "Examine good collection of insects with this in view" (B47), "Tabulate Mammalia on this principle" (B169). Transcribers note that he often opened a query with a reversed question mark, so the doubt arrives before the sentence.

Specimens appear as compressed arguments. The two South American rheas are "strong argument of possibility of such change; as we see them in space, so might they in time" (B16–17). Fossil and living armadillos produce a family joke: the "parent of all armadilloes might be brother to Megatherium.— uncle now dead" (B54).

The switchboard carries noise as well as signal. On B32–34, B71 and B179–82 Darwin records claims about race, several attributed to Smith, in the hierarchical language of the period. A research pack that preserves raw notes keeps these too. Cleaning them out would yield a more comfortable notebook and a less truthful one.

### 5. Following B36 into print

The only illustration in *On the Origin of Species* (1859) is a folding diagram placed between pages 116 and 117: lettered species at the base, lines climbing through horizontal bands that each stand for a thousand generations, most lineages ending, a few reaching the top. Darwin wrote that naturalists could hardly describe affinities "without the aid of a diagram" (1859, p. 431).

The evidence linking it to B36 is real but indirect. Both use letters to compare distances among surviving forms; both make extinction structural rather than accidental. Van Putten traces seventeen pre-1859 sketches in Darwin's papers, among them an 1843 sheet captioned "a tree not good simile—endless piece of seaweed dividing" (DAR 205.5.90v), and argues that the series culminates in the published figure. Others read the lineage differently: Horst Bredekamp sees coral rather than tree throughout; J. David Archibald places B36 in a long tradition of visual metaphors for natural order. No document has Darwin pointing from the 1859 diagram back to B36; the connection is a historians' reconstruction.

The differences teach as much as the resemblance. The 1859 diagram illustrates natural selection and what Darwin called the principle of divergence. His *Autobiography* places that principle in a flash of insight in the 1850s; Robert Richards argues it is already stated in the 1844 Essay (Richards 2012). Either way, B36 offers no account of why diverging descendants should be favoured. The shape persisted for twenty-two years while the explanation beneath it changed.

A textual thread is easier to follow. On B101 Darwin argues that astronomers once supposed God ordered each planet's path, "but how much more simple, & sublime power let attraction act according to certain laws." On B114 he notes Geoffroy's "grand idea god giving laws … then leaving all to follow consequences," and Gavin de Beer's notes trace that idea into the 1842 Sketch, the 1844 Essay and the *Origin*, whose last sentence still sets descent beside "the fixed law of gravity."

### 6. What the notebook cannot tell us

The Notebook B we read is not the notebook Darwin filled. On 7 December 1856, during the years he was drafting his long species manuscript, he cut out the pages he judged useful and said so on the inside cover; he "again looked through" it in April 1873. Twenty-seven pages were excised; twenty-three have been found among his papers, mostly in DAR 208, and restored by editors. Four are still missing. His son Francis later marked passages in pencil with brackets and "FD," and in print misread "immense ages" as "immersage." The notebook left its box at Cambridge University Library around 2000–2001, was reported stolen in 2020, and was returned anonymously on 9 March 2022, wrapped in cling film inside a pink gift bag.

So the notebook cannot establish the moment a theory emerged. It does not record what was said in Gould's rooms, how much time passed between two lines, or whether the B36 sketch preceded its captions (van Putten thinks so, a reasonable reading of layout rather than a recorded fact). It cannot tell us what the four missing pages held. What it shows is a sequence: reading, specimens, conversation, a guess, a picture, a count, a doubt, a revision.

Confident summaries erase exactly that. Coverage of the 2022 return described the sketch as "mapping out his theory of natural selection" (NBC News), a theory Darwin would not reach for another year. Something similar happened, mechanically, while this essay was prepared: a plain-text extraction of the online transcription silently dropped the strikethrough on B5, so "separating partaking" ran together as if Darwin meant both.

### 7. Studio exercise: your page 36

Make one notebook page for your current project, on one sheet, in any medium, with three elements.

1. **Three references with exact locators:** page, timecode, object or specimen ID, or URL with access date. One must be a person you spoke to, recorded the way Darwin records Smith: who, what they said, and how far you trust it.
2. **A sketch drawn in under five minutes** that commits to a structure before you can justify it. Include one feature whose meaning you cannot yet name: a spacing, a gap, a branch that stops.
3. **One unresolved question,** written as a question and dated, plus your own *Cuidado*: the place you are most likely to fool yourself.

Then bring in AI twice. First, ask a model to transcribe the page exactly, keeping every crossing-out, doubtful word and question mark, and marking what it cannot read as illegible instead of guessing. Second, ask it for a summary. Compare the two, and circle every place where the summary turned a question mark into a full stop, a "perhaps" into a claim, or your unnamed gap into a definite meaning.

The rule for the week: AI may index, transcribe, find references, and propose further questions in the hedged register of a good editor ("probably personal communication," "reference uncertain"). It may not answer your question before you have done the next piece of research. Don't cut pages out. Pin the page up with the circled summary beside it.

## Five close readings

### 1. Inside front cover: the notebook dates itself, later

**Image:** [CUDL, MS-DAR-00121, image 2](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-DAR-00121/2) **Caption:** *Notebook B, inside front cover. Darwin's retrospective dating, a list of page numbers, and his 1856 note on cutting out "useful" pages.*

*Documented.* "C. Darwin"; a list, "p. 26 30 41 46 50 54 56 67 69 76 79 91 93 107 Ireland 113 117"; "This book was commenced about July, 1837. p. 235 was written in January 1838"; "All useful Pages cut out December 7th /1856/ (& again looked through April 21 1873)." De Beer's 1960 introduction prints the second date as 29 April; his transcription and Darwin Online give 21 April. The front cover also carries a later pencil mark, "C4(2) 49."

*Reading.* This is the most handled page in the book: three moments of Darwin's own use (1837–38, 1856, 1873) on one surface. The page list probably indexes passages he wanted to find again; it begins with 26, the first sketch page, and does not include 36. We do not know when the list was made or what it was for, but it is a reminder that the page now on souvenirs was not necessarily the one Darwin flagged.

### 2. B26: tree or coral?

**Image:** [CUDL, image 28](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-DAR-00121/28) · sketch details at Darwin Online: [fig. 1](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/manuscripts/Darwin_C_R_Notebook_B_CUL-DAR121_fig01.jpg), [fig. 2](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/manuscripts/Darwin_C_R_Notebook_B_CUL-DAR121_fig02.jpg) **Caption:** *B26: the notebook's first sketches, drawn just after Darwin wonders whether the tree of life should be called "the coral of life."*

*Documented.* The page finishes the B25 thought ("this again offers contradiction to constant succession of germs in progress"), shows two small branching figures mixing solid and broken lines, and asks: "Is it thus fish can be traced right down to simple organization.— birds — not." On B27 Darwin explains that the bases of branches "deaden," so in mammals and birds lineages "would only appear like circles."

*Reading.* The metaphor is being revised as it is drawn. A tree implies a visible trunk; coral implies a dead base whose connections "cannot be seen." The broken lines put that missing evidence into the picture itself: the drawing shows what Darwin expects not to find.

### 3. B36: "I think"

**Image:** [CUDL, image 38](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-DAR-00121/38) · [Darwin Online, fig. 3](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/manuscripts/Darwin_C_R_Notebook_B_CUL-DAR121_fig03.jpg) **Caption:** *B36: two words, then a branching figure from a circled 1. Branches ending in a crossbar mark lines with living descendants; A–D label four of them.*

*Documented.* The text breaks off at "I think." The figure is followed by two enclosed notes on extinction and the comparison of A, B, C and D; on B37 Darwin counts thirteen surviving forms against twelve that "left no offspring." Barrett's edition confirms the same split in the drawing.

*Reading.* The drawing works as a calculating device: by marking ends, Darwin makes the balance of survival and extinction visible and countable. It also holds an unresolved ambiguity about distance (why B and C are "finest gradation" but B and D "rather greater distinction"), which the prose names but does not explain.

### 4. B29–30: a page cut in half

**Image:** [CUDL, image 31](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-DAR-00121/31) (p. 29; compare [image 30](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-DAR-00121/30), p. 28) **Caption:** *B29: the top half stayed in the notebook; the bottom half, cut out in 1856, is now DAR 208.4. On it: "Monad has not definite existence."*

*Documented.* The surviving top reasons that if every species of a genus shared one fixed life, "whole class would die out, therefore not." The editorial markers place "Monad has not definite existence" on the excised lower half. In Cambridge's image manifest, pages 29–30 are framed at roughly half the width of the full pages around them.

*Reading.* One of Notebook B's clearest changes of mind survives because an archivist later matched a fragment to its stub. The argument is now physically split between two boxes. Any account of Darwin's thinking here depends on that reassembly.

### 5. B44: "Cuidado"

**Image:** [CUDL, image 46](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-DAR-00121/46) **Caption:** *B44: after eight pages of speculation on descent, gaps and extinction, Darwin writes "Heaven know whether this agrees with Nature: Cuidado."*

*Documented.* The line follows the claim that, between great groups, "the gradation will be sudden." Barrett glosses *Cuidado* as "Be careful!" The next line continues: "The above speculations are applicable to non progressive development."

*Reading.* Doubt here is an instruction, not a conclusion. Darwin flags his own reach and keeps working in the next sentence. The warning does not stop the inquiry; it marks where it is weakest.
