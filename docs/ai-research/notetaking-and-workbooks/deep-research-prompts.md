## 1 Getting ready to work

**Results:** [*Before the First Mark*](getting-ready-to-work/before-the-first-mark.md).

Research the cultural history of getting your materials together before beginning intellectual or creative work. Write an illustrated essay for TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI, and the Learning Lab's week 5 exhibition on research, notebooks, workbooks, dramaturgy, and preparation. The essay will be printed and hung in the studio to inspire people who are assembling their own research and production environments.

Start with two recognizable scenes: an eighth grader choosing school supplies and a first-year college student acquiring the books on a syllabus. Connect these to artists preparing studios, theatre companies beginning rehearsals, researchers assembling working libraries, and cooks arranging mise en place. Select four or five well-documented cases and investigate what people actually acquire, label, arrange, test, and keep within reach. Include the workbook as a distinctive object: a prepared structure that invites or requires its user to fill it in. How does that differ from a blank notebook, a textbook, or a folder of sources?

Examine the emotional and social dimensions of preparation: anticipation, belonging, self-invention, money, taste, institutional requirements, and the hope that a new arrangement will make a new kind of person possible. Investigate when setup supports work and when it becomes a substitute for beginning. Treat that as an open question, not a foregone productivity lesson.

Produce an accessible, substantial essay of approximately 1,800–2,400 words, a 150-word wall label, and a visual checklist of six telling objects or scenes. Research with the web and library collections; support historical claims with linked sources, favoring original supply lists, catalogues, photographs, manuals, and participants' accounts. For each visual, provide a source link, date, caption, and reuse information when available. Finish with three concrete experiments students could try when setting up a research workbook or studio, including one involving AI. Explain what each experiment might reveal rather than prescribing one ideal system.

## 2 How people have stored thinking outside their heads

**Results:** two answers, [*The Future in the File*](stored-thinking/the-future-in-the-file.md) and [*Kept for Later*](stored-thinking/kept-for-later.md).

Investigate how people have collected, arranged, and retrieved material for future thinking across different historical settings. Create an illustrated research essay for the week 5 wall exhibition in TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI. Readers are preparing research packs, notebooks, and creative projects; the exhibition should give them historical possibilities to think with.

Compare ancient notes and excerpt collections, a medieval florilegium, an early modern or eighteenth-century commonplace book, a nineteenth-century working notebook, a twentieth-century slip-box, and a contemporary digital research folder. Use six concrete examples rather than attempting an exhaustive chronology. Begin with Ann Blair's research on scholarly information management, including [Too Much to Know](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300168495/too-much-to-know/) and [Humanist Methods in Natural Philosophy](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-8744-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b), then consult the objects and primary texts themselves.

For every example, explain what enters the system, how it is transformed, how it can be found again, and what new work it helps someone produce. Compare copying, excerpting, annotating, indexing, cross-referencing, rearranging, and discarding. Attend to paper and other materials, physical scale, expense, literacy, collaborators, and the labor that makes a seemingly personal collection possible. Include at least one substantial example beyond the European scholarly tradition, researched in its own terms.

Distinguish documented transmission between practices from similarities noticed by a modern observer. Resist a story in which every old notebook inevitably develops into a computer or AI system. Ask what each arrangement makes easy, what it makes difficult, and what disappears when material is detached from its original setting.

Deliver 2,000–2,500 words of readable prose, a 150-word wall label, a compact comparison table, and six sourced visual candidates with explanatory captions and reuse information where available. Cite specific primary objects and scholarly research with links and page or folio references. End by identifying three historical design choices students could test in a research pack and one question AI-assisted research introduces that these precedents do not settle.

## 3 Ancient notebooks and the art of gathering

**Results:** two answers, [*Nothing Read Without Excerpting*](ancient-notebooks/nothing-read-without-excerpting.md) and the PDF [*Ancient Notes and the Work of Reading*](ancient-notebooks/ancient-notes-and-the-work-of-reading.pdf) ([text](ancient-notebooks/ancient-notes-and-the-work-of-reading-pdf-text.md)).

Research ancient Greek and Roman practices of keeping notes, collecting excerpts, and turning reading into new writing or a way of living. Prepare an illustrated essay for TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI, whose week 5 research will hang on the studio walls as inspiration for students building their own workbooks.

Investigate the range of meanings and uses of hypomnemata rather than assuming the term names one standardized kind of notebook. Anchor the essay in Seneca's Letter 84 on reading and writing and Pliny the Younger's Letters 3.5, which describes his uncle's reading and excerpting habits. Consult named scholarly editions or reliable translations of those primary texts. Consider wax tablets, papyrus, and surviving writing tablets as material evidence, while being explicit about whether any surviving object actually exemplifies the practices discussed in the literary sources.

Explore the difference between recording an event, saving an extract, rehearsing a maxim, and assembling material for composition. What happens when someone copies another person's words and then returns to them? Investigate selection, repetition, digestion, memory, and authorship. Explain how servants, enslaved readers or scribes, access to books, and leisure enter the documented examples. If you discuss Michel Foucault's account of ancient self-writing, distinguish his modern interpretation from the ancient evidence.

Write approximately 1,800–2,200 words, with a vivid opening grounded in a source, a 150-word wall label, and four visual candidates. Give each visual a repository link, date, material description, and caption explaining what it can and cannot show. Cite primary passages and historical scholarship; identify uncertain reconstructions and never invent a surviving notebook. End with a small studio experiment in collecting and reworking passages, then consider what changes when an AI selects or paraphrases them for the reader.

## 4 A medieval collection of useful passages

**Results:** [*A Handful of Flowers: Retrieval before the Index*](manipulus-florum/a-handful-of-flowers.md).

Investigate the medieval florilegium through a close study of Thomas of Ireland's Manipulus florum. Produce an illustrated research essay for the week 5 exhibition in TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI, where research on notebooks, workbooks, and dramaturgy will be printed and hung around the studio.

Explain what a florilegium is, what the flower-gathering metaphor suggests, and how this particular compilation organized passages for subsequent use. Begin with the [IRHT record for the Manipulus florum](https://fama.irht.cnrs.fr/en/oeuvre/268409), the [MANIPULUS manuscript database](https://manipulus.ucm.es/handle/20.500.12238/243), and scholarship by Richard and Mary Rouse and Chris Nighman. Locate a digitized manuscript or early printed copy and inspect actual pages. Distinguish the compilation's date from the date of the copy you examine.

Reconstruct the retrieval process through one concrete theme: show how a user might locate relevant passages, interpret their headings and attribution, follow any cross-references, and put the excerpts to work in a sermon or another composition. Label a demonstration you construct as a reconstruction unless a source documents an actual user's practice. Ask who selected the authorities, how excerpts changed when removed from context, and how organization shaped the moral or intellectual world available to readers.

Make the physical interface central: layout, rubrication, alphabetical order, navigation aids, abbreviations, and the work involved in copying and consulting it. Compare the compilation with a personal commonplace book and a modern curated source pack without collapsing their different purposes.

Deliver approximately 1,800–2,200 words, a 150-word wall label, and four visual candidates with repository links, shelfmarks, folio or page numbers, dates, captions, and reuse information if available. Link claims to manuscripts and scholarly sources. Finish with a practical exercise in building a small themed excerpt collection and a question about authority that remains relevant when AI assembles quotations today.

## 5 John Locke and the eighteenth-century commonplace book

**Results:** [*Places Before Subjects*](locke-commonplace-book/places-before-subjects.md).

Research John Locke's method of keeping a commonplace book and its use or adaptation in the eighteenth century. Write an illustrated essay for TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI, as part of a week 5 wall exhibition about research, workbooks, and gathering the materials for creative production.

Start with the 1706 English edition of [A New Method of Making Common-Place-Books](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL26873677M/A_new_method_of_making_common-place-books), following the catalogue's link to the digitized original. Establish the earlier publication history from reliable scholarship. Explain the indexing scheme carefully enough that a reader could try it. Demonstrate it with a small, clearly labeled example, and examine the problem it addresses: how to place entries as they arrive while still finding them by subject later.

Compare the instructions with two surviving eighteenth-century books or commercially prepared commonplace books. Seek one example beyond the familiar philosopher or statesman, such as a woman's literary collection, a household compilation, or a student's book. Let the evidence determine whether it follows Locke's method; do not infer adoption merely because it has an index. Explain the distinction between commonplace books, diaries, recipe books, miscellanies, and albums while allowing objects to cross those categories.

Ask how headings influence attention, whether copying can become composition, and what a personal selection of other people's words reveals about its compiler. Examine blank space, handwriting, page numbering, additions over time, and the relationship between purchased structure and individual invention.

Produce 1,800–2,400 words, a 150-word wall label, a worked indexing example, and four or five visual candidates with collection links, dates, page references, captions, and reuse information where available. Use primary objects and book-history scholarship, with linked citations. End with an invitation to try a commonplace book for a creative project and a precise comparison with search, tags, or AI summaries that acknowledges what those tools change.

## 6 Darwin's notebooks and ideas in progress

**Results:** [*"I think": Notebook B and the shape of research before an argument*](darwin-notebooks/i-think-notebook-b.md).

Conduct a close study of Charles Darwin's working notebooks as evidence of research taking shape before a finished argument exists. Prepare an illustrated essay for the week 5 wall exhibition in TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI. The audience is making research packs and preparing creative projects; the notebook should offer a concrete example of how gathering, sketching, questioning, and revising interact.

Begin with Notebook B, available through [Cambridge Digital Library](https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-DAR-00121/1), and consult the scholarly transcriptions and editorial notes in [Darwin Online](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/pdf/1987_Darwins_notebooks_F1817.pdf). Study the pages around the branching sketch associated with the words “I think,” then select three or four less famous entries that complicate a simple discovery narrative. Establish dates and page references carefully. Explain what these notebooks reveal about developing ideas and what they cannot establish about the exact moment a theory emerged.

Trace the interplay of reading, observation, correspondence, speculation, diagrams, and questions. Where possible, follow one entry into a later draft or publication, identifying the evidence for the connection. Investigate how notebook entries related to Darwin's other papers, books, specimens, and collaborators. Examine crossings-out, uncertain language, changes in direction, and later handling of the pages.

Keep the notebook's material and visual form in view. How does a quick diagram do work that a polished paragraph cannot? What gets lost if unfinished notes are rewritten as confident summaries? Avoid treating every apparent dead end as secretly destined for success.

Deliver approximately 1,800–2,200 words, a 150-word wall label, and five close readings of actual pages, each with an image-source link and a short caption. Cite manuscripts and historical scholarship and distinguish interpretation from established fact. End with a studio exercise that combines references, a sketch, and an unresolved question, and consider how AI could help preserve or prematurely close that uncertainty.

## 7 Inside Niklas Luhmann's Zettelkasten

**Results:** two answers, [*Junior Partner*](luhmann-zettelkasten/junior-partner.md) and the PDF [*Niklas Luhmann and His Working Zettelkasten*](luhmann-zettelkasten/luhmann-zettelkasten-exhibition.pdf) ([text](luhmann-zettelkasten/luhmann-zettelkasten-exhibition-pdf-text.md)).

Research Niklas Luhmann's actual Zettelkasten as a working intellectual system. Write an illustrated essay for TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI, to be printed for a week 5 exhibition about research, notebooks, workbooks, and preparation for making things.

Use the digitized [Niklas Luhmann Archive and its tutorial](https://niklas-luhmann-archiv.de/bestand/zettelkasten/tutorial), Luhmann's own writing on communicating with slip-boxes, and scholarship by the archive's researchers. Explain the physical boxes, note identifiers, branching sequences, references, keyword index, and relationships between reading notes and other notes, specifying which features belong to which parts or periods of the collection. Choose a small set of actual linked slips and walk the reader through them using their identifiers. Explain the content as well as the navigation.

Investigate how a note gets added, how a reader enters the collection, and how a path through existing material can produce a new question or argument. Where sources permit, trace a relationship between notes and a publication. Compare this evidence with contemporary advice about Zettelkasten, atomic notes, backlinks, and personal knowledge management. Identify which recommendations are documented features of Luhmann's practice and which are later adaptations.

Ask about friction, surprise, forgotten material, maintenance, and the time required to learn one's own system. Avoid unsupported claims that the box wrote his books for him or that one prescribed note format guarantees creativity. Consider whether a linked collection might serve a theatre-maker differently from a sociologist.

Produce approximately 1,800–2,400 words, a 150-word wall label, a simple diagram of the chosen note relationships, and four sourced visual candidates with identifiers, links, dates where known, and captions. Cite primary evidence and scholarship. Conclude with a manageable experiment students could run using paper slips or Markdown files, and examine what AI-generated links might add or obscure.

## 8 The binder and the Trapper Keeper

**Results:** two answers, Codex's [essay](binder-and-trapper-keeper/essay.md) and Claude's [*Keep all my papers*](binder-and-trapper-keeper/claude/essay.md); the folder is [`binder-and-trapper-keeper/`](binder-and-trapper-keeper/README.md).

Research the loose-leaf binder and Mead's Trapper Keeper as designed systems for organizing schoolwork and expressing identity. Produce an illustrated essay for the week 5 studio exhibition in TDM 155AI, Making Stuff in the Age of AI, connecting the familiar ritual of choosing school supplies with the creation of a research workbook.

Give a concise, sourced history of loose-leaf organization, then focus on the Trapper Keeper's development, introduction, design, advertising, and use. Investigate the different accounts of who designed it and distinguish test marketing, national rollout, and later redesigns. Seek patents, dated advertisements, product catalogues, surviving objects, and interviews with participants. Use [The History of the Trapper Keeper](https://www.mentalfloss.com/culture/education/history-trapper-keeper) as a route to those sources, checking its attribution discussion and separating retrospective memory from contemporary evidence.

Explain the object by following a student's day: inserting a handout, keeping subjects apart, carrying work between rooms, finding yesterday's notes, rearranging sections, and closing the whole collection. Study pockets, rings, dividers, closures, cover imagery, size, and portability. What promises did the design make to students, parents, and teachers? Investigate documented praise, restrictions, or complaints rather than repeating unsourced school-ban folklore.

Explore how school supplies combine functional organization with taste, status, belonging, and a hoped-for new self at the start of the year. Include comparison with an ordinary inexpensive binder and a student-customized example. Discuss cost and access with evidence from a specific place and period.

Deliver 1,800–2,200 words, a 150-word wall label, an annotated anatomy of the binder, and six visual candidates with dated source links, captions, and reuse information where available. Support historical claims with citations. Finish by identifying design choices students could borrow for a physical or digital research pack, including what a literal pocket or a rearrangeable page can do that search alone cannot.

Include the shift toward electronic texts, access codes, library reserves, and open resources where relevant to the selected recent case. Verify dated prices and terms from authoritative sources if you include them; distinguish ownership from temporary access. Give cost and unequal access substantive attention without letting the essay become only a pricing report.

Produce approximately 1,800–2,400 words, a 150-word wall label, and five visual candidates such as a reading list, a book bill, an annotated copy, a bookstore scene, and a personal shelf, each with source links and captions. End with a practical proposal for assembling a small, affordable working library for a creative project, considering what AI can help locate and what it cannot supply merely by summarizing.

Distinguish a dramaturgical dossier, a director's notebook, a designer's reference collection, a stage manager's prompt book, and a model book documenting a production. Explain their overlaps and different jobs. Establish when each object was assembled, by whom, for which readers, and how it was revised. A record made after a production should not be presented as its original research pack.

Follow at least two specific items—a historical document, photograph, map, sound reference, textual variant, or interview—into a documented rehearsal or staging decision. Where that connection cannot be established, say so. Attend to contributors whose research or organizational labor is less visible. Ask how a shared dossier can open possibilities and how it can harden into an authoritative interpretation that limits them.
