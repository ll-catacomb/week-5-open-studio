# Digital research and AI: keeping the path from source to claim

What must a digital notebook retain so that a reader can examine how its writer arrived at an argument? The answer involves more than saving documents or attaching a bibliography. A research essay transforms selected passages into notes, notes into relationships, and relationships into claims. A useful system lets the researcher move forward toward composition and backward toward the evidence that might require a revision.

This report follows four cases: associative archives and shared hypertext; a citation manager's connection between annotation and composition; executable notebooks; and AI synthesis with evaluated attribution. They expose different problems rather than successive stages of one inevitable invention. A trail records a chosen route; a bibliographic record identifies a work; an executable cell specifies a transformation; a generated paragraph proposes a verbal synthesis. None performs all the others' jobs.

The central argument is that digital research becomes more accountable when its intermediate objects remain inspectable. The researcher should be able to distinguish an excerpt from a paraphrase, a paraphrase from an inference, and a working arrangement from the finished argument. This is a proposed design criterion, not a claim that any particular interface improves essay quality. Historical demonstrations establish capabilities; manuals describe supported operations; empirical studies can test particular outcomes. Those kinds of evidence must stay separate.

## 1. Memex and NLS: a proposed trail and an implemented working structure

In his July 1945 essay, Vannevar Bush proposed the memex, a desk-based personal archive using microfilm, projection, controls, and photographic entry. Its distinctive operation was an associative trail linking items, with comments and branches. In section 7, his imagined reader follows material about bows into sources about elasticity, then copies a trail for a friend. This is a hypothetical research episode within a design vision, not a surviving user's notebook or evidence of a completed memex. Its proposed output is a shareable route through material, not necessarily an essay. [Bush, “As We May Think,” sections 6–8](https://web.njit.edu/~evank/download/as-we-may-think.pdf).

For essay writing, a trail suggests a useful intermediate form. It makes the selection and order of reading visible before they become the apparently natural order of prose. Yet a connection cannot supply its own warrant. Two excerpts might be linked because they agree, contradict, concern different periods, or merely remind the reader of one another. A modern reconstruction would need to name the relationship rather than treating every link as support. The same issue applies to a beautifully connected archive whose writer never returns to the surrounding passages.

Douglas Engelbart's 1962 framework located intellectual capacity in a trained person working with artifacts, language, and methods. It explicitly treated these as an interacting system. Equipment alone was not the unit of improvement. This was a research program and conceptual model, including imagined scenarios, rather than a controlled demonstration that computers made scholars better writers. Its importance here is the insistence that changed tools require changed practices. [Engelbart, *Augmenting Human Intellect*, II.A–B](https://dougengelbart.org/pubs/augment-3906-Framework.html).

By 1968 there was an implemented system. Engelbart and William K. English described NLS as a working environment for their own research group, encompassing documentation and development work. Text had hierarchical statements and reference links. Crucially, they distinguished a location number, vulnerable to rearrangement, from a statement name that survived changes around it. The report thus identifies an engineering problem directly relevant to notes becoming drafts: evidence needs an identity that outlasts its present position. [Engelbart and English, “A Research Center for Augmenting Human Intellect,” pp. 395–398, especially §3c2c](https://dougengelbart.org/pubs/papers/scanned-original/1968-augment-3954-A-Research-Center-for-Augmenting-Human-Intellect.pdf).

The December 9, 1968 demonstration presented hypertext, object addressing, dynamic file links, and shared-screen collaboration. Its archival clip guide identifies segments on group use and the online manual, as well as collaboration. I accessed that guide and the conference paper; I have not independently watched and timed the entire video. The guide documents a collaborative research environment, while the paper explains operations; neither alone measures the quality of its users' finished writing. [1968 demonstration and clip guide](https://www.dougengelbart.org/mousesite/1968Demo.html).

This changes the social picture of the notebook. The work belongs to a group maintaining tools, documentation, and conventions, rather than only to an individual collecting ideas. A question for historical research is how much labor disappeared behind the public image of a single operator at a console. Time reports and working files, discussed below, offer a route toward that question.

## 2. Citation management: a source can travel with its note

Zotero provides a bounded case within the larger history of bibliographic software. Its public beta launched on October 5, 2006. A contemporary November 29 tutorial already described searchable notes and directed users to attach a note to its source item to preserve their connection. This matters because the note was an object within a bibliographic collection, not just a passage in a separate text file. It is evidence of a supported workflow, not evidence of how commonly researchers adopted it. [Launch announcement](https://www.zotero.org/blog/zotero-10-public-beta-launch/), [2006 note-taking tutorial](https://www.zotero.org/blog/feature-spotlight-note-taking/).

The March 17, 2022 release announcement for Zotero 6 documents a more continuous route: mark a PDF, transfer annotations into notes with citations, and insert notes into a word processor while retaining active citations. It also describes Markdown export with links back to items and PDF pages. The important historical change is that copying an excerpt into another working surface need not detach it from its bibliographic identity. This report uses that release as a documented transition, not as the beginning of digital citation management. [Zotero 6 announcement](https://www.zotero.org/blog/zotero-6/).

Current documentation specifies that annotations added to notes include page links and citations, with a command to reopen the annotation in context. It also warns that changing PDF pages in external software can misalign annotations. A locator therefore depends on a particular source object and its integrity. “Page 12” is ambiguous when the printed numbering, PDF page count, and edition differ. The note should preserve those distinctions when relevant. [PDF reader and note editor manual](https://www.zotero.org/support/pdf_reader).

Another material distinction is easy to miss: Zotero stores its own annotations in a database rather than modifying the PDF itself, although annotated PDFs can be exported. The developers explain this partly through the problem of simultaneous group annotation and whole-file synchronization conflicts. A folder of PDFs is consequently not always the complete research notebook; the relationships and annotations may reside elsewhere. [Annotation-storage explanation](https://www.zotero.org/support/kb/annotations_in_database).

For composition, I would separate the source record, the reading note, and the argument note. A reading note asks what a passage says in context. An argument note asks what use the writer might make of it. Keeping both lets a writer abandon an interpretation without erasing the reading that produced it. Bibliographic software can preserve an association, but a source attached to a paragraph does not prove that the paragraph represents it fairly. The author still decides which evidence is relevant, what qualifiers survive the paraphrase, and whether two texts can reasonably be compared. These are interpretive responsibilities, not formatting operations.

## 3. Computational notebooks: an account that can run

The Jupyter team's 2016 paper describes notebooks as documents interleaving executable code, outputs, and explanatory prose, with export into formats such as HTML and PDF. It traces Jupyter to IPython and discusses notebooks supporting published research, including an open gravitational-wave data tutorial. The notebook can therefore be a research output in its own right, rather than preparatory material abandoned when the paper appears. These are documented affordances and examples of publication practice, not general proof of reproducibility. [Kluyver and colleagues, “Jupyter Notebooks,” pp. 87–90](https://elpub.architexturez.net/system/files/121_elpub2016.pdf).

For an essay that uses quantitative evidence, the difference is substantial. A note saying “this pattern increased” gives its reader only an assertion. A notebook can retain the dataset reference, the selection rule, the calculation, the graphic, and a prose interpretation. The calculation and interpretation remain different objects: reproducing a number does not establish that the categories were appropriate or that the historical explanation follows. An executable transformation can make one part of the reasoning inspectable while leaving another open to argument.

The apparent order of the document also needs scrutiny. Official documentation explains that the kernel is a long-running process retaining objects across cell executions. Thus an exploratory session can preserve working state while the writer changes and reruns parts of the notebook. The state used to generate a displayed result may depend on earlier actions that the final arrangement no longer makes obvious. [Jupyter's architecture and kernel explanation](https://docs.jupyter.org/en/latest/what_is_jupyter.html).

Pimentel and colleagues tested this gap empirically in 2019. They collected 1,159,166 unique GitHub notebooks; among 863,878 attempted executions of valid Python notebooks with defined version and execution order, 24.11% ran without errors and 4.03% produced matching results under their procedure. Missing dependencies and hidden state were among the problems. These figures describe that corpus, eligibility rule, and execution setup; they are not a current failure rate for all scientific notebooks. [Pimentel and colleagues, “A Large-scale Study,” abstract and §IV.G](https://leomurta.github.io/papers/pimentel2019a.pdf).

The lesson for humanities research is methodological rather than an instruction to turn every essay into code. Saving the evidence is different from saving the operations performed on it. A researcher can record exclusions, transcription corrections, grouping decisions, and revisions even when those operations cannot be executed by a machine. The benefit is an account another person can question. Such documentation requires labor: someone must describe the choices that the polished output makes hard to see. Conversely, keeping every intermediate action indiscriminately can bury the consequential decisions. Selection remains part of making a usable record.

## 4. AI synthesis: attribution is a test, not a visual feature

AI changes the proposed workflow most sharply when it supplies the sentence that connects sources. A writer may delegate tentative paraphrases, comparisons, or outline variations. The danger is not only an invented source: a real source can be attached to a statement broader than its evidence, or a disagreement can disappear into smooth synthesis. An accountable workflow must retain what was supplied to the model and distinguish its proposal from the author's accepted claim.

Rashkin and colleagues' 2023 attribution framework offers a precise question: can a generated statement be verified against an identified source? Their human annotation procedure separates interpretability from attribution. Importantly, the guidelines separate accurate representation of a source from assessment of the source's own trustworthiness. A response can represent a mistaken document faithfully. This framework evaluates one necessary component of quality, not truth, relevance, or an essay's complete argument. [“Measuring Attribution in Natural Language Generation Models,” §4, §6 and Appendix B](https://aclanthology.org/2023.cl-4.2.pdf).

The 2023 ALCE benchmark evaluates retrieval-and-generation systems, distinguishing claim support from irrelevant citations. Human evaluation shows imperfect citation performance; automated checking also struggles with partial support. These results concern specified models and datasets in 2023. They establish why citation appearance is an inadequate test, not the performance of whatever model a class uses in October 2026. [Gao and colleagues, “Enabling Large Language Models to Generate Text with Citations,” §3.3, Tables 8–9 and Appendix G.5](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.398.pdf).

A 2025 survey adds evidence about the organization of labor. Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers, collecting 936 reported examples. Greater confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical thinking; their qualitative analysis identified shifts toward verification, integration, and overseeing tasks. This was self-report across work activities, not a randomized essay-writing trial or proof of lost intellectual ability. It suggests questions about where effort moves when generation becomes easy. [Lee and colleagues, CHI 2025, abstract and §§4–5](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf).

My proposed response is a claim ledger: one row per consequential statement, with source identity, exact location, quotation or paraphrase status, inferential steps, and a human review decision. An AI system can propose entries or flag missing support. The writer must inspect the underlying passage, determine whether its context changes the claim, and accept responsibility for the final wording. Bibliographies, retrieved passages, and automated entailment scores are aids to that judgment; none substitutes for it.

## A source → note → arrangement → paragraph walkthrough

**Modern reconstruction, not a documented historical user's workflow.** Suppose the essay asks whether rearrangeable notes can remain accountable.

1. **Source.** Save the 1968 NLS paper, identify its edition, and record printed p. 398, §3c2c, separately from PDF page 4. Preserve the file used. The passage distinguishes a statement's location number from its name.
2. **Note.** Create `NLS-identity-01`. Mark a paraphrase: “The authors distinguish position-dependent references from names retained during structural change.” Add a separate inference: “Evidence identifiers should survive outline revision.” The second sentence is my application, not their finding about essay writing.
3. **Arrangement.** Place the note under “What survives rearrangement?” alongside an independently checked annotation-manual note. Record the comparison as a question about stable identity, not a claim of direct historical influence. Keep a contrary note: stable identity cannot prevent misleading interpretation.
4. **AI proposal.** Ask for two possible paragraph arrangements using only those labeled notes, preserving their identifiers and marking any new inference. Keep the prompt and response. Reject a proposed sentence that equates stable links with reliable arguments.
5. **Draft/output.** Write: “Digital notes can retain identities while their arrangement changes. That makes revision easier to inspect, but leaves the writer responsible for whether the cited passage supports the new claim.” Attach the NLS reference to the historical observation; identify the second sentence as the essay's argument. Reopen the passage before accepting the paragraph.

This exercise produces a paragraph and a record of one interpretive decision. It deliberately preserves the rejected equivalence, so the final prose does not conceal how the argument was narrowed.

## Comparison of the four cases

| Case | Working object and operation | What can become an output? | What the evidence establishes | Unresolved responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Memex / NLS | Associative trail / addressable, structured statements | Shared route / documentation and collaborative work | A proposal / an implemented system and demonstration | Explain why a connection matters |
| Citation manager | Source record, annotation, note, citation | Cited prose and bibliography | Documented supported operations | Read context; preserve qualifiers |
| Computational notebook | Data, code, output, explanation | Executable account and published supplement | Capabilities plus corpus-specific reproducibility tests | Explain selection and interpretation |
| AI synthesis | Retrieved passages, generated claims, attribution judgments | Draft paragraph or claim ledger | Benchmark and survey evidence with limited scope | Verify support and own the argument |

## Two feasible week 5 experiments

**1. Rearrangement with and without provenance, 45–60 minutes.** Give pairs the same three short sources and six excerpts. Half arrange plain excerpts; half arrange notes carrying source IDs, locations, and quotation/paraphrase labels. Each pair writes a 200-word argument, then exchanges drafts for a source audit. Count time to locate evidence, unsupported expansions, and changes after context is reread. Swap conditions for a second question if time permits. This tests auditability locally, not general superiority in learning or creativity.

**2. AI synthesis with a human claim audit, 60 minutes.** Prepare a small source pack containing one disagreement and one qualified claim. Generate a paragraph using a fixed prompt and recorded model/version. Students classify each factual clause as fully supported, partially supported, unsupported, or their own inference, checking exact passages. Revise the paragraph and retain the claim ledger. Compare citation count with supported-claim count. Record both generation and verification time; a quicker draft is not necessarily quicker completed research.

## Gaps and further research

This is a selective operational history. It does not establish a genealogy from Bush to every later system, document early commercial bibliography managers, or cover other important hypertext and notebook traditions. A fuller account should inspect early reference-software manuals and actual migrated libraries, not backdate present features. It should also study access: who could obtain the hardware, full texts, training, and staff time?

The largest missing link is a documented essay's complete development across source files, notes, successive outlines, and drafts. Software manuals show what can happen, not what did happen. The proposed experiments can produce inspectable local evidence of that chain, but they cannot establish long-term learning effects. AI benchmarks should likewise be rerun or complemented with a dated local audit before making claims about current model performance. Finally, preserving a record requires decisions about permissions, sensitive material, and the labor of verification; the record should be useful to readers without assuming that every private working note belongs in public.
